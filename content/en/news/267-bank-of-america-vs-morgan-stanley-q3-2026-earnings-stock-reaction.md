## What Happened

A day after JPMorgan Chase, Goldman Sachs, Citigroup, and Wells Fargo kicked off bank earnings season on Tuesday, October 13, the second wave arrived before the open on Wednesday, October 14: Bank of America (BAC) and Morgan Stanley (MS). The results split almost as sharply as Tuesday's did. Bank of America's net income fell 17% year-over-year to $6.93 billion, with earnings per share of $0.91. That figure includes $1.21 billion in combined penalties levied by three separate U.S. Treasury agencies - the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network's share of that penalty ranks as the third-largest in Treasury history. The Treasury said the bank failed to report more than $5 billion in suspicious transactions in a timely manner, and the bank separately settled a $11.6 million sanctions-related matter. Stripping out the penalty, adjusted EPS came to $1.08 - which still missed the LSEG consensus estimate of $1.10-$1.12. Revenue of roughly $30 billion missed expectations by about $570 million, driven largely by a 17% drop in fixed-income trading revenue to $2.55 billion; equities trading revenue rose 10% but still fell short of forecasts. On the positive side, net interest income climbed 8% year-over-year to $16.6 billion, and total loans grew by $34 billion from the prior quarter to $1.25 trillion. Shares fell as much as 8% in premarket trading on the news.

Morgan Stanley told the opposite story. The bank posted Q3 EPS of $3.03, comfortably beating the analyst consensus of $2.92, on revenue of $20.3 billion versus roughly $19.95 billion expected - an 11% increase from $18.2 billion a year earlier. The standout was Wealth Management, which posted record quarterly revenue of $9.1 billion with a 30.4% pretax margin and pulled in $232 billion in net new assets for the quarter alone. The Institutional Securities division generated $10.0 billion in revenue, powered by strong client activity in equities trading and robust advisory (M&A) fees. Return on tangible common equity came in at 22.6%, which the bank itself noted marks its fifth consecutive quarter above the 20% threshold.

## Why Two "Big Banks" Diverged So Sharply on the Same Day

The first thing separating the two results is the presence of a one-time charge. Bank of America's $1.2 billion Treasury penalty is unrelated to day-to-day operating performance - it's the cost of past regulatory violations catching up with the bank. What worried investors more was that even stripping out that charge, adjusted EPS still missed consensus. In other words, this wasn't simply "a one-time charge made the headline number look bad" - it was a double disappointment: strip away the one-time item, and the core business still underdelivered. The 17% decline in fixed-income trading revenue especially echoes a theme from a day earlier, when JPMorgan reported its first drop in fixed-income trading revenue in over two years. Taken together, the two reports point to a broader fixed-income trading slowdown across big banks this quarter, not an isolated issue at either firm.

Morgan Stanley's surprise, by contrast, traces back to its business mix. Unlike a traditional deposit-and-lending commercial bank, Morgan Stanley's revenue skews heavily toward wealth management, investment banking, and trading - a structure that benefited directly from this year's strong equity markets and the recovery in IPO and M&A activity. The $232 billion of net new assets flowing into Wealth Management reflects a virtuous cycle: rising markets lift the value of existing client assets, which directly increases the fee revenue the firm earns on managing them. Strong advisory revenue in Institutional Securities likewise tracks this year's active IPO and M&A dealmaking. The real takeaway isn't that one bank is simply "better run" than the other - it's that different business models carry different sensitivities to the same macro backdrop (rising rates, elevated market volatility, and a strong capital-markets environment). A bank like Bank of America, with a heavier deposit-and-lending base, gets a cushion from rising net interest margin in a higher-rate environment but is more exposed to a trading slowdown; a bank like Morgan Stanley, weighted more toward capital-markets businesses, captures a more direct benefit when equity markets are strong.

This earnings day also fits into a broader pattern across the entire October bank-earnings season. On Tuesday, Goldman Sachs beat estimates on strong equity trading while JPMorgan missed on a trading slowdown. On Wednesday, Morgan Stanley repeated Goldman's "equities and wealth management strength" pattern with its own beat, while Bank of America missed even more sharply than JPMorgan, compounding a similar trading slowdown with a regulatory penalty on top. Looking at both days together, a clear pattern emerges: banks weighted toward trading and wealth management (Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley) outperformed those built around traditional commercial banking (JPMorgan, Bank of America) this quarter.

The nature of the regulatory penalty itself is also worth examining. The fact that FinCEN's portion ranks as the third-largest in Treasury history signals this wasn't treated as a minor administrative slip but as a materially serious compliance failure. Suspicious Activity Report (SAR) obligations require banks to flag potentially illicit transactions - money laundering, fraud, and similar activity - to regulators within a defined window, and the sheer transaction volume that a bank of Bank of America's size processes makes timely compliance structurally harder to maintain. Several outlets, including Benzinga, pointed out that the penalty itself is small relative to the market capitalization wiped out by the stock's single-day decline, which suggests the market reacted less to the dollar figure and more to the uncertainty the episode raises: if this control gap existed, what else might be lurking beneath the surface. For investors, the lasting overhang from an episode like this often isn't the fine itself but the reassessment of internal-controls quality that it triggers.

## What to Take Away From This

- **Separate one-time charges from core business weakness.** Bank of America had a clear, identifiable one-time item in its $1.2 billion penalty, but the fact that even its adjusted results missed consensus was the worse signal for the stock. Before concluding a miss is "just a one-time charge," check the adjusted figures too.
- **Business mix matters more than the "bank" label.** Traditional deposit-and-lending institutions and trading/wealth-management-heavy investment banks respond very differently to the same macro environment. When evaluating financial stocks, looking at the underlying revenue mix is more useful than treating "bank" as a single homogeneous category.
- **A fixed-income trading slowdown showing up at multiple banks in the same quarter is a sector signal, not a company-specific failure.** JPMorgan and Bank of America both reported declining fixed-income trading revenue in the same quarter, which more likely reflects a shift in overall market trading conditions than a mistake unique to either firm.
- **Strong equity markets don't lift every financial company equally.** Morgan Stanley's $232 billion in Wealth Management net new assets is a direct byproduct of this year's market strength. Checking how other asset managers and wealth platforms performed in the same period helps build a fuller picture of the quarter.
- **Regulatory risk is hard to predict but can hit earnings hard.** A $1.2 billion penalty is large enough to meaningfully dent a quarter's net income. When evaluating large financial institutions, a history of regulatory violations or ongoing investigations is worth weighing as a real, if unpredictable, risk factor.

## FAQ

### Why did Bank of America's stock fall so much more than the size of the fine itself?

The $1.2 billion penalty is relatively small next to Bank of America's roughly $30 billion in quarterly revenue. The stock still fell as much as 8% because the market read the episode as a signal of something more fundamental: that even after stripping out the penalty, the core trading business missed expectations. The reassessment of internal controls triggered by the fine likely added to the pressure as well.

### Which bank should be considered the "better" one based on this quarter?

It's hard to declare one bank definitively superior based on a single quarter, because the two have fundamentally different business models. Bank of America leans more heavily on traditional deposits and lending, while Morgan Stanley is weighted toward wealth management and trading. This particular quarter's strong equity markets and active capital-markets environment simply favored Morgan Stanley's model - a different macro backdrop could easily produce a different outcome.

### Does the fact that both JPMorgan and Bank of America reported a trading slowdown point to an industry-wide issue?

Both banks reporting declining fixed-income trading revenue in the same quarter does suggest a broader shift in market trading conditions rather than an issue specific to either firm. That said, Morgan Stanley posted strength in equities trading on the very same day, so it's more accurate to say the slowdown and strength diverged by asset class rather than describing "trading" as uniformly weak across the board.
