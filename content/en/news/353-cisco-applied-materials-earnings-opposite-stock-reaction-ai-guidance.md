## What Happened

After the closing bell on Thursday, November 12, two very different pillars of the AI infrastructure trade reported earnings on the same night: networking giant Cisco Systems (Nasdaq: CSCO) and the world's largest semiconductor equipment maker, Applied Materials (Nasdaq: AMAT). Both beat Wall Street's revenue and EPS estimates. What happened to their stocks afterward could not have looked more different.

Cisco's fiscal first-quarter 2027 revenue came in at $18.5 billion, ahead of the roughly $18.1 billion analysts had modeled, while non-GAAP EPS of $1.40 cleared the $1.32-$1.33 consensus. GAAP net income was $4.6 billion ($1.14 per share) and non-GAAP net income was $5.6 billion ($1.40 per share). Cisco didn't stop at a beat - it raised full-year fiscal 2027 guidance to $74.8-$75.8 billion in revenue and $5.47-$5.53 in non-GAAP EPS, and guided the next quarter to $18.0-$18.2 billion in revenue with EPS of $1.34-$1.36. The stock had already climbed 1.24% to $102.01 during Thursday's regular session, building on a 2.66% gain the day before as investors positioned ahead of the print. Multiple outlets reported further gains once the numbers and guidance went public, though the exact size of that extended-hours move varied by report - what's consistent across the coverage is that the move was clearly upward and meaningful.

Applied Materials' numbers weren't bad either. Fiscal fourth-quarter revenue hit a record $10.41 billion, up 53% year-over-year and ahead of the roughly $10.30 billion consensus. Adjusted EPS of $4.16 beat estimates by about a dime. Notably, DRAM-related tool sales climbed to roughly a third of the company's total semiconductor equipment revenue - direct confirmation that the memory boom rattling chip stocks all year is showing up in actual equipment orders. Guidance for the next quarter called for revenue of $11.1 billion, plus or minus $500 million (above the roughly $10.89 billion consensus), with adjusted EPS guided to a wide $4.15-$4.55 range. The catch: China's share of revenue fell to about 25% this quarter from 29% a year earlier, and management told analysts on the call that China's share would likely shrink further below 25% heading into 2027.

The stock reaction told the real story. Reports vary somewhat on the exact figures, but Applied Materials shares were already down roughly 3% in Thursday's regular session before the report, and traded within about 1% either way in after-hours action once the numbers came out - CNBC's list of Thursday's biggest movers filed AMAT under "down less than 1%." For a company that just posted record revenue and 53% year-over-year growth, that is a remarkably muted response.

## Why This Matters: The Mechanism Behind Two Beats Going Opposite Ways

The first reason comes down to which direction each company's numbers pointed. Cisco's headline message was explicitly forward-looking: the company said hyperscaler AI infrastructure orders totaled roughly $9.3 billion across fiscal 2026, and it now expects AI infrastructure revenue to exceed $9 billion in fiscal 2027 - more than double the prior year. That's a mature, legacy networking business showing a brand-new growth engine accelerating in real time. Markets tend to react most strongly to signals about what's coming next, not what already happened, and Cisco delivered that signal clearly.

Applied Materials' numbers, by contrast, read more like confirmation of a story the market already knew. The memory boom - surging DRAM prices and tight supply - has been priced into chip stocks for months, most visibly through huge rallies in Micron, SanDisk, and Western Digital. Applied Materials' record quarter mostly confirmed that this demand is translating into actual equipment orders, rather than revealing anything the market hadn't already priced in. A well-telegraphed positive, even when it shows up exactly as expected in the numbers, tends to generate a much smaller stock reaction than genuinely new information.

The second reason is geopolitical direction. Applied Materials' shrinking China revenue share - down to 25% from 29% a year ago, with management flagging further declines ahead - read as a concrete warning sign rather than a footnote. With U.S.-China semiconductor equipment export restrictions showing no sign of easing, a structural decline in exposure to the world's second-largest chip market raises real questions about the company's longer-term growth trajectory. Cisco faced no comparable single-country overhang in its own report.

The third reason is how convincing each company's forward guidance looked. Applied Materials' EPS guidance range ($4.15-$4.55) was wide enough that it didn't read as a clear upward signal, even though the revenue guide topped consensus. Cisco, meanwhile, explicitly framed its move as a guidance "raise" across both revenue and EPS for the full year - language that signals management's own growing confidence, not just a quarter that happened to clear the bar.

Finally, it matters where each sector sits in its own cycle. Semiconductor equipment stocks have already ridden a massive run-up this year on the memory supercycle, and cyclical industries carry a familiar warning: the best quarter often lands close to the peak. Cisco, on the other hand, has been viewed as a comparatively under-owned, secondary beneficiary of the AI buildout rather than a direct one. When a stock like that produces concrete evidence of accelerating AI-related orders, investors tend to see more room left to run than they do in a sector that has already repriced sharply.

## What to Take Away From This

- Distinguish a "well-known positive" from genuinely new information. A theme the market has already priced in for months - like the memory boom - may not move a stock much even when earnings confirm it; a less-anticipated growth driver can move the same type of stock far more.
- Watch the language around guidance, not just whether a number beat consensus. An explicit "raise" with a tight range reads very differently from a wide guidance band that technically clears the bar.
- Revenue-mix shifts by region or customer can matter more than the headline beat. Applied Materials' shrinking China exposure is a long-term signal worth tracking separately from the quarter's top-line growth.
- Check where a sector sits in its cycle. A sector that has already rallied hard (semiconductor equipment) and one offering a newer, less-priced-in growth story (networking's AI upside) can react very differently to similarly "good" earnings.

## FAQ

### Does Applied Materials' muted stock reaction signal weakening AI chip demand?
Not really. The company posted record quarterly revenue and guided next-quarter revenue above consensus. The flat stock move looks more like a function of an already-priced-in memory boom theme, shrinking China exposure, and a wide EPS guidance range than any sign that underlying demand is slowing.

### Why is Cisco considered an AI-related stock?
Cisco is traditionally classified as an enterprise networking equipment maker, but hyperscalers building out AI data centers have sharply increased demand for high-performance networking and switching gear, repositioning Cisco as a secondary AI infrastructure beneficiary. The AI order and revenue figures in this report reinforce that framing.

### What should investors watch next from these two companies?
For Cisco, the key test is whether AI infrastructure revenue actually accelerates as promised in the next quarterly report. For Applied Materials, watch whether China's revenue share keeps shrinking as management guided and whether DRAM-driven equipment demand holds up alongside the broader memory market. Nvidia's November 17 earnings report looms as a catalyst that could reset sentiment across chips and AI infrastructure broadly.
