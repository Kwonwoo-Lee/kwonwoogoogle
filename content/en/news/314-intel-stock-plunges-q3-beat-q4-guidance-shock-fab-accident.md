## What Happened

Intel (NASDAQ: INTC) reported third-quarter results after Thursday's close on October 29 that, on paper, looked close to flawless. Revenue came in at $16.5 billion, up 21% year over year. Non-GAAP earnings per share hit $0.52, blowing past both the company's own guidance of $0.38 and the roughly $0.40 Wall Street consensus. GAAP EPS came in at $0.60. The headline number investors had been waiting for was data center and AI (DCAI) revenue, which jumped roughly 78-80% year over year to $7.3 billion - what the company described as its strongest data center revenue growth in 15 years. For a chipmaker that has spent two years trying to convince investors it isn't being left behind in the AI buildout, this looked like real, numerical proof that demand was finally shifting its way.

Then Friday's open happened. Intel shares plunged sharply as the regular session began, with reports diverging on the exact magnitude depending on timing: CNBC reported a 21% drop, Investing.com put it at more than 28%, and Yahoo Finance described a decline approaching 30% intraday. Whichever figure ultimately holds at the close, the scale is the same story - a company that had just beaten on both the top and bottom line erased roughly a quarter of its market value in a single session, one of the sharpest post-earnings reversals of this entire reporting season. Bernstein semiconductor analyst Stacy Rasgon cut his price target on Intel to $75 from $110 the same day.

The real trigger wasn't the third-quarter numbers - it was the fourth-quarter guidance issued alongside them. Intel guided Q4 revenue to $15-16 billion, well below the roughly $17 billion analysts had modeled. Adjusted EPS guidance of about $0.25 came in at barely half the $0.43 consensus. Adjusted gross margin guidance also stepped down sharply, falling from 44.1% in Q3 to an expected 38% in Q4. A quarter that had just delivered a clean beat was immediately followed by a guide that undercut nearly every metric investors use to value the stock.

Two separate problems compounded to produce that guidance. First, PC demand: Intel's Client Computing Group (CCG), its PC chip business, posted revenue of $8.1 billion, down roughly 9% sequentially. On the earnings call, management said it expects PC market weakness to persist through at least the first half of next year - not a one-quarter blip, but a drawn-out soft patch. Second, an unplanned operational hit: Intel disclosed that an incident at a fabrication facility in Israel is expected to cut roughly $300 million from fourth-quarter revenue and around 200 basis points from gross margin. Layer a structural PC slowdown on top of an unexpected production disruption, and the combined guidance came in far more conservative than the market had priced.

## Why a Beat Couldn't Save the Stock

What makes this case genuinely instructive is the paradox at its center: Intel beat on both revenue and earnings, and the stock still lost nearly a third of its value. That paradox reinforces one of the most important, and most frequently forgotten, rules in how markets price stocks - share prices react to changes in forward expectations, not to results that already happened. The Q3 numbers had largely been baked into analyst models months in advance. The Q4 guidance, by contrast, was brand-new information, and because it came in dramatically worse than expected, no amount of trailing-quarter strength could offset it.

The second lesson sits inside the AI segment's own blowout number. A 78% surge in data center and AI revenue is an unambiguously strong result, yet it still wasn't enough to protect the stock. That tells you investors are evaluating a fast-growing division and a company's overall margin and cash-flow trajectory as two separate questions, not one. Intel's foundry business remains unprofitable, and with blended gross margin guidance collapsing from 44.1% to 38% for the next quarter, the "AI winner" narrative was overwhelmed by the more immediate reality of a manufacturer whose profitability is compressing. A single segment growing explosively doesn't rescue a stock when the company-wide margin structure is moving the other direction.

Third, this episode mixed a controllable risk with an uncontrollable one. Softening PC demand is a macro trend management can at least forecast and plan around. A fab accident in Israel is not - it's an operational shock that arrived with no warning. Having both land in the same quarter's guidance made it hard for investors to separate "one-quarter accident" from "the start of a structural downturn," and when that kind of ambiguity shows up, markets tend to price toward the more pessimistic interpretation first and sort out the details later.

Finally, this fits a pattern Intel has shown repeatedly in recent years: extreme, outsized stock reactions around its earnings reports. In August 2024, Intel fell 26% in a single session after weak results, a suspended dividend, and sweeping layoffs. This time the underlying quarter was the opposite - genuinely strong - yet the stock's reaction was comparable or larger. Sitting at the center of a once-in-a-generation industry transition (foundry expansion, the AI pivot, a maturing PC market), Intel keeps delivering some of the most violent single-day moves of any large-cap name on the market, in either direction.

The timing also matters beyond Intel itself. One day earlier, on October 29, Alphabet lost roughly 12% and about $500 billion in market value on 2027 capex concerns - its own "worst day" record broken for the second time in three months. Amazon's own Q3 report the same week showed AWS growing 39% yet still drew a soft stock reaction on an EPS miss and weak Q4 guidance. Intel's guidance shock landed in the middle of that same wave of "AI capex anxiety" sweeping through this earnings season, suggesting investors across megacap tech are now scrutinizing forward guidance and margin trajectories far more harshly than they're rewarding reported growth.

## What to Take Away From This

- **Forward guidance moves stocks more than trailing results.** Intel beat on revenue and EPS, yet the stock cratered because Q4 guidance missed badly. When reading an earnings report, weight the forward outlook at least as heavily as the headline beat or miss.
- **One hot segment doesn't guarantee a healthy stock.** Data center and AI revenue surged 78%, but collapsing company-wide gross margin guidance overwhelmed that strength. Always check whether a standout segment's growth is translating into improved margins and cash flow at the consolidated level.
- **Markets often can't - or won't - cleanly separate one-time shocks from structural trends.** The Israel fab incident may prove to be a single-quarter issue, but layered onto a PC slowdown management flagged as lasting into next year, the market priced both pessimistically together. Expect an outsized initial reaction whenever a one-time event and a structural concern are disclosed in the same breath.
- **Size positions around earnings for stocks with a history of violent swings.** Intel has now posted two 20%+ single-day moves in roughly 26 months, in opposite directions for opposite reasons. Options-implied volatility ahead of earnings is a useful input for sizing exposure into a report like this.
- **Read a single company's reaction against the week's broader earnings backdrop.** Intel's guidance shock didn't happen in isolation - it landed the same week as Alphabet's capex-driven selloff and Amazon's soft guidance reaction. Comparing reactions across megacap tech names reporting in the same window often reveals more about market sentiment than any one report in isolation.

## FAQ

### Intel beat earnings estimates - so why did the stock crash?
Stock prices react to changes in forward expectations, not to results that already happened. Third-quarter revenue and EPS were largely already reflected in analyst models. The fourth-quarter guidance for revenue, EPS, and gross margin all came in well below consensus, and that forward-looking disappointment outweighed the backward-looking beat.

### What exactly was the Israel fab incident?
Intel disclosed in its earnings materials that an incident at a production facility in Israel is expected to reduce fourth-quarter revenue by roughly $300 million and gross margin by about 200 basis points. The company has not yet released detailed specifics on the cause or the repair timeline, so additional disclosures are worth watching for.

### Data center and AI revenue grew 78% - why wasn't that enough to support the stock?
Strong segment growth and overall company health are evaluated separately by investors. Intel's foundry business remains unprofitable, and blended gross margin guidance is set to fall sharply in Q4. The AI segment's strength wasn't enough to offset concerns about the company's broader profitability trajectory.

### Does this signal trouble for the broader chip sector?
PC demand weakness is a macro factor that could affect other chipmakers exposed to personal computers, not just Intel. At the same time, demand for AI and data center chips appears to remain strong industry-wide, so this report may be better read as evidence of a widening gap between PC-exposed chip demand and AI/data-center-exposed chip demand, rather than a sector-wide warning.
