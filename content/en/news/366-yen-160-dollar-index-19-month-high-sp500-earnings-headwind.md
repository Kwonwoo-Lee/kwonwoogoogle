## What Happened

On Friday, November 13, the dollar climbed as high as 160.03 yen intraday, pushing above the 160 line for the first time since early September. That level isn't arbitrary - it's the threshold where Japan's finance ministry and the Bank of Japan have actually stepped into the market to buy yen twice already this year: once during a record-setting intervention around the late-April to early-May Golden Week holidays, and again on July 30-31 in a rare joint operation that even drew in the US Treasury. This marked the third time in 2026 that 160 has come under serious test. Japan's Vice Finance Minister for International Affairs, Atsushi Mimura, warned that "recent currency moves are too rapid," while Finance Minister Satsuki Katayama flagged "one-sided" yen moves and said Tokyo would coordinate with the US Treasury. No new intervention was announced immediately, but two senior officials issuing back-to-back verbal warnings in the same week was itself read by traders as a signal that Tokyo's patience is thinning.

The other side of the move is broad dollar strength. The dollar index (DXY), which tracks the greenback against a basket of six major currencies, pushed above the 103 level in the second week of November, its highest level since April 2025 - a 19-month high. The euro slid in tandem, touching $1.10585, its weakest level over the same stretch. The driver is straightforward: betting on a December Fed hike has gone from likely to nearly certain. As of November 12, CME FedWatch implied roughly a 91% probability of a December move; three days later, on November 15, Fed Governor Christopher Waller raised the stakes further by floating the possibility of a half-point hike (see our November 15 coverage: [Fed's Waller Puts a 50-Basis-Point December Hike on the Table](/en/news/fed-waller-half-point-december-hike-odds-99-percent/)), which pushed the implied odds of some hike to roughly 99%. The mechanism is simple carry-trade math: the more confident markets become that a currency's yields are heading higher, the more attractive it becomes to hold, which pulls in buyers.

What makes this more than a one-sided story is that the Bank of Japan isn't standing still either. The BOJ already raised its policy rate to 1.25% on September 18, its highest level since 1995 (see [Bank of Japan Hikes to 1.25%](/en/news/boj-rate-hike-1-25-percent-yen-carry-trade-unwind-nasdaq-treasury/)), and Chief Economist Seisaku Kameda has signaled a further hike could come in December. Yet the yen has kept sliding anyway. The reason isn't direction - it's the gap. The Fed's benchmark sits at 3.75%-4.00% against Japan's 1.25%, so even if both central banks hike in the same month, the absolute interest-rate differential barely narrows, and may even widen. Moving in the same direction doesn't erase a carry trade's appeal if the pace of closing the gap stays this slow.

## Why This Isn't Just a Currency-Market Story

It's worth asking why a yen exchange rate and a Japanese intervention threat should matter to anyone trading US stocks. The answer splits into two parts.

The first is yen carry-trade unwind risk. For years, global investors have borrowed cheap yen to fund higher-yielding US assets - Treasurys, growth stocks, mega-cap tech. When Japan actually steps in and buys yen aggressively, the currency can spike sharply in a short window, forcing leveraged carry-trade holders to unwind those borrowed positions fast, which often means selling the US assets they bought with the borrowed yen. A version of this played out violently in August 2024, and every time 160 comes back into play, markets brace for a repeat.

The second, more direct channel runs straight through corporate earnings. According to asset manager Apollo, more than 41% of S&P 500 revenue now comes from outside the United States - the highest share since 2013. Bank of America's rule of thumb puts the earnings impact at roughly 3 percentage points off S&P 500 EPS for every 10% year-over-year rise in the dollar; Morgan Stanley has estimated a similar roughly 0.5-percentage-point hit to EPS growth for every point of dollar-index appreciation. The mechanism is pure accounting: when revenue earned abroad in yen or euros gets translated back into dollars for reporting, a stronger dollar shrinks the reported dollar figure even if local-currency sales didn't change at all. Mega-cap names like Apple and Microsoft, which derive close to half their revenue overseas, feel this more acutely than domestically-focused firms.

The timing is notable. This currency flare-up lands right at the tail end of Q3 earnings season and just a day before Nvidia's November 17 report. Nvidia draws a significant share of its data-center revenue from overseas hyperscalers across Asia and Europe, meaning that even a strong underlying sales quarter can carry a hidden FX drag once results are translated into dollars - which is exactly why investors increasingly check "constant-currency" growth figures alongside the headline numbers on earnings day.

## What to Take Away From This

- **Currency moves aren't just macro background noise - they flow directly into reported earnings.** A stronger dollar mechanically shrinks the reported EPS of companies with heavy overseas revenue exposure. Check a company's foreign-revenue share, and where available, its constant-currency growth rate, alongside headline numbers around earnings day.
- **Verbal intervention from central bank officials is itself a signal.** Even without actual buying, back-to-back warnings from senior finance officials in the same week suggest the 160 threshold is getting close to an actual trigger point - both of Japan's prior 2026 interventions followed similar verbal warnings within a short window.
- **Moving in the same policy direction isn't the same as closing the gap.** The BOJ is hiking too, but as long as the absolute rate differential with the Fed stays wide, the carry trade keeps its appeal regardless of which direction both central banks are nominally moving.
- **Yen carry-trade unwinds can spill over into US equities and bonds, not just FX markets.** The August 2024 episode is the reference case: a sharp yen move didn't stay contained to currency trading, it triggered forced selling in US mega-cap tech. That transmission channel is worth monitoring whenever 160 comes back into focus.

## FAQ

### What is the dollar index (DXY), and why does a 19-month high matter?
The dollar index tracks the dollar's average value against a basket of six major currencies, including the euro, yen, and pound. A 19-month high means the dollar is now the strongest it has been against other major currencies since April 2025, which reflects growing market conviction that the Fed will keep policy tighter, for longer, than previously expected.

### Why does Japan use currency intervention instead of just raising rates faster?
Raising rates too quickly would increase debt-servicing costs on Japan's large government debt load and strain mortgage holders and small businesses, so the BOJ has strong reasons to move gradually. FX intervention - selling foreign reserve assets to buy yen - lets authorities slow the pace of currency depreciation in the short term without having to abruptly shift the broader interest-rate path.

### How does a strong dollar actually hurt US company earnings?
When a foreign subsidiary's local-currency revenue and profit get translated into dollars for the parent company's financial statements, that translation happens at the prevailing exchange rate. As the dollar strengthens, the same amount of foreign-currency revenue converts into fewer dollars, which mechanically understates reported growth for any company with meaningful international revenue exposure - even when nothing changed in the underlying local-currency business.
