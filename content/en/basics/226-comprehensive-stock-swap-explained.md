## What Dunamu's Shareholders Are Getting Isn't Cash — It's Naver Financial Stock

In November 2025, Naver Financial and Dunamu (the operator of Korea's largest crypto exchange, Upbit) announced a comprehensive stock swap agreement. Dunamu's shareholders would hand over their shares to Naver Financial, and in return receive not cash, but newly issued Naver Financial shares — at an exchange ratio of roughly 2.54 Naver Financial shares for every 1 Dunamu share. Once the deal closes, Dunamu won't be dissolved or absorbed into Naver Financial. It will keep its own corporate identity intact and simply become a wholly-owned subsidiary of Naver Financial. The closing date has since been pushed back repeatedly while the deal clears regulatory approvals — antitrust clearance from Korea's Fair Trade Commission, and approval of the change in controlling shareholder from financial regulators — and as of this writing the expected closing has moved to March 2027. That repeated slippage is itself a clue: this isn't a simple stock purchase, it's a structured transaction that has to clear several distinct legal hurdles. The mechanism behind it is neither an ordinary merger nor a straightforward buyout of shares. It's called a **comprehensive stock swap**.

## What a Comprehensive Stock Swap Actually Is: Same Company, Different Shareholders

A comprehensive stock swap is governed by a specific set of provisions in Korea's Commercial Act. One company (the one that will become the wholly-owning "complete parent") issues new shares and swaps them, all at once, for every single share held by every shareholder of another company (the one that will become the wholly-owned "complete subsidiary"). Once the swap is done, the subsidiary's former shareholders no longer hold any of its stock directly — they now hold shares of the parent instead. The subsidiary itself doesn't disappear. It continues to exist as a legal entity in its own right; only its ownership has shifted entirely into the parent's hands.

This is the key structural difference from an ordinary merger, which [What Is a Merger Swap Ratio](/en/basics/merger-swap-ratio-explained/) covers in detail. In a merger, the absorbed company's legal existence ends outright — it's dissolved and folded into the surviving entity. In a comprehensive stock swap, the subsidiary-to-be's legal personality, employees, contracts, and operations are untouched. The only thing that changes is who owns its shares. That's exactly why this structure gets used for a company like Dunamu, which needs to keep its own business licenses, customer agreements, and brand intact — swapping shares is far cleaner operationally than merging two companies' entire legal and operational infrastructure together. And because the parent pays entirely in newly issued stock, no cash changes hands at all. A deal worth trillions of won can close without the acquiring company raising a single won of acquisition financing.

## Stock Swap vs. Stock Transfer: Joining an Existing Company or Creating a New One

Korean law actually splits this mechanism into two variants, and news coverage often blurs the distinction. A **stock swap** (주식교환) is when an already-existing company becomes the complete parent — this is what's happening in the Naver Financial-Dunamu deal. A **stock transfer** (주식이전) is when a brand-new company is incorporated specifically to become the complete parent at the moment the deal closes. This second form is common when several affiliated companies are being reorganized under one newly created holding company at once, sometimes through a "joint stock transfer" where two or more companies simultaneously become wholly-owned subsidiaries of the same new holding entity. The underlying mechanics are identical in both cases — existing shares get swapped wholesale for new parent shares. The only difference is whether the parent slot is filled by a company that already existed or one created purely for this transaction.

## Why Holding Company Conversions Lean on This Tool

Comprehensive stock swaps and transfers have become the standard tool for restructuring Korean conglomerates into holding company structures, and the reason ties directly into Korea's Fair Trade Act. To legally qualify as a holding company, a firm must own at least 30% of a listed subsidiary (or 50% of an unlisted one). Founding families often don't start out holding anywhere near that stake in every affiliate, and buying up shares on the open market to get there would take enormous amounts of cash and time. A comprehensive stock swap sidesteps both problems: by issuing parent-company shares wholesale to the subsidiary's existing shareholders, ownership can jump straight to 100% in one transaction, with no cash outlay at all. The Seah Group's use of a comprehensive stock swap to fold Seah Specialty Steel into Seah Holdings as a wholly-owned subsidiary — eliminating a dual-listing structure in the process — is a well-known example. Because this same tool also happens to be an efficient way for a controlling family to tighten its grip on group-wide ownership, nearly every such transaction draws scrutiny from minority shareholders over whether the exchange ratio was set fairly.

## How the Exchange Ratio Gets Set — and Why Two Different Ratios Show Up

The basic framework for setting the exchange ratio mirrors the one used for merger ratios: listed companies generally rely on a recent-price-based reference value under capital markets regulations, while deals involving unlisted companies or non-affiliated parties typically require an independent valuation. Here's where investors most often trip up: the "enterprise value ratio" and the actual "per-share exchange ratio" reported in the deal are not the same number.

The Naver Financial-Dunamu deal illustrates this well. The independent valuation put Naver Financial's enterprise value at roughly ₩4.9 trillion and Dunamu's at roughly ₩15.1 trillion — a ratio of about 1 : 3.06. But the per-share exchange ratio actually disclosed was 1 : 2.54, noticeably lower than the enterprise-value ratio. The reason is that the two companies had very different total share counts outstanding. Even if the overall value split stays fixed, dividing that value across a different number of "slices" (shares) changes how much value lands on each individual share. Dunamu's implied per-share exchange value came out to roughly ₩439,252, against roughly ₩172,780 for Naver Financial — and 439,252 ÷ 172,780 works out to almost exactly 2.54. The enterprise-value ratio tells you how the two companies' total worth is being split; the per-share ratio tells you how many new shares your one existing share actually converts into once each company's total share count is factored in. Headlines often report the enterprise-value ratio first, with the per-share ratio confirmed later in the formal filing — conflating the two will give you the wrong number of shares you're actually entitled to.

## A Simplified Worked Example

A cleaner hypothetical makes the mechanics easier to see. Company A (the future parent) has an enterprise value of ₩10 trillion and 100 million shares outstanding. Company B (the future subsidiary) has an enterprise value of ₩2 trillion and 20 million shares outstanding. The enterprise-value ratio is 10:2, or 1:0.2. Converted to a per-share basis, Company A's value per share is ₩10 trillion ÷ 100 million = ₩100,000; Company B's is ₩2 trillion ÷ 20 million = ₩100,000 as well — coincidentally identical, which makes the per-share exchange ratio 1:1. Now suppose Company B had 40 million shares outstanding instead of 20 million. Its per-share value drops to ₩50,000, and the per-share exchange ratio becomes 1:0.5 — even though the underlying enterprise-value ratio (1:0.2) hasn't moved at all. The lesson: always check whether a reported ratio is measuring total company value or value per individual share, because share count outstanding can push those two numbers in very different directions.

## The Parent Company's Shareholders Are Affected Too — Dilution

It's easy to focus only on the subsidiary-side shareholders, but the parent-to-be company's existing shareholders feel this deal as well. Because the parent pays entirely in newly issued shares, its total share count outstanding rises once the swap closes, and existing shareholders' ownership stakes get diluted by however many new shares go to the former subsidiary's shareholders. Returning to the worked example: if Company A started with 100 million shares and issues 10 million new shares (20 million × the 0.5 ratio) to Company B's former shareholders, its total share count rises to 110 million. A shareholder who held 1% of Company A before the deal now holds roughly 0.91% afterward. That dilution isn't automatically a loss — Company A has also gained 100% ownership of Company B's business in exchange — but existing parent-company shareholders should ask whether the value of what's being absorbed actually offsets the ownership stake being handed out to pay for it. That's also why, in the Naver Financial-Dunamu deal, Naver Financial held its own separate shareholder vote and extended appraisal rights to its own dissenting shareholders — the transaction isn't only a Dunamu-side issue.

## Three Things Minority Shareholders Should Check

**First, the right to dissent.** A comprehensive stock swap requires a special shareholder resolution (at least two-thirds of votes present, representing at least one-third of total outstanding shares). Shareholders who oppose the deal can file written notice before the vote and, within 20 days of the resolution, exercise **appraisal rights** to force the company to buy back their shares. In the Naver Financial-Dunamu deal, the agreement included a clause letting either side walk away from the deal entirely if appraisal-right claims from either company's shareholders exceeded ₩1.2 trillion — a reminder that appraisal rights aren't just an individual exit option; collectively, they can be large enough to blow up the whole transaction.

**Second, appraisal rights don't always apply.** Korean law carves out simplified procedures for smaller deals. If the would-be parent issues new shares equal to 10% or less of its total shares outstanding, the deal qualifies as a "small-scale stock swap," letting the parent's board approve it without a full shareholder vote — but in that case, the parent's own shareholders lose their appraisal rights entirely. On the other side, a "simplified stock swap" (used when the subsidiary's shareholders have already unanimously consented) replaces that company's shareholder vote with board approval, but appraisal rights still apply there. Which rights you actually have depends on which side of the deal you're on and which simplified procedure, if any, is being used.

**Third, the exchange ratio can turn into a tax issue.** Korean courts have handled real cases where tax authorities assessed gift tax on the difference when shares received through a comprehensive stock swap were valued well above what tax law considered fair market value — particularly where a controlling shareholder or related party appeared to receive disproportionately more value from the ratio used. The basis for how an exchange ratio was calculated can end up being a tax dispute, not just a fairness debate among shareholders.

## Key Takeaways

- A comprehensive stock swap exchanges every share held by a subsidiary-to-be's shareholders for newly issued parent-company shares, all at once — the subsidiary keeps its legal identity and continues to operate.
- Unlike a merger, where the absorbed company's legal existence ends, a stock swap (or transfer) leaves the company itself untouched and only changes who owns it.
- If an existing company becomes the parent, it's called a stock swap; if a brand-new company is created to be the parent, it's called a stock transfer.
- Watch for the difference between the "enterprise value ratio" and the "per-share exchange ratio" — differing share counts outstanding can make these two numbers diverge sharply.
- Dissenting shareholders generally have appraisal rights, but statutory exemptions like the small-scale stock swap can remove that right for the parent company's shareholders.

## FAQ

### Does the subsidiary get delisted after a comprehensive stock swap?
If the subsidiary-to-be was a listed company, its shareholders all exchange their shares for parent-company stock and stop holding the subsidiary's shares directly, which typically triggers delisting (or a delisting review). The company itself, however, doesn't dissolve — it keeps operating as a 100%-owned subsidiary of the parent.

### Who decides the price if I exercise appraisal rights?
The price is first negotiated between the company and the shareholder. If they can't agree, the formula set out in capital markets regulations applies for listed shares, and if there's still disagreement, either side can ask a court to determine the price — the same basic framework covered in [What Is a Merger Swap Ratio](/en/basics/merger-swap-ratio-explained/).

### What can I do if I think the exchange ratio is unfair to me?
Voting against the deal and exercising appraisal rights within the deadline are the most direct options. Courts have generally been reluctant to strike down a ratio calculated through the legally required formula and procedure on fairness grounds alone — absent clear evidence of manipulation or falsified filings, overturning the ratio itself is difficult.
