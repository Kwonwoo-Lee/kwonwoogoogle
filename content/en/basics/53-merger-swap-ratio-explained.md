## One Ratio Decides What Your Shares Are Worth

Just as the distribution mechanics matter when a company [spins off or carves out](/en/basics/spin-off-vs-carve-out/) a division, when two companies announce a merger, the first number an investor needs is the **swap ratio**. A ratio of 1 : 0.35 means one share of Company B converts into 0.35 shares of Company A. That single number determines exactly how much of the surviving company your existing stake turns into, which is why it deserves attention before any talk of synergies or strategic rationale in the merger announcement. What makes this ratio worth understanding as a concept, rather than just a headline number, is that Korean-listed companies don't get to negotiate it freely — the law prescribes exactly how it must be calculated, and that formula is precisely why merger ratios keep becoming a source of controversy in Korean markets.

## How the Ratio Gets Calculated — An Average of Recent Prices

Korea's Enforcement Decree of the Financial Investment Services and Capital Markets Act sets out a specific method for the "reference stock price" used in mergers involving a listed company, especially between affiliates. As of the day before whichever comes first — the board resolution or the merger agreement signing — each company's reference price is the arithmetic mean of three figures: the one-month volume-weighted average closing price, the one-week volume-weighted average closing price, and the most recent closing price. Once both companies' reference prices are calculated this way, the ratio between them becomes the merger ratio directly. If Company A's reference price comes to 100,000 won and Company B's to 35,000 won, the ratio is 1 : 0.35. Affiliate mergers are allowed to deviate from this formula by only ±10% (versus ±30% for non-affiliate mergers), which in practice means affiliated companies end up following the formula almost exactly.

The underlying logic sounds reasonable enough: the market's real-time price is treated as the most objective available consensus on value, so averaging it seems like a neutral approach. The problem is that this formula looks at recent price alone — it captures nothing about a company's actual asset base, earnings power, or growth prospects.

## Why It's Controversial — Prices Can Be Skewed Without Anyone Manipulating Them

Because the reference-price formula only looks at a one-week-to-one-month window, if one company's stock happens to be temporarily depressed when the merger is announced, an unfavorable ratio for that company's shareholders sails straight through the legal formula untouched. No illegal manipulation is required — a merger announced during a period of sector-wide weakness or a temporary setback can by itself produce a ratio disconnected from the companies' real asset and earnings scale. This risk is structural in affiliate mergers specifically: when the same controlling shareholder holds stakes in both companies, there's a built-in incentive to time the announcement for a moment when the company where they hold the smaller stake looks cheap on paper.

The best-known case is the 2015 merger of Samsung C&T and Cheil Industries. The formula-derived ratio came out around 1 : 0.35 (roughly 0.35 Cheil shares per Samsung C&T share), at a moment when Samsung C&T's stock sat near a yearly low while Cheil's was rising — despite Samsung C&T holding far more in total assets. The National Pension Service, a major Samsung C&T shareholder, later turned out to have voted in favor despite an internal assessment that the ratio was unfavorable — a decision that fed directly into the influence-peddling special counsel investigation, and one the fund is estimated to have taken a substantial cumulative loss from over the following years. The controlling family, meanwhile, held a larger stake in Cheil — meaning the ratio strengthened their effective control over the merged entity, which was the core of the controversy.

A similar dispute played out in 2024 around the restructuring involving Doosan Robotics, Doosan Bobcat, and Doosan Enerbility. Doosan Bobcat, generating roughly ₩9 trillion in 2023 revenue and over ₩1 trillion in operating profit, was to be folded into a structure anchored by Doosan Robotics, a much smaller company posting an operating loss — and minority shareholders argued loudly that the resulting ratio undervalued Bobcat. Korea's Financial Supervisory Service repeatedly rejected the registration filing, and Doosan ultimately withdrew the original plan and came back with adjusted ratios. Both cases share the same structural feature: the controlling shareholder held different ownership stakes in the two companies, and that gap lined up with which way the ratio ended up tilting.

## A Worked Example — When Asset Size and Stock Price Diverge

Take two hypothetical affiliates. Company A holds ₩20 trillion in total assets and ₩1.5 trillion in annual operating profit, but its stock has fallen 30% over six months on sector concerns, pulling its reference price down to ₩100,000. Company B holds only ₩5 trillion in assets and ₩200 billion in operating profit, but a recent rally on new-business optimism has pushed its reference price up to ₩70,000. Applying the formula mechanically produces a ratio near 1 : 0.7.

By asset and earnings size, Company A is nearly four times larger — but the ratio barely reflects that gap. If the controlling family holds a much larger stake in Company B than in Company A, this ratio ends up strengthening their control over the merged entity. Company A's existing minority shareholders, in effect, trade into the merger holding less of the combined company than the underlying asset and earnings value would suggest. The example illustrates the core problem: because the legal formula uses one yardstick — recent price — a small shift in announcement timing can swing the outcome substantially.

## Protections for Minority Shareholders — Appraisal Rights and Outside Valuation

Korean law provides some safeguards. Shareholders who vote against a merger gain **appraisal rights**: if exercised within 20 days of the shareholder meeting's resolution, the company must buy back their shares within two months. Price is first negotiated between company and shareholder; absent agreement, listed shares default to the same formula-based price, and either side can petition a court for a final determination.

It's worth being clear about what this doesn't do. Appraisal rights don't change the ratio itself or resolve an unfairness dispute. Korean courts have generally held that a ratio calculated according to the statutory formula and procedure isn't unlawful merely for being unfavorable — absent clear defects like fabricated data or manipulation, courts have been reluctant to void the ratio itself.

That gap has pushed regulatory change. A 2023 reform relaxed the mandatory formula for non-affiliate mergers, conditioned on mandatory third-party valuation and a ban on the same firm handling both valuation and advisory — but left affiliate mergers, the very cases where controversy concentrates, still bound to the formula. Legislation passed in 2026 extends reform to affiliate mergers as well, replacing the market-price-only reference standard with a broader "fair value" concept that also weighs asset value and income value (including discounted cash flow), and applying the same logic to how appraisal-right prices are set. Because implementing rules still need to be finalized after passage, exactly how this applies to real mergers going forward remains something to watch as concrete cases emerge.

## What to Check When You See a Merger Ratio

Start by checking which side the ratio favors relative to each company's asset base, earnings, and recent stock trend. In an affiliate merger, check which company the controlling shareholder holds more of, and whether either stock was notably depressed right before the announcement — and if so, whether that looks like ordinary sector weakness or suspiciously convenient timing.

If an outside valuation report is attached to the filing, compare its methodology and conclusion against market reaction and independent analyst views — and note whether it merely restates the formula result or adds genuine independent analysis using asset or income value. Watch how the stock reacts after the ratio is announced, and whether minority shareholder groups, including [activist funds](/en/basics/shareholder-activism-explained/), or institutional investors are pushing back. If you're considering appraisal rights, remember the appraisal price itself is set by a similar market-price formula, so dissenting alone doesn't fully insulate you from an unfavorable ratio's economic effect. Reading a merger ratio well means reading past the number itself to the timing it was calculated from, the formula's blind spots, and the ownership structure sitting behind it.

## Takeaways

- The merger swap ratio isn't negotiated by management — it's set by a formula in the Enforcement Decree of the Capital Markets Act, averaging one-month and one-week volume-weighted prices with the most recent closing price.
- Because this formula reflects only recent stock price, not asset or earnings scale, the ratio can diverge sharply from underlying value depending on when the merger is announced.
- Cases like Samsung C&T–Cheil Industries (2015) and Doosan Robotics–Doosan Bobcat (2024) show a recurring pattern: favorable timing and ratios for the side the controlling shareholder holds more of.
- Appraisal rights give dissenting shareholders an exit, but they don't change the ratio or resolve an unfairness claim.
- After 2023 reforms relaxed the formula for non-affiliate mergers (in exchange for mandatory outside valuation), 2026 legislation extends reform to affiliate mergers, moving toward a "fair value" standard that also weighs assets and income, not price alone.

## FAQ

### If I think a merger ratio is unfair, can I overturn it in court?
Korean courts have generally been reluctant to void a ratio calculated under the statutory formula and procedure just because it seems unfavorable. Absent clear defects like manipulated data, the more realistic paths are voting against the merger and exercising appraisal rights.

### How much do I get if I exercise appraisal rights?
Price is first negotiated between company and shareholder; if unresolved, listed shares default to a formula-based price, with either side able to petition a court for a final determination. That price is still calculated in a way that doesn't stray far from market price.

### Does the formula still apply to mergers between unrelated (non-affiliate) companies?
Since the 2023 reform, non-affiliate mergers no longer have to follow the statutory formula exactly, but mandatory outside valuation applies instead. Affiliate mergers remain bound to a much narrower deviation band, so in practice they still largely follow the formula.
