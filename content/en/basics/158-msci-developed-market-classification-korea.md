## Every June, the Same Headline

Late every June, the same story runs: "Korea misses out on MSCI developed market status — again." It happened in 2025, and it happened again in 2026. Korea stayed classified as an Emerging Market (EM) and didn't even make it onto the watch list, the first step toward developed-market (DM) status. What makes this interesting is that Korea isn't a marginal economy struggling to qualify — by most measures of GDP and market size, it already looks like a developed market. So why does the same outcome repeat year after year? To answer that, you need to separate what MSCI actually scores a country on from the specific box Korea keeps failing to check. This is a related but distinct question from the [Korea discount](/en/basics/korea-discount-value-up-program/) — the two issues overlap, but they aren't the same problem.

## What MSCI Classification Actually Measures

MSCI (Morgan Stanley Capital International) builds the benchmark indexes that trillions of dollars in passive and benchmark-tracking active money follow worldwide. Once a year, it re-reviews which bucket — Developed Markets, Emerging Markets, or Frontier Markets — each country's stock market belongs in. This classification matters for a very mechanical reason: funds that track an MSCI index don't decide country weightings based on judgment. They're required to hold whatever weight MSCI assigns to each country, in whichever index that country sits in. Think of it as the country-level version of the [index inclusion effect](/en/basics/index-rebalancing-effect/) covered elsewhere in this course — just scaled up from a single stock to an entire national market.

MSCI's classification rests on three formal pillars:

- **Economic development**: per-capita national income needs to sit at a sustained high-income level by World Bank standards. This criterion only applies on the way up from EM to DM — a country that later gets poorer doesn't get demoted back to emerging-market status because of it.
- **Size and liquidity**: the number of listed companies, the market cap of large-cap names, and average trading volume all need to clear quantitative thresholds.
- **Market accessibility**: whether foreign investors can actually get money in and out of that market freely — covering currency market openness, the ease of registering as an investor and opening accounts, restrictions on capital movement, settlement and clearing infrastructure, and information availability.

Korea has cleared the first two criteria for years. The problem, every single review cycle, is the third one.

MSCI actually breaks accessibility down further in a separate annual publication, the Global Market Accessibility Review, into five sub-criteria: openness to foreign ownership, ease of capital inflows and outflows, efficiency of the operational framework (account opening, trading, settlement), availability of investment instruments (derivatives, hedging tools), and stability of the institutional framework — eighteen individual measures in total. Crucially, these scores aren't MSCI's own desk judgment; they come from surveys and interviews with the asset managers, custodian banks, brokers, exchanges and regulators who actually operate in that market day to day. In other words, a country can announce a reform on paper, but the score only moves once investors who've actually tried to trade there report that the change holds up in practice. That lag between "the rule changed" and "market participants trust it works" is a big part of why Korea's repeated reforms haven't moved the needle as fast as policymakers hoped.

## Where Korea Keeps Getting Stuck: Can Money Actually Move Freely?

The specific item MSCI flags most consistently is restricted offshore trading of the won. The Korean won effectively only trades in the domestic interbank market; there's no officially open offshore market where investors abroad can freely buy and sell it. Instead, offshore investors have relied on non-deliverable forwards (NDFs) — contracts that settle the price difference in a third currency rather than exchanging actual won — to manage currency risk. MSCI doesn't consider that a substitute for a fully functioning offshore FX market at developed-market standard. Starting in 2023, the government scrapped the foreign investor registration system (which previously required foreigners to obtain a separate ID number before trading Korean stocks) and extended onshore FX trading hours to 2 a.m. local time. Yet in both the 2025 and 2026 reviews, MSCI's core complaint stayed the same: actual trading liquidity during the extended hours still hasn't built up enough, and the structural barrier to direct offshore won settlement remains in place.

A temporary, blanket short-selling ban that ran through 2024–2025 added to the problem. Foreign institutional investors treat short selling as a basic tool for hedging and arbitrage, and having it shut off for an extended period weighed negatively on the accessibility score. Short selling resumed in March 2025, which helped, but MSCI said it still needed more time to see whether the resumption was holding up smoothly. Put together, there isn't one single disqualifying flaw — it's a stack of smaller shortfalls across FX openness, short-selling accessibility, and the predictability of regulatory change that keeps adding up to the same "not yet" verdict.

## The Long Road to Inclusion: Why the Watch List Matters

No country jumps straight into the developed-market index. At its annual June review, MSCI first decides which countries to add to its watch list, and a country typically needs to sit on that list for at least a year before it's even eligible for an inclusion decision. The full sequence runs roughly like this:

1. **Watch-list addition** — once reform momentum is judged credible, a country is added for observation.
2. **At least a year of observation** — MSCI watches whether the announced reforms actually function in live trading.
3. **Inclusion decision** — if conditions are judged to be met, MSCI announces formal inclusion at a subsequent June review.
4. **Actual index effect** — even after the announcement, there's typically a six-month-to-one-year implementation window before the country is actually added to the index funds track.

Korea has repeatedly failed to clear even the first gate. It was added to the watch list once before, in 2008, on hopes of FX market liberalization, but was dropped again in 2014 after failing to resolve offshore trading restrictions and a rigid investor-registration system. More than a decade of repeat attempts since then haven't gotten it back on the list — not in the 2025 review, and not in 2026 either. The next realistic shot is the June 2027 annual review. The government has set its sights on structural fixes like round-the-clock FX market operation and offshore won settlement infrastructure, but whether those land in time to clear the next review is still an open question.

## Is Inclusion Even an Unambiguous Positive?

Developed-market inclusion is usually treated as an automatic win for the stock market, but the fund-flow mechanics are less straightforward than that. Korea currently carries a fairly large weight — in the high single digits — within the MSCI Emerging Markets index. Funds that mechanically track that index would have to sell off that entire weight the moment Korea exits it. The developed-market index Korea would join, meanwhile, is already dominated by the United States, Japan, and Europe, so the realistic weight Korea could claim there is likely only 1–2%. The total pool of money tracking developed-market indexes is several times larger than EM-tracking money, but whether that larger pool actually buys fast enough, and in enough size, to offset the EM-side selling at the moment of transition is something that can only be confirmed after the fact — not guaranteed in advance.

A simplified example makes the asymmetry clearer. Say Korea's weight in the MSCI EM index is roughly 10%. A fund that fully replicates that index would be holding Korean stocks equal to 10% of its assets — and would need to sell that entire slice in one move the moment Korea exits. In the developed-market index it would join instead, the US alone typically accounts for more than 70% of the weight, with Japan, the UK, and France filling most of the rest; even under an optimistic scenario, Korea's share there would likely cap out around 1–2%. The developed-market fund pool is larger in aggregate, but whether that gap between "10% exiting EM" and "1–2% entering DM" closes cleanly is exactly the kind of question that only gets answered once the transition actually happens. That's why domestic brokerage research repeatedly flags the possibility of a short-term supply-demand air pocket right around the index-change date itself, even while arguing that the reforms required to earn developed-market status should improve the quality of foreign capital inflows over the longer run.

Past reclassifications elsewhere suggest this caution isn't misplaced. Israel's benchmark index fell nearly 40% over the two years following its 2010 promotion to developed-market status, and Greece's stock index dropped roughly 45% after its 2001 developed-market classification. In both cases, the widely cited explanation isn't that reclassification itself caused the decline — it's that each country, once grouped with much larger developed markets, simply struggled to compete for investor attention as a small, relatively unglamorous name at the bottom of a crowded league table, rather than standing out near the top of the emerging-market group it had left. Whether the same pattern would play out for Korea is unknowable in advance, but those precedents are a useful reminder not to treat inclusion as an automatic catalyst without weighing the flows running in both directions.

## Key Takeaways

- MSCI sorts countries into developed, emerging, and frontier markets using three criteria — economic development, size and liquidity, and market accessibility — and that classification mechanically determines how the world's passive and benchmark-tracking capital is allocated by country.
- Korea clears the economic-size and market-size bars, but keeps falling short on market accessibility, mainly because of restrictions on offshore trading of the won.
- Developed-market inclusion is a two-stage process requiring at least a year on the watch list first. Korea was on that list once, from 2008 to 2014, and has failed to get back on in both the 2025 and 2026 reviews.
- Inclusion isn't automatically bullish. The passive selling triggered by exiting the emerging-market index could outpace the passive buying from entering the developed-market index in the short run, and Israel and Greece both saw their markets fall after their own developed-market promotions.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Would MSCI developed-market inclusion fix the Korea discount?
Not directly. The [Korea discount](/en/basics/korea-discount-value-up-program/) is a valuation problem rooted in shareholder returns, corporate governance, and accounting transparency, while MSCI classification is about foreign-investor market access. That said, the FX and regulatory reforms needed to pursue developed-market status could indirectly improve the broader investment environment.

### Does just being added to the watch list move stock prices?
Watch-list news can give short-term sentiment a lift, but actual index inclusion is still at least two to three years away even from that point, so watch-list news alone doesn't change underlying fundamentals.

### When is Korea's next realistic shot at reclassification?
Korea didn't make the watch list in either the 2025 or 2026 annual reviews. The next plausible opportunity is the June 2027 annual market classification review, though that timeline is only a current expectation and can shift — check MSCI's official announcements for the latest schedule.

### Is the Korea Value-up Index the same thing as MSCI developed-market status?
No, they're entirely separate. The Korea Value-up Index, created under the [Value-up Program](/en/basics/korea-discount-value-up-program/), is a domestic benchmark the Korea Exchange built from around 100 listed companies with strong shareholder returns and profitability. MSCI's developed-market classification is a completely different global framework that decides whether Korea as a country gets regrouped from "emerging" to "developed." The two efforts aim at the same underlying goal — raising how foreign investors value Korean markets — but they are not the same mechanism.
