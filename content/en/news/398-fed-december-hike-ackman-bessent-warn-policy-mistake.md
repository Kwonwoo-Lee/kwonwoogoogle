## What Happened

The Fed's last policy meeting of the year is now about eleven days away, scheduled for December 9-10. Markets are already treating a hike as close to a foregone conclusion: in a Reuters poll of 101 economists conducted November 6-10, every single respondent expected the Fed to raise its benchmark rate by a quarter point to 4.00%-4.25%. The Fed lifted rates to 3.75%-4.00% on September 16 - its first hike since 2023 - then held at that level on October 28-29 in a 10-2 vote, with Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack and Governor Lisa Cook dissenting in favor of an immediate increase. St. Louis Fed President Alberto Musalem has since warned that as many as four more hikes could be needed by next spring, and Governor Christopher Waller floated the idea of a 50-basis-point "jumbo" move in December instead of the standard quarter point. A hotter-than-expected October CPI report, released November 10, added fuel to the hawkish case: headline inflation rose 0.4% month-over-month versus a 0.3% forecast, while core CPI rose 0.3% versus a 0.2% forecast. Taken together, the Fed's own internal debate and the market's pricing both point toward the same outcome - a hike is coming.

What's notable this week is that two prominent voices outside the Fed are warning that outcome could still be a mistake - and they're arriving at that same conclusion from almost opposite directions. Pershing Square's Bill Ackman posted on social media on September 25 that "I think the Fed might have just made a mistake," taking aim at the September hike itself. His argument is unusual: companies racing to build AI infrastructure are competing for what he frames as a "near-infinite ROI" in a winner-take-all contest, so they're unlikely to slow data-center spending just because borrowing costs rise. If that's true, the standard channel through which rate hikes cool the economy - raising the cost of capital to discourage investment - simply doesn't apply to this particular pocket of demand. Meanwhile, the higher financing costs from the hike itself get baked into prices across the rest of the economy, which Ackman argues could create a self-reinforcing loop: hike, higher embedded costs, more inflation, another hike. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh didn't directly rebut the argument so much as lean into the underlying data, noting that AI-related equipment and software investment has grown roughly 20% over the past year - which he treats as evidence of inflation risk rather than evidence that rate hikes won't work. ARK's Cathie Wood staked out a third position entirely, arguing AI is ultimately a deflationary technology that lowers costs over time.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent's warning comes from a completely different angle. In an October 28 CNBC interview, he said another hike wouldn't "necessarily" be a policy mistake - a fairly neutral stance at the time. By November 13, in a separate CNBC interview, his position had hardened considerably. He argued that much of the current inflation pressure traces back to an oil-driven "energy shock" tied to the US-Iran conflict rather than to overheated demand, described recent data as "very noisy," and called the underlying US economy "very strong" - a combination of claims that amounts to saying a hike would be overcorrecting for a supply problem that's already easing. San Francisco Fed President Mary Daly pushed back, saying the incoming data still points toward a hike regardless.

## Why Two Critics Reached the Same Conclusion By Two Different Roads

Lined up side by side, Ackman and Bessent aren't actually making the same argument dressed in different language - they're making genuinely different ones. Ackman's case is about the transmission mechanism itself: if a meaningful slice of investment demand (AI infrastructure) simply doesn't respond to interest rates the way textbook models assume, then the rate tool is partially defanged for that slice of the economy, even as its cost side still passes through to broader prices. Bessent's case is the more conventional demand-shock-versus-supply-shock distinction that shows up in any introductory monetary economics discussion: a central bank is generally advised not to fight a supply-driven price spike - like a war-related oil shock - with a demand tool like interest rates, because raising rates does nothing to bring oil prices down and instead just squeezes unrelated, rate-sensitive sectors such as housing and durable goods.

There's also a structural wrinkle worth noting: one of these warnings is coming from a sitting Treasury Secretary, not an outside investor. The Fed is deliberately designed to operate independent of the executive branch, and Treasury Secretaries have historically avoided publicly second-guessing specific rate decisions. Bessent's shift from a neutral October comment to a pointed November warning is plausible as a straightforward reading of incoming data - but it's also the kind of pattern that gets read, rightly or wrongly, as political pressure on the central bank, especially against the backdrop of earlier reporting that the administration has pushed Bessent to get the Fed to move in a particular direction. That ambiguity is itself part of why markets are watching his comments closely rather than dismissing them as one more economist's opinion.

The sharper irony, though, is timing. The same week these mistake warnings were landing, Wall Street posted its best week in well over a year: the S&P 500, Dow, and Nasdaq each logged their strongest weekly gains since April 2025 and October 2022, respectively. None of that rally was being driven by a shift in rate expectations - it was Nvidia's Jensen Huang returning as CEO, a surprise memory-chip demand story, and a handful of other company-specific catalysts. In other words, the market isn't betting the Fed backs off. It's making a different bet entirely: that whatever happens with rates, the earnings momentum in a handful of industries will outrun the drag from higher borrowing costs. A rate-path debate this unsettled and a record-setting equity rally can, apparently, coexist in the same week.

## What to Take Away From This

The probability markets assign to an event and the wisdom of that event are two separate questions, and it's easy to conflate them. A quarter-point December hike being almost universally expected doesn't mean the debate over whether it's the right call is settled - Ackman and Bessent are proof that "what will happen" and "what should happen" can diverge sharply even among people with access to the same data.

Pay attention to *why* two critics reach the same headline conclusion, not just the fact that they agree. If Ackman's AI-inelastic-demand thesis is right, the read-through runs toward semiconductor, data-center, and power-infrastructure stocks. If Bessent's energy-shock thesis is right, oil and broader commodity prices become the more important variable to track. The mechanism behind a forecast usually matters more for positioning than the forecast's conclusion alone.

Macro uncertainty and a strong equity market aren't mutually exclusive. This week is a clean example of a stock rally running almost entirely on company- and industry-specific momentum while a genuinely unresolved rate debate played out in the background. Don't let a single macro variable crowd out more direct signals coming from specific sectors' underlying fundamentals.

## FAQ

### Why does it matter that a sitting Treasury Secretary is publicly criticizing the Fed's rate plans?
The Fed is structured to make monetary policy independent of political pressure. When a Treasury Secretary repeatedly comments publicly on a specific upcoming rate decision, markets tend to read it as a signal of executive-branch pressure on the central bank, regardless of how the comments are framed economically. That perception, more than the economic argument itself, is part of why Bessent's remarks are drawing extra scrutiny.

### If a hike is already almost fully priced in, why do these warnings matter for investors?
The probability of a hike and the economic consequences of that hike are different questions. Even a fully expected move can turn out to be the wrong tool if it's responding to the wrong kind of inflation - and if either Ackman's or Bessent's diagnosis proves correct, the aftermath (further hikes, a slower economy, or continued high energy prices) would matter a great deal for how markets trade in the weeks after the decision itself.

### What's the practical difference between demand-driven and supply-driven inflation?
Demand-driven inflation happens when spending and investment outpace what the economy can supply, and raising rates to cool that demand tends to work reasonably well. Supply-driven inflation - like an oil-price spike from a geopolitical conflict - stems from production costs or availability, not excess demand, so raising rates doesn't address the root cause and mainly just slows down unrelated, rate-sensitive parts of the economy, such as housing. That's why central banks are generally more cautious about using rate hikes to respond to supply shocks.
