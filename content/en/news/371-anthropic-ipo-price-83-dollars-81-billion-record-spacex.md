## What Happened

On Wednesday, November 18, Anthropic finalized its Nasdaq IPO price at $83 a share - five dollars above the top of the $70-to-$78 range it had set just over a week earlier. The offering itself also grew, from the original 825 million shares to roughly 980 million shares. Multiply the two together and Anthropic raised approximately $81.3 billion through the public offering alone, eclipsing SpaceX's $75 billion IPO from this past June. Bloomberg, CNBC and several other outlets are now describing it as the largest IPO on record. Shares begin trading on the Nasdaq Global Select Market under the ticker ANT on Thursday, November 19 - the day after pricing.

The signs that this listing would land here were visible days in advance. Around November 16, Bloomberg reported that the roadshow's order book was already "covered" - fully allocated within the original price range - on its very first day, a process that typically takes anywhere from several days to a couple of weeks (related coverage: [Anthropic's IPO Book Covered on Roadshow's First Day](/en/news/anthropic-ipo-oversubscribed-pricing-above-range-november-18/)). The exact oversubscription multiple was never disclosed, but the outcome speaks for itself: demand was strong enough to push the final price five dollars past the top of an already-aggressive range.

The deal structure carries one more wrinkle that sets it apart from a typical IPO. Alongside the $81.3 billion public offering, Anthropic is closing a separate $26 billion private placement of non-voting Class N shares. Nvidia and Google (Alphabet) are each putting in roughly $10 billion, AMD is contributing $2.5 billion, Broadcom, Micron and Microsoft are each adding $1 billion, and TSMC is chipping in $500 million. Add the public and private tranches together and the combined total runs closer to $107 billion - which is worth flagging, because different outlets report different numbers depending on whether they count only the public offering or the full package when calling this the "biggest IPO ever."

The valuation implied by the $83 price is itself a striking data point. Applied to Anthropic's fully diluted share count, it works out to roughly $1.5 trillion. For context, Anthropic was valued at $350 billion in a private funding round just ten months earlier, in January 2026 - itself nearly double the $183 billion valuation from its September 2025 Series F round. In other words, the IPO price implies the company's valuation has climbed more than fourfold in under a year. That is not a number to simply accept at face value; it's worth understanding exactly how it was calculated before drawing conclusions from it.

## What's Behind the Record Number - and What It Doesn't Tell You

The "largest IPO ever" headline is attention-grabbing, but the more useful questions are why demand ran this hot, and whether that heat is likely to carry through into actual trading.

The first factor is simple pricing mechanics. Underwriters set the final price based on the volume and quality of orders received during the roadshow, and when a book fills completely on day one, underwriters have every incentive to push the price toward - or past - the top of the range, since that maximizes proceeds for the issuer. But that choice cuts both ways: historically, when an IPO prices at or above the top of its range, the first-day "pop" - the gain investors get buying at the open relative to the offer price - tends to be smaller, because much of the optimism is already baked into the starting price. SpaceX followed a similar oversubscription pattern before its own listing, which makes Anthropic's first days of trading a useful reference point for how other large AI-related listings might be priced and received going forward.

The second factor is the character of the demand itself. Every company participating in the $26 billion private placement - Nvidia, Google, AMD, Broadcom, Micron, Microsoft and TSMC - either supplies Anthropic with chips or cloud infrastructure, or uses Anthropic's AI models in its own business. Money that flows in to secure a commercial relationship behaves very differently after listing than money chasing pure price appreciation: strategic investors tend to hold through short-term volatility, which can dampen early price swings, but it also makes it harder to tell how much of the apparent demand reflects genuine market appetite versus suppliers and customers reinforcing each other within the same supply chain. A structure where the supplier becomes an investor, and that investment flows back into the supplier's own chip or cloud revenue, is the same circular-financing dynamic that has drawn scrutiny across the AI sector throughout the year.

The third factor is simply how the headline valuation is calculated. The $1.5 trillion figure is a fully diluted number - it treats every stock option, restricted share, and the newly issued non-voting Class N shares as if they were already ordinary common stock. Counting only shares currently outstanding would produce a lower figure. Comparing a fully diluted valuation for one company against a non-diluted figure for a competitor, such as OpenAI's reported private valuation, risks drawing a conclusion based on numbers that were never calculated on the same basis to begin with.

The fourth factor is the flip side of record size itself. A larger float of shares outstanding after listing can support eventual index inclusion and give institutional investors more room to build positions - but it also concentrates trading interest and attention in the first days after listing, which historically has made price discovery choppier rather than calmer. SpaceX's own stock swung more than usual in the weeks immediately following its debut, a pattern worth keeping in mind here.

## What to Take Away From This

- **A "biggest IPO ever" headline is a separate question from whether the stock is a good buy on day one.** A large deal size says nothing by itself about where the stock goes from the open; if anything, pricing at or above the top of the range tends to leave less room for an immediate pop.
- **Strategic, relationship-driven demand reads differently than pure market demand.** It can stabilize a stock in its early days, but it's a weaker signal of what the broader market actually thinks the company is worth.
- **Always check how a valuation number was calculated before comparing it to a peer's.** Fully diluted and non-diluted valuations for the same company can differ substantially, and comparing mismatched bases across competitors produces misleading conclusions.
- **Expect above-average volatility in the first days of trading.** Large, high-profile IPOs tend to see outsized trading volume and price swings right out of the gate; a first-day or first-week price is a poor proxy for long-run fair value.
- **Check the lock-up expiration calendar in advance.** However hot initial demand looks, insider and early-investor lock-ups expiring months down the road frequently bring a fresh wave of supply that can pressure the stock, regardless of how strong the opening days were.

## FAQ

### Exactly when and how can I buy ANT shares?
The IPO priced at $83 on Wednesday, November 18, and trading opens on Thursday, November 19, on the Nasdaq Global Select Market under the ticker ANT. Buying at the IPO price itself is generally limited to institutional investors and private-placement participants; retail investors will buy at whatever price the market sets once trading opens. The very first trade of the day often doesn't happen right at the 9:30 a.m. bell - it can take longer for the opening auction to clear.

### Is the deal worth $81.3 billion or $107 billion?
Both figures are accurate, they just measure different things. The $81.3 billion refers to the public offering alone - the shares any investor could eventually trade. The roughly $107 billion figure adds in the separate $26 billion private placement of non-voting Class N shares sold to Nvidia, Google and other strategic partners. Which number an outlet reports as "the deal size" depends on whether it's counting the public tranche only or both tranches combined.

### How was the $1.5 trillion valuation calculated?
It's the $83 offer price multiplied by Anthropic's fully diluted share count - meaning every stock option, restricted share, and the newly created non-voting Class N shares are counted as if converted into ordinary shares. A valuation based only on shares currently outstanding would come in lower. When comparing this figure to another company's valuation, it's worth checking whether that company's number was calculated the same way.

### Why are chip and cloud suppliers like Nvidia and Google buying into the private placement?
Each of these companies either supplies Anthropic with GPUs or cloud infrastructure, or relies on Anthropic's models in its own products, and the investment formalizes and deepens that commercial relationship. At the same time, the arrangement has drawn criticism as a form of circular financing, since money a supplier invests in a customer can flow back to the supplier as revenue from chip or cloud sales.
