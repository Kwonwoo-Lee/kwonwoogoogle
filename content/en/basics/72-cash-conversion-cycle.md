## Rising Sales, Shrinking Cash: How That Happens

As covered in [What Is Earnings Quality?](/en/basics/earnings-quality-accruals/), net income and actual cash flow often tell different stories. One of the most common reasons for that gap is simple: **the speed at which sales grow doesn't match the speed at which those sales turn into cash in the bank.** A business that buys inventory ahead of demand, sells on credit, and waits months to collect on that credit can show fast-growing revenue and profit on paper while the cash sitting in its bank account barely moves. In severe cases, this is exactly how a profitable company ends up going under from a cash shortage. The **Cash Conversion Cycle (CCC)** covered in this lesson compresses that gap into a single number. Where [ROIC vs WACC](/en/basics/roic-vs-wacc-value-creation/) asks how efficiently invested capital turns into profit, CCC asks a more operational question one level down: how many days does cash stay tied up just running the business?

This isn't only a management concern. An investor who reads only the income statement and concludes "revenue is growing, so this is a good company" can easily miss whether the cash to fund that growth is actually available inside the business. The more attractive a growth story looks, the faster inventory and receivables tend to swell alongside it — and if the company can't cover that working capital burden internally, it ends up plugging the gap with a share issuance or more borrowing, which dilutes existing shareholders or adds interest expense. Tracking CCC alongside revenue growth tells you whether that growth is cash-generative or cash-hungry.

## What CCC Measures: Three Turnover Periods Combined

CCC measures, in days, how long it takes a company to get cash back after spending it on raw materials or inventory. The formula is:

```
CCC = Days Inventory Outstanding (DIO) + Days Sales Outstanding (DSO) − Days Payable Outstanding (DPO)
```

Picture it as a timeline. The clock starts the moment the company pays for raw materials or inventory. DIO is how long that inventory sits before it's sold; DSO is how long it takes, after the sale, to actually collect the cash owed. Add those two together and you get the stretch of time during which the company has spent money but not yet received it back. But the inventory purchase itself usually isn't paid for immediately either — most businesses buy on credit from their own suppliers, and that grace period is DPO. Because that period is effectively "funded by someone else's money" rather than the company's own cash, it gets subtracted from the total.

## What Each Component Actually Measures

**DIO (Days Inventory Outstanding)** shows how long raw materials or goods sit in inventory before they're sold.

```
DIO = (Average Inventory ÷ Cost of Goods Sold) × 365
```

A higher DIO means inventory isn't moving quickly — and that much cash is tied up sitting on shelves rather than circulating.

**DSO (Days Sales Outstanding)** shows how long it takes, after a sale, to actually collect the cash.

```
DSO = (Average Accounts Receivable ÷ Revenue) × 365
```

A rising DSO can mean the company is offering more generous payment terms to win business — or that customers' own finances are deteriorating, delaying collection.

**DPO (Days Payable Outstanding)** shows how long the company takes to actually pay its own suppliers.

```
DPO = (Average Accounts Payable ÷ Cost of Goods Sold) × 365
```

A longer DPO means the company is using that much more of its suppliers' money to fund operations. Unlike the other two, a larger DPO works in the company's favor.

All three ultimately reflect a mix of bargaining power and industry norms. Companies with short DSO tend to be cash-heavy consumer or service businesses, or ones with products competitive enough that they don't need to offer lenient terms to win a sale. Companies with long DSO are often B2B businesses where long payment terms are standard practice, or ones competing hard enough that generous terms are the price of winning the deal. The same logic applies to DPO: large buyers with real leverage over suppliers can stretch payment out, while smaller companies are often the ones being asked to pay their own suppliers early.

## A Worked Example: Hypothetical Retailer C

Take a hypothetical retailer, Company C, with the following recent annual figures:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Revenue | $3.65 billion |
| Cost of goods sold | $2.92 billion |
| Average inventory | $480 million |
| Average accounts receivable | $300 million |
| Average accounts payable | $400 million |

Each component works out to:

- DIO = ($480M ÷ $2.92B) × 365 ≈ **60 days**
- DSO = ($300M ÷ $3.65B) × 365 ≈ **30 days**
- DPO = ($400M ÷ $2.92B) × 365 ≈ **50 days**

That gives CCC = 60 + 30 − 50 = **40 days**. On average, Company C has to fund its own operations for 40 days between paying for inventory and collecting cash from the sale. If Company C announces plans to double revenue next year, inventory and receivables will generally scale with that growth, meaning the working capital tied up in those 40 days needs to roughly double too. This is the paradox at the heart of CCC: the faster a company grows, the more cash it typically needs locked up in working capital — and if that funding isn't lined up in advance, a genuinely profitable company can still run into a cash crunch.

## Negative CCC: Funding the Business With Someone Else's Money

CCC can, in theory, go negative. If DPO is larger than DIO plus DSO combined — meaning the company collects cash from customers before it even has to pay its own suppliers — CCC comes out negative. A company with this structure needs less incremental working capital as it grows, and revenue growth itself can effectively generate spare cash rather than consuming it. Large e-commerce platforms and big-box retailers are commonly cited as textbook examples: they collect payment from customers immediately (cash or card at checkout) while negotiating long payment terms with suppliers, thanks to sheer purchasing scale and leverage. That structure, though, is generally available only to a handful of players with dominant scale or negotiating power. A smaller company in the same industry that tries to force DPO higher without that leverage risks straining supplier relationships in ways that eventually show up as worse pricing or supply reliability.

## What to Watch For: Comparing Across Industries and Over Time

The most common mistake in reading CCC is comparing it directly across industries with fundamentally different structures. Semiconductor manufacturers or heavy-equipment makers have inherently long production cycles and hold inventory longer, so a long DIO — and therefore a long CCC — is normal for them. Large retailers or online platforms, by contrast, turn inventory quickly and collect payment instantly, so a short or even negative CCC is typical. CCC is far more useful compared against peers within the same industry, or tracked over time for a single company, than compared across industries.

Strategy can shift the normal range even within one industry. Two apparel brands can both look "normal" for the sector while running very different playbooks: one that produces small batches quickly each season will tend to show a shorter DIO, while one that manufactures in bulk ahead of the season and sells through it will structurally show a longer DIO. Neither pattern is automatically superior — whether it actually translates into profitability and growth is a separate question, best checked against [Operating Leverage](/en/basics/operating-leverage-explained/) and revenue trends.

A sudden shift in CCC is worth paying attention to. A DSO that's rising on its own can signal that customers' finances are deteriorating. A rising DIO can mean demand forecasting has missed and unsold inventory is piling up. A sharply rising DPO could reflect improved negotiating leverage — or it could mean the company is delaying supplier payments because its own cash position has weakened, so it's worth checking which one it is against other signals. As with the working capital ratio covered in [Altman Z-Score](/en/basics/altman-z-score-bankruptcy-risk/), CCC is best read alongside the cash flow statement and liquidity ratios rather than in isolation.

## Key Takeaways

- CCC = Days Inventory Outstanding (DIO) + Days Sales Outstanding (DSO) − Days Payable Outstanding (DPO), measuring in days how long a company's own cash stays tied up between paying for inventory and collecting on sales.
- A longer CCC means more working capital is needed to fund revenue growth — and if that funding isn't secured in advance, even a profitable company can hit a cash crunch.
- CCC can go negative when DPO exceeds DIO plus DSO, a structure common among retailers and platforms with strong supplier negotiating leverage.
- Because "normal" CCC varies widely by industry, it's most useful compared against peers or tracked over time for one company, not compared across unrelated industries.
- A sharp change in CCC isn't good or bad on its own — you have to break it down into which of DIO, DSO, or DPO actually moved to understand why.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How is CCC different from the current ratio?
The current ratio is a static snapshot — current assets relative to current liabilities at a single point in time. CCC is a dynamic measure that captures the actual speed at which inventory, receivables, and payables cycle into cash over time. The two complement each other rather than substitute for one another in assessing liquidity.

### Where can I find a company's CCC?
You can calculate it directly from the income statement and balance sheet — inventory, accounts receivable, accounts payable, revenue, and cost of goods sold are all you need. Many brokerage research platforms and financial data providers also publish CCC figures directly, though numbers can vary slightly depending on whether a provider uses beginning, ending, or average balances.

### Is a negative CCC always a sign of a great business?
Not necessarily. A negative CCC built on genuine negotiating leverage is a real positive. But one built by forcing suppliers to accept longer payment terms than they're comfortable with can strain those relationships and eventually show up as worse pricing or supply risk. What matters is understanding how the structure was built, not just the number itself.
