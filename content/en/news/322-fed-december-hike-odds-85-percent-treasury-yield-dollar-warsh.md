## What Happened

Just days after the Federal Open Market Committee held its benchmark rate at 3.75%-4.00% on October 28, bond markets had already moved decisively to reprice what happens at the next meeting, on December 9-10. The CME FedWatch tool's implied probability of a quarter-point hike in December stood at 78.3% on Tuesday, October 27, the day before the decision. Right after the hold was announced, that probability jumped to 85.0%. It eased slightly to roughly 83% once Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's press conference wrapped up, but even that pullback left the market firmly positioned for a hike rather than another hold. A separate gauge based on overnight index swap (OIS) pricing, which uses a different methodology, put the same probability closer to 73% around the same time. The exact number depends on which market you're reading, but every measure told the same directional story: traders had shifted from treating a December hike as a coin flip to treating it as the base case.

What makes this notable is how completely it reverses where market expectations stood just months earlier. For most of this year, the dominant assumption among investors was that the Fed's next move would be a cut. That changed in September, when the committee voted unanimously, 12-0, to raise rates for the first time in three years, lifting the federal funds rate from 3.50%-3.75% to 3.75%-4.00%. At October's meeting, the committee held rates, but the vote split 10-2. Both dissenters, Fed Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack, argued for a quarter-point hike rather than a hold. Cook's vote carried particular weight because she has generally been read as one of the more labor-market-focused, dovish members of the committee. A traditionally dovish governor siding with the hawks sent a signal to markets that discomfort over inflation had spread well beyond the usual hawkish holdouts - and bond traders translated that signal directly into a higher probability of a December hike.

The reaction in rates and currency markets backed up that shift. On the day of the announcement, the 10-year Treasury yield rose more than 3 basis points to somewhere in the 5.02%-5.05% range. A rate hold typically doesn't move long-end yields much, since it's read as preserving the status quo, but this time the hold arrived packaged with hawkish signals - two dissents in favor of tightening, plus unusually blunt language from Warsh - and those signals pushed long-term yields higher anyway. Over the following days, the 10-year pulled back to around 4.93%, suggesting markets had initially overshot before settling into a more measured read of the decision. The U.S. Dollar Index (DXY) fell for three straight sessions from October 28 through 30, slipping to the low 101 range. That's a somewhat unusual pairing - rising hike odds typically coincide with dollar strength, not weakness - and it points to markets worrying less about the hike itself than about the inflation pressure that's forcing the Fed's hand. Equities, by contrast, barely reacted: the S&P 500 closed up 0.28% at 7,743.98 and the Nasdaq Composite gained 0.27% on decision day, since a roughly 93% probability of a hold had already been priced in well before the meeting.

## Why This Matters for Markets

A Fed that looks like it's leaning toward hikes across two consecutive meetings directly contradicts the "rate cuts are coming" narrative that dominated markets for most of this year. Growth stocks, small caps, and high-dividend REITs - asset classes whose valuations were built on the assumption of lower future rates - are now facing renewed pressure to reprice as the hike-not-cut scenario becomes the market's working assumption. October's roughly 5% Nasdaq decline, its worst month since March 2025, wasn't driven solely by AI capex valuation concerns; this reassessment of the rate path was running underneath it the whole time.

Warsh's explicit rejection of forward guidance compounds the volatility this creates. Previous Fed chairs tended to leave at least some linguistic breadcrumbs in statements and press conferences that let markets infer the likely next move. Warsh, by contrast, has said flatly that "barring an exceptional circumstance or crisis, I don't believe in forward guidance." That means every nonfarm payrolls report, CPI print, and PCE reading between now and the December 9-10 meeting will move market pricing on its own, with no Fed commentary to soften or contextualize it. The swing in CME FedWatch odds from 78% to 85% and back to 83% is itself a small-scale preview of this kind of data-dependent repricing. With the usual guidance "shock absorber" removed, each individual data release is likely to trigger sharper market reactions than it would have under the Fed's old communication style.

This repricing window overlaps with two other major catalysts: the November 3 midterm elections and the October jobs report due November 6. Given that September's jobs report came in well below expectations at just 29,000 jobs added, a similarly weak October reading could quickly unwind the market's current 85% hike bet. Conversely, if both employment and inflation data come in firm, a December hike could move from "base case" to something closer to a foregone conclusion. With both outcomes still plausible, that uncertainty is itself the central risk investors have to manage under a Fed that has deliberately stopped signaling its own intentions.

## What to Take Away From This

- **A quiet market reaction on decision day doesn't mean the market is done digesting the news.** Stocks and currencies barely moved on October 28, but the bond market's probability repricing continued over the following days. Don't assume the initial reaction captures the full picture.
- **Rising hike expectations and dollar weakness can coexist.** The textbook pairing is higher hike odds, stronger dollar - but when the hike is being driven by inflation concerns layered on top of a softening labor market, the dollar can weaken instead. Check the underlying narrative before leaning on a simple correlation.
- **Without forward guidance, every data release carries more weight.** Expect sharper moves around payrolls, CPI, and PCE releases between now and December 9-10, and size positions with that added volatility in mind.
- **Rate-sensitive assets are worth revisiting.** Growth stocks, small caps, and REITs whose valuations assume lower rates are the most exposed if the market's working assumption shifts fully from "cuts ahead" to "hikes ahead."

## FAQ

### Why do CME FedWatch and OIS-based estimates give different probabilities?

CME FedWatch backs out its probability from Fed funds futures prices, while OIS-based estimates draw on pricing in the overnight index swap market - a different market with a different calculation method. It's common for the two to diverge by several percentage points at any given moment. When both point in the same direction, as they do here (clearly favoring a hike), that agreement makes the underlying signal more credible, even if the exact number varies.

### If the Fed actually hikes in December, what does that mean for stocks?

Rate hikes generally raise the discount rate applied to future cash flows, which weighs more heavily on growth and AI-related stocks whose valuations depend heavily on earnings far in the future. Banks and insurers, which tend to benefit from wider net interest margins when rates rise, are often relative beneficiaries. That said, a hike that's already priced in at 85% probability would shock markets far less than a surprise move, so tracking how this probability evolves between now and the meeting matters more than fixating on the headline number itself.

### Why did the 10-year yield fall back after its initial spike?

The jump past 5% immediately after the October 28 announcement looks like a short-term, somewhat overdone reaction to the hawkish signals in the vote and press conference. Over the following days, the market pulled back to around 4.93% as traders weighed the fuller picture - a labor market that's still cooling, alongside the midterm elections and jobs report still to come. That pattern reflects an ongoing repricing process rather than a one-directional move.
