## Every Short-Selling Story Mentions the Borrower. Almost None Mention the Lender.

Headlines about short selling always talk about borrow fees spiking or lending balances climbing. The [short selling](/en/basics/short-selling-explained/) lesson covered this borrowing transaction from the borrower's side — the trader who wants to bet against a stock. But a borrower can only exist if someone, somewhere, is willing to lend. So who actually lends out all those shares, and why? This lesson looks at the same transaction from the other side of the table: the lender. It isn't about short-selling strategy either — the focus here is on how simply holding a stock can quietly generate a second stream of income.

## The Basic Structure: Three Parties, One Transaction

Securities lending is an arrangement where whoever holds shares (the lender) lends them, for a fee and a fixed period, to whoever needs them (the borrower). An intermediary — typically a custodian bank, a central depository, or a brokerage — usually sits between the two sides, matching supply with demand. The sequence looks like this:

1. A lender (a pension fund, asset manager, insurer, or even a retail investor) registers shares it holds as available to lend through an intermediary or broker.
2. A borrower — usually an institution or foreign investor planning to short the stock — requests to borrow a specific name, and the intermediary matches it against available supply.
3. The borrower posts collateral (cash, government bonds, or other securities) with the lender and receives the shares.
4. For as long as the borrower holds the position, it pays the lender a daily fee, quoted as an annualized rate.
5. When the agreed term ends, or the lender recalls the shares, the borrower returns the same security and quantity, and the collateral comes back.

The key point is that this isn't a sale — legal title changes hands temporarily, against collateral, more like a rental than a transfer of ownership. In exchange, the lender collects something closer to rent than a trading profit.

## Why Lend at All? Idle Assets Earning a Second Income

Large pension funds and asset managers often hold core positions for years without any intention of selling. For shares that are going nowhere anyway, lending them out for a fee is close to free money — which is exactly why the biggest lenders in any lending market tend to be long-term institutional holders: pension funds, asset managers, insurers, and foreign institutional investors. In Korea, brokerage data from February 2025 put outstanding securities-lending balances at over 48 trillion won, a buildup widely read as institutions stocking up on lendable supply ahead of the resumption of short selling that year.

What's less widely known is that a growing number of brokers now extend similar lending programs to retail investors. Shares sitting idle in a brokerage account can be automatically matched against borrower demand, generating a small but real fee for doing essentially nothing. Not every broker or every stock participates, and the terms — minimum lot sizes, available names, payout timing — vary by firm, so check the specific program's terms before assuming your shares qualify. If there's little or no borrow demand for a given stock, a lending request simply won't get matched; conversely, holding a stock that's hard to borrow can turn into a surprisingly steady source of extra income.

## How the Fee Gets Set: Supply and Demand, Nothing Else

The borrow fee isn't a fixed rate set by an exchange or regulator — it's determined stock by stock, purely by supply and demand. Large, liquid blue chips with ample lendable supply — often called "general collateral" names in the market — typically trade in a fee range of roughly 0.1% to 1% annualized. Stocks with heavy short demand or thin available supply, by contrast, become "hard-to-borrow," and fees on those names can climb past 10% a year. As a rule, the more short interest piles up relative to lendable supply in a given name, the more expensive it gets to borrow.

A concrete example makes this easier to picture. Lend out $100,000 worth of stock at an annualized fee of 2% for a year, and you'd collect roughly $2,000 in fee income — simple arithmetic. Lend the same $100,000 worth of a heavily shorted, hard-to-borrow name where the fee has spiked to 15%, and that income jumps to $15,000 over the same period. Because of this relationship, the trajectory of a stock's borrow fee is sometimes watched as an indirect gauge of how aggressively the market is betting against it. How to actually trade off that signal is strategy-course territory; here, it's enough to understand that the fee itself moves purely with supply and demand.

## Why Collateral Matters: Protecting the Lender

The lender's biggest risk is simple: what if the borrower can't return the shares? Collateral exists to cover exactly that. Borrowers typically have to post collateral worth more than the shares they're borrowing, whether in cash, government bonds, or other high-quality securities. Collateral is marked to market daily, and if the price of the borrowed stock rises enough that the posted collateral no longer covers it, the borrower has to top it up. In this respect, securities lending shares a structural feature with short selling itself: it's a credit-backed arrangement that depends on continuous, real-time monitoring of whether that credit still holds.

## Where the Lending Agent Fits In — and How the Revenue Gets Split

Large pension funds and asset managers rarely go out and find borrowers themselves. Instead, they typically hand their lendable portfolio over to a lending agent — usually a custodian bank or broker — who handles matching, collateral management, and recalls on their behalf. Under this arrangement, the lender doesn't keep 100% of the fee income the loan generates; the agent takes a cut, often cited in industry guides at somewhere between 5% and 20% of total lending revenue, with a commonly referenced split giving the lender roughly 75% and the agent 25%. When the collateral is cash, there's an extra layer: the agent reinvests that cash in short-term, high-quality instruments, earns a return on it, and passes part of that return back to the borrower as a rebate — the spread between the reinvestment return and the rebate becomes the revenue split between lender and agent. Non-cash collateral arrangements skip this step entirely, with the borrower simply paying a set fee directly. Either way, what a lender actually nets out is the market-clearing fee minus whatever cut the intermediary takes.

## What Disappears While Shares Are on Loan — and What Doesn't

The single biggest thing that changes while shares are out on loan is voting rights. Because legal title passes to the borrower, [shareholder voting rights](/en/basics/shareholder-voting-quorum-explained/) go with it. If a pension fund has lent out a large chunk of its position in a company right before that company's shareholder meeting, the fund — the original, long-term owner — may simply have no vote to cast. That's exactly why some institutional lenders temporarily recall shares ahead of meetings with sensitive agenda items, specifically to get their voting rights back in time.

Economic rights, however, aren't lost. If the stock pays a dividend while the loan is outstanding, the borrower has to pay the lender an amount equal to that dividend — commonly called a "manufactured dividend." This is the exact same payment the [short selling](/en/basics/short-selling-explained/) lesson described from the other direction, where a short seller has to compensate the lender for dividends paid during the short. Seen from the lender's side, that payment is simply dividend income flowing back as promised. In short, a lender gives up the vote temporarily but keeps collecting on two fronts: the lending fee and the dividend, made whole either way.

## Recall Risk: The Lender Can Always Ask for the Shares Back

Most lending agreements include a recall clause letting the lender demand the shares back at essentially any time — whether to sell the position outright or, as above, to reclaim voting rights before a meeting. When a recall hits, the borrower has to return the shares, and if it's a short seller who can't immediately source the same shares from another lender, it may be forced to buy the stock on the open market to cover — the same short covering mechanism covered in the short-selling lesson. When recalls cluster around a single name at the same time that replacement supply is scarce, this can become one of the triggers behind a forced short squeeze. From the lender's perspective, a recall is just an ordinary exercise of a contractual right; from the market's perspective, a wave of recalls hitting a tight lending market can add real volatility on its own.

## Takeaway

- Securities lending is a transaction where a lender lends out shares it holds against collateral and a fee; long-term institutional holders (pension funds, insurers, asset managers, foreign investors) are the dominant lenders, and some brokers now extend similar programs to retail investors.
- The borrow fee isn't fixed — it's set purely by supply and demand, ranging from roughly 0.1–1% annualized on liquid blue chips to 10%+ on heavily shorted, hard-to-borrow names.
- Lending agents typically intermediate for large institutions, taking a cut (often 5–20%) of lending revenue, with cash-collateral deals adding a reinvestment-and-rebate layer on top.
- Voting rights pass to the borrower while shares are on loan, but dividend rights are preserved through a manufactured-dividend payment; lenders can recall shares at any time, and clustered recalls can trigger forced short covering.

## FAQ

### Can retail investors earn income by lending out their own shares?
Yes, where a broker offers the program — a growing number do. Availability, eligible stocks, and fee splits vary by broker, so check the specific program's terms before assuming your holdings qualify.

### Can a lender still sell shares that are out on loan?
Generally yes, by exercising the recall right to get the shares back first. How quickly that recall actually completes — same day or with a short delay — depends on the lending agreement and the broker's systems.

### Is securities lending the same thing as short selling?
No. Securities lending is the lending transaction itself — borrowing and returning shares. [Short selling](/en/basics/short-selling-explained/) is what a borrower does with those shares afterward: selling them on the open market to bet on a decline. Borrowed shares get used for other purposes too, such as covering settlement shortfalls.

### What does a high borrow fee on a stock actually signal?
It means demand to borrow that stock — usually short-selling demand — exceeds the available lendable supply. That can be read as a sign of how much bearish positioning exists against the name, though how to act on that signal is strategy-course territory, not something this lesson covers.
