## What Happened

Two very different pieces of news hit the market within minutes of each other before the bell on Friday, November 13 - one macro, one company-specific - and together they pulled two of the market's biggest megacaps in opposite directions.

First, the macro news. At 8:30 a.m. ET, the Bureau of Labor Statistics released October's Producer Price Index, and it came in cooler than Wall Street feared. Headline PPI rose 0.3% month over month, below the 0.4% consensus, and 6.5% year over year, versus an expected 6.6%. Core PPI, which strips out food and energy, rose 0.3%, exactly matching expectations. The reason this mattered so much: just a month earlier, September's PPI had come in red-hot at 1.3-1.4% month over month and 6.3% year over year, a surprise that had pushed the market-implied odds of a December Fed rate hike to nearly 90%. October's softer print was the first real signal that September's spike might have been a one-off rather than the start of a trend.

Futures moved almost instantly on the release. Dow futures, which had been sitting at a quiet +0.1% (roughly 68 points) in the pre-dawn hours, jumped to as much as +226 points (about 0.4%) once the data crossed the wires. Nasdaq 100 futures flipped from flat to positive. The 10-year Treasury yield held around 5.10%, staying above the symbolically important 5% threshold, while the VIX - which had climbed to around 18 the day before - eased back somewhat on the news.

Against that friendlier macro backdrop, Nvidia shares rose roughly 2.3% in premarket trading - a sharp turnaround from Thursday, November 12, when the stock had plunged as much as 13% intraday. That earlier drop followed the news that CEO Jensen Huang had resigned and CFO Colette Kress had been named interim CEO (see our earlier coverage: [Jensen Huang Resigns as Nvidia CEO](/en/news/jensen-huang-resigns-colette-kress-interim-ceo-stock-drops-bernstein-cuts-target/)). A day later, markets appeared to be digesting that shock and buying back in. In fact, Nvidia's 4.34% decline in Thursday's regular session was the single biggest drag behind that day's broader slide - the S&P 500 closed down 0.35% at 7,592.40 and the Nasdaq Composite fell 0.39% to 25,366.09, with decliners (377) outnumbering advancers (124) by roughly three to one.

Microsoft moved the opposite way on the very same morning. A global outage hit several of its core services - Outlook, Teams, Copilot, OneDrive, and Azure were all affected - starting the previous day and dragging on for at least 18 hours. Downdetector reports peaked at nearly 130,000 before falling below 10,000 by the afternoon as services gradually recovered, but the duration of the disruption kept investor unease elevated well into Friday morning. Microsoft shares fell roughly 1% to 2.7% in premarket trading, and some estimates put the potential market-value hit from the outage at as much as $100 billion.

## Why One Macro Tailwind Moved Two Megacaps in Opposite Directions

This divergence is a clean illustration of how macro factors and idiosyncratic, stock-specific risk operate on entirely different layers at the same time. A PPI release is a macro variable that shifts the market's expectations for the discount rate used to value future cash flows across nearly the entire market. Cooler-than-expected inflation data reads as a sign that the Fed has less need to hike again in December, which lowers the discount rate applied to growth and tech names broadly - and that tends to matter most for stocks that got hit hardest by stock-specific bad news the day before. Nvidia, which had been sold off for a reason that had nothing to do with chip demand or fundamentals, was a natural candidate for mean-reversion buying once the macro backdrop turned supportive. In other words, Nvidia's bounce wasn't really "people buying Nvidia because PPI was good" - it was closer to "people buying back a stock that had been oversold for unrelated reasons, in a window when the macro tape made it easier to do so."

Microsoft's decline, by contrast, has nothing to do with discount rates at all. It's a pure operational event. A global service outage doesn't immediately change next quarter's revenue or earnings estimates, but markets treat prolonged outages as a trust problem. Because Azure and Microsoft 365 are core, recurring-revenue cash cows built around enterprise customers, a sufficiently long and visible outage raises a legitimate question about reliability at scale - and that reliability concern gets partially priced into the stock in real time, independent of whatever the macro backdrop happens to be doing that day. No amount of good inflation data offsets a same-day, company-specific headline like this one.

The key investing principle here is that "the same macro event, on the same day" does not move every stock in the same direction. If you try to explain your entire portfolio through one macro narrative - in this case, "inflation cooled" - you'll miss stocks like Microsoft that are being driven by a distinct, same-day event layered on top. The reverse is also true: reading Microsoft's drop as evidence that "the market is weak today" would miss the PPI-driven rebound happening simultaneously in Nvidia and much of the semiconductor complex. It's also worth noting that Nvidia remained well below its 52-week high even after the bounce - Bernstein had cut its price target on the stock in half, from $400 to $200, just a day earlier - which supports reading this move as a reversion from an extreme oversold condition rather than a verdict that the leadership crisis is resolved.

Microsoft's outage is also a reminder of concentration risk at the infrastructure level inside a single company. Azure, Microsoft 365, and Teams all sit on shared underlying infrastructure, so one failure point can cascade across multiple customer-facing products simultaneously - a classic single-point-of-failure problem. This isn't unique to Microsoft; AWS and Google Cloud have faced similar scrutiny after their own high-profile outages, and in each case the affected company's stock has typically seen some short-term pressure tied directly to reliability concerns rather than to underlying demand.

## What to Take Away From This

The first lesson is not to assume one macro data point dictates every stock's direction on a given day. A benign inflation print can lift the broad market while a same-day, company-specific event - here, a global outage - pulls an individual megacap the other way. Separate the macro layer from the stock-specific layer when you're reading a day's price action.

The second lesson is that oversold bounces tend to get amplified when they coincide with a favorable macro shift. Nvidia's rebound illustrates how a stock beaten down by company-specific news can see outsized buying interest once the broader environment turns supportive - but that's a reversion trade, not evidence that the underlying issue has actually been resolved.

The third lesson is that large platform companies built on shared, centralized infrastructure carry a persistent outage risk. The bigger the share of revenue tied to cloud and subscription services, the more a prolonged, visible outage can morph from an operational hiccup into a genuine trust-and-reliability concern that shows up in the stock.

The fourth lesson is that the direction of an inflation surprise feeds directly into rate expectations, which in turn affects the valuation of almost every rate-sensitive growth stock. A softer-than-expected PPI, like today's, reads as reduced pressure on the Fed to tighten further - a tailwind that disproportionately helps higher-multiple tech names.

## FAQ

### Why does the October PPI report matter so much?
The Producer Price Index measures price changes at the wholesale and input level, often serving as an early signal of inflation pressure before it shows up in consumer prices. October's softer-than-expected reading suggests September's hot surprise may have been a one-off rather than the start of a new trend, which eases some of the pressure for the Fed to hike rates again in December.

### Does Nvidia's rebound mean the leadership crisis is over?
Not necessarily. While Colette Kress has stepped in as interim CEO and Harvey Jones was named independent board chair, real uncertainty remains until a permanent CEO is named and until Nvidia reports third-quarter earnings on November 17. This rebound looks more like a technical reversion from an oversold extreme, amplified by favorable macro data, than a sign that the underlying situation has been resolved.

### How bad was the Microsoft outage?
It affected core services including Outlook, Teams, Copilot, OneDrive, and Azure. Downdetector reports peaked at nearly 130,000 before falling below 10,000 by the afternoon. The disruption lasted at least 18 hours, with gradual recovery through the day. Some estimates pegged the potential market-value impact at up to $100 billion.

### How might this PPI data affect the Fed's December decision?
PPI is just one of several inputs the Fed weighs, alongside upcoming jobs data and the Consumer Price Index ahead of the December 9 meeting. Still, it's notable that December rate-hike bets, which had surged after September's hot PPI surprise, could ease somewhat following this cooler October reading.
