## What Does "Traded Over the Counter" Actually Mean Here?

Financial news in Korea occasionally mentions a private company's shares "trading at such-and-such a price on the OTC market." Who buys and sells stock in a company that was never listed on an exchange, and how? A large share of this activity doesn't happen through a broker's messaging app or a personal connection — it runs through **K-OTC**, an official market operated by Korea's self-regulatory body for securities firms. You can place orders the same way you would for a KOSPI or KOSDAQ stock, through your regular brokerage app, which makes it feel familiar. But the mechanics underneath are quite different from exchange trading. This lesson covers what K-OTC actually is, which companies show up on it, how prices get set, and how the tax treatment differs from ordinary listed-stock investing.

## A Market Run by an Association, Not an Exchange

Unlike the KOSPI and KOSDAQ markets, which the Korea Exchange (KRX) operates, K-OTC is run by the **Korea Financial Investment Association (KOFIA)**, the self-regulatory body for Korea's securities industry, under the Financial Investment Services and Capital Markets Act. It launched in 2014 as the successor to an earlier platform called FreeBoard, with the goal of giving unlisted companies a liquidity channel and giving investors an official, regulated alternative to risky private deals. Because it runs through ordinary brokerage accounts with settlement cleared through Korea's central securities depository, it counts as part of the regulated market infrastructure — but it is not an exchange listing in the legal sense. A company trading on K-OTC has not gone through the listing review that KOSPI or KOSDAQ requires, which is exactly what sets its legal status apart from the two exchange markets.

## Which Companies Show Up — Registered vs. Designated

Companies land on K-OTC through one of two tracks. The first is **registered companies**, which apply to KOFIA voluntarily and meet a set of financial and disclosure requirements — an unqualified external audit opinion, standardized share certificates, and similar baseline conditions. These are typically unlisted firms actively seeking a liquidity channel for their shareholders. The second is **designated companies**, which KOFIA places on the market directly, without the company's own application, once it meets certain threshold conditions. Because this happens without the company's consent, some designated companies have pushed back and sought removal from the list. The practical difference that matters most to investors is disclosure: designated companies generally have no standalone disclosure obligation on K-OTC itself, and even registered companies fall well short of the detailed, timely disclosure that exchange-listed companies must provide. In short, a stock being tradable on K-OTC says nothing about whether you'll get the kind of information access you're used to from a KOSPI or KOSDAQ filing.

## How Trading Differs — Matched Bargaining, Not a Continuous Order Book

KOSPI and KOSDAQ run on continuous double-auction trading, where buy and sell orders accumulate in a live order book and match by price-then-time priority. K-OTC works on a different principle: **matched bargain trading**. A buy order only executes when there's a sell order at the exact same price. If the two prices differ even slightly, the order simply sits unmatched. This is a much thinner market than an exchange order book — plenty of names see only a handful of trades a day. The daily price-swing limit is set at plus or minus 30% of the prior day's volume-weighted average price, numerically the same band KOSPI and KOSDAQ use. But because so much less volume forms that reference price in the first place, the effective gap between quotes — and the real-world volatility an investor experiences — can be far wider than on an exchange-listed stock. The number of names actually tradable on K-OTC is also a small fraction of all unlisted companies in Korea.

## A Worked Example — How a Matched-Bargain Trade Actually Clears

Say a hypothetical unlisted company, H Corp, is a registered company on K-OTC. Investor A wants to buy 100 shares at 10,000 won each. At that moment, the only standing sell orders are 50 shares at 9,800 won and 30 shares at 10,500 won. On an exchange, price-time priority would fill the 9,800-won sell order immediately against part of the buy order, leaving the rest in the book. Under K-OTC's matched-bargain rule, nothing happens unless a sell order appears at exactly 10,000 won — A's order just sits there unfilled. To trade right now, A would have to resubmit at 9,800 or 10,500 won, matching an order that already exists. The lesson here: on K-OTC, what decides whether a trade clears isn't how much you're willing to pay, but whether a matching order exists at that exact price at that exact moment — and the thinner the name, the more often that simply isn't the case.

## Taxes — A Higher Transaction Tax, But a Possible Capital Gains Exemption

K-OTC's tax treatment cuts in two opposite directions compared to listed stocks. The [securities transaction tax](/en/basics/securities-transaction-tax-korea/), levied on the sale amount regardless of profit or loss, is set higher on K-OTC than on KOSPI or KOSDAQ. [Capital gains tax](/en/basics/capital-gains-tax-stocks-korea/) runs the other way. For listed stocks, an ordinary small shareholder — someone below the "major shareholder" threshold — generally pays no capital gains tax at all. Unlisted shares work on the opposite default: capital gains are taxable regardless of shareholding size. The exception is that a small shareholder (below set ownership and holding-value thresholds) who trades shares of a small or mid-sized company specifically through K-OTC can be exempted from that capital gains tax. This single exception is really the reason K-OTC exists as a formal channel in the first place: trade the exact same unlisted shares privately, outside K-OTC, and the capital gains tax obligation applies regardless of whether you're a major shareholder, while routing the trade through K-OTC opens a path out of that liability for qualifying small shareholders. Because thresholds and rates shift with tax-law revisions, always check the current rules with the National Tax Service or a tax professional before trading.

## What Thin Liquidity Actually Means for You

K-OTC's most fundamental risk is liquidity itself. Under matched-bargain trading, your order simply won't fill if no counterparty exists at your price — buying can stall, and selling in a hurry can mean settling for a much worse price just to get a trade done at all. It's the same underlying mechanism covered in [ETF tracking error and premium/discount](/en/basics/etf-tracking-error-premium-discount/) and [closed-end fund discounts](/en/basics/closed-end-fund-discount-explained/) — a shortage of tradable supply pulling price away from underlying value — just showing up in a far more extreme form. Layer on weaker disclosure requirements than exchange-listed peers, and verifying a company's actual financial condition becomes much harder to do on your own. It's also worth knowing that a meaningful share of K-OTC registered and designated companies run at a loss or fall short of their periodic reporting obligations, something worth weighing before committing capital.

## Not the Same as Private Off-Platform Trading

K-OTC is often confused with **private unlisted-share deals** arranged through brokerage platforms or informal communities. K-OTC runs through KOFIA, a regulated body, with settlement cleared through Korea's securities depository. A private deal outside K-OTC is closer to a person-to-person negotiation over price, shares, and payment, with no regulated intermediary standing behind it — meaning that if you wire money and the shares never arrive (or the reverse), there's little recourse. "Buying unlisted shares" sounds like the same activity either way, but whether it runs through K-OTC's regulated channel is the single biggest factor separating a safe transaction from a risky one.

## How It Differs From a Direct Listing or a Delisting

K-OTC is easy to confuse with a [direct listing](/en/basics/direct-listing-vs-ipo/), but they're different categories entirely. A direct listing is a method of *entering* an exchange — putting existing shareholders' stakes directly onto KOSPI, KOSDAQ, or a US exchange without an underwriter. K-OTC is a secondary market that lets shares *keep trading* for companies that never went through that listing process, or no longer can. It's a question of ongoing liquidity, not of market entry. Separately, some companies that get pushed through [administrative designation and delisting](/en/basics/administrative-issue-delisting-korea/) and exit the exchange end up designated onto K-OTC afterward, where trading continues on a limited basis. The same weak disclosure and thin liquidity apply there too, which is exactly why a stock that moved from an exchange to K-OTC after delisting deserves extra caution rather than less.

## What to Take Away as an Investor

K-OTC gives investors curious about unlisted companies a legitimate, regulated way in — without the fraud risk that comes with a purely private deal. But carrying over habits from trading KOSPI or KOSDAQ stocks can trip you up in a few specific ways: matched-bargain trading means your order may simply not fill at your price, weaker disclosure means you're on your own to verify company fundamentals, and the universe of tradable names is small to begin with. On the upside, the capital gains exemption available to small shareholders in small and mid-sized companies trading through K-OTC is a real tax advantage worth knowing about.

## Key Takeaways

- K-OTC is an unlisted-stock market run by KOFIA, not the Korea Exchange — a regulated channel you access through an ordinary brokerage account, placing orders much like you would for a listed stock.
- Companies enter either as **registered companies** (voluntary application) or **designated companies** (KOFIA places them without consent), and both face far looser disclosure obligations than exchange-listed firms.
- Prices are set through **matched-bargain trading** rather than a continuous order book — a trade only clears when a buy and sell order share the exact same price — with a daily band of plus or minus 30% of the prior day's weighted average price.
- The securities transaction tax rate runs higher than on exchanges, but small shareholders trading small/mid-cap company shares through K-OTC can qualify for a capital gains tax exemption that doesn't apply to private off-platform deals.
- Liquidity is thin and disclosure can be weak; private unlisted-share deals struck outside K-OTC carry much greater fraud risk since no regulated clearing body stands behind them.

## FAQ

### Can I buy shares of any unlisted Korean company on K-OTC?
No. Only companies that have been registered or designated are tradable there, a small fraction of all unlisted companies in Korea. You can check whether a specific company is listed on K-OTC through its official website or your brokerage's stock search.

### Can I trade on K-OTC directly from my regular brokerage account?
Yes. Most Korean brokerages support K-OTC trading through the same app used for KOSPI and KOSDAQ, either on the same screen or a separate menu. Keep in mind that order matching works differently (matched-bargain, not a continuous order book) and that commission rates for K-OTC trades can differ from standard exchange trades.

### Why is disclosure so much weaker for K-OTC companies?
Designated companies carry no standalone disclosure obligation on K-OTC itself, and even registered companies aren't held to the detailed, timely disclosure standard that exchange-listed companies must meet. As a result, K-OTC investors often have to rely on limited sources — audit reports and outside news coverage — to form a view on a company's condition.

### Is K-OTC the same thing as KONEX?
No. KONEX is a formal exchange market run by the Korea Exchange, designed to lower the listing bar for smaller companies relative to KOSPI and KOSDAQ. K-OTC is a market for companies that never went through an exchange listing at all, operated by an association rather than the exchange — the legal status and the operating body are entirely different.
