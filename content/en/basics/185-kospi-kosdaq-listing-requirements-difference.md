## That Small "Kospi" or "Kosdaq" Tag Next to a Ticker

Open a Korean brokerage app and search for a stock, and right next to the ticker you'll see a small tag reading either "Kospi" or "Kosdaq." Most investors treat it as little more than a venue label — which exchange floor the stock happens to trade on. But behind that tag sits a completely different set of listing requirements that determined whether the company could go public in the first place. Everyone has heard the shorthand: Kospi is where large, established names like Samsung Electronics and Hyundai Motor sit, while Kosdaq is built for smaller, growth-stage and venture companies. The more interesting question is why that split exists, exactly what separates the two sets of requirements, and what that difference actually means for the risk you're taking on as an investor. This lesson covers why Kospi's and Kosdaq's listing systems were designed so differently and what that structural gap implies for investors — not whether any specific stock is better off on one market or the other, which is a strategies-course judgment call, not a conceptual one.

## Why One Exchange Runs Two Separate Markets

Korea Exchange (KRX) is legally a single exchange, but internally it operates two markets — Kospi (the main board) and Kosdaq — each with its own listing rulebook. The reason is straightforward: the companies that want to go public are too different in kind to be judged by one yardstick. A conglomerate with decades of steady revenue and profit, and an early-stage biotech or semiconductor-equipment venture with real technology but no profit yet, can't both be fairly measured against the same bar. Set the bar at the conglomerate's level, and growth-stage companies never get access to public markets at all. Set it at the venture's level, and large-cap investors lose the stability and information reliability they expect. So Kospi was built as a stability-first market that demands "proven scale and track record," while Kosdaq was built as a capital-raising market that accepts "provable growth potential" in place of scale. That design philosophy is what produces the concrete differences in listing tracks below.

## Kospi: A Single Bar for "Already-Proven" Companies

A Kospi listing review generally assumes a company has already built substantial scale and a track record. Figures commonly cited in practice put the general track at roughly 30 billion won or more in equity capital with at least three years of operating history, plus a revenue test requiring either 100 billion won in revenue for the most recent fiscal year (with a three-year average above 70 billion won), or, as an alternative, a market cap above 600 billion won combined with equity capital above 200 billion won. Distribution requirements — a minimum number of general shareholders and free-float ratio — and a clean audit opinion come on top. The key point: companies seeking a Kospi listing have almost no exception track built around unproven future growth. Kospi bakes its philosophy — accept only companies that have already demonstrated results repeatedly — directly into the listing bar. (These figures are commonly cited reference points, revised periodically under the exchange's rules; a 2026 reform is also phasing in higher maintenance thresholds.)

## Kosdaq: Multiple Tracks That Reward "Potential," Not Just Profit

What fundamentally sets Kosdaq apart is that it runs several distinct tracks at once rather than a single bar. The general-company track is built around a "profit-realized" path — a set level of pre-tax recurring profit plus an equity-capital threshold — or an alternative that looks at market cap and revenue alongside profit. What makes Kosdaq genuinely different, though, is its **pre-profit company exception**. A company still posting losses can list through a track based only on market cap combined with revenue, or on equity capital alone — and the best-known version is **technology special listing**: clear a minimum technology-evaluation grade from two independent outside evaluators, and your revenue or operating-profit requirements get waived or deferred entirely. Add a **growth-potential special listing**, where the lead underwriting brokerage effectively vouches for the company's prospects, and a track for larger pre-profit firms meeting equity-capital or revenue thresholds, and Kosdaq becomes a market open not just to companies making money today, but to companies whose ability to make money tomorrow can be independently verified.

## What Tech-Special Listing Has Done: Over Half of Kosdaq's Market Cap

How much the pre-profit exception has reshaped Kosdaq's actual market structure shows up clearly in the numbers. Technology special listing, first introduced in 2005, has now produced more than 300 cumulative listed companies as of 2026, and reporting indicates these tech-special-listed companies accounted for 52.4% of Kosdaq's total market capitalization as of 2025. In other words, more than half of Kosdaq's total market value now comes from companies that would have been categorically ineligible under a traditional profit-based bar. That can be read as the system working exactly as designed rather than failing: industries like new-drug development or semiconductor equipment require heavy upfront investment and long development timelines, often long enough that by the time real revenue shows up, the company may already have listed on a market overseas instead. At the same time, this same statistic is a warning for anyone investing in Kosdaq: being listed, by itself, says nothing about whether a company is actually profitable. The recurring controversy over how objective and rigorous the technology evaluations really are comes from exactly this tension.

## The 2026 Reform: Both Markets Raising Their Maintenance Bar Together

The gap isn't confined to the initial listing review — the ongoing-maintenance standards a company must keep clearing to stay listed also differ by market, and both have recently moved to tighten together. In February 2026, Korea's Financial Services Commission and Korea Exchange announced a delisting reform plan, with detailed rule changes finalized that May. The centerpiece: the market-cap threshold required to keep a listing rises in steps — for Kospi, from 30 billion won in July 2026 to 50 billion won by January 2027; for Kosdaq, from 20 billion won to 30 billion won over the same window, with the review cadence also sped up from annual to twice-yearly. A new rule brought stocks trading below 1,000 won ("penny stocks") into delisting review starting in July 2026, and the first delisting reportedly triggered under the tightened market-cap standard occurred that June — confirming the reform has already produced real exits, not just an announcement. Just as the two markets' listing "entrances" use deliberately different numbers, their delisting "exits" differ too, but both are now moving the same direction: tighter.

## What This Actually Means for Investors: Index Composition and Risk Profile

This listing-requirement gap isn't an abstract regulatory story — it shows up directly in the index composition and risk character investors actually encounter. Kospi-centered benchmarks like the Kospi 200 are populated with large, rigorously vetted companies that cleared the strict track described above, while the Kosdaq 150 includes growth-stage companies that listed through the pre-profit exception. That structurally changes the risk profile of passive money tracking each index. Applying the volatility concept from [Beta](/en/basics/beta-and-volatility/), the Kosdaq index tends to swing harder than Kospi under the same macro shock, simply because it carries a relatively larger weight of companies whose earnings outcomes are genuinely uncertain. Take a hypothetical: Company N, a still-unprofitable biotech with 5 billion won in equity capital, could never clear Kospi's general track — but a sufficient grade from an outside technology evaluation opens a path onto Kosdaq through the tech-special track. An investor in Company N is taking on a fundamentally different kind of risk than a Kospi large-cap investor would — specifically, the risk that an as-yet-unproven technology fails to reach commercialization. A Kospi-listed company, by contrast, at least cleared a minimum filter of already-verified revenue and profit at the time of listing. None of this collapses into "Kospi is always safe, Kosdaq is always risky," though — regardless of which track a company listed through, its current financial condition and business outlook are the more direct determinants of actual risk.

The valuation lens applied to each market tends to shift along with this split. Kospi large-caps already generate stable earnings, so they can be assessed reasonably consistently with traditional earnings- and asset-based metrics like [PER and PBR](/en/basics/valuation-multiples-per-pbr-psr/). A Kosdaq company that listed through the tech-special track, on the other hand, often has no net income at all, or a negative one, making a PER calculation impossible in the first place. Such stocks instead get valued on forward-looking yardsticks — revenue growth rates, or clinical and technology development milestones — which means a wrong assumption by whoever is doing the valuing can move the stock price by a much wider margin. The market label itself, in the end, isn't a number that tells you how risky a stock is — but it's a useful signal for how that stock should be valued in the first place.

## Key Takeaways

- Kospi and Kosdaq run on the same exchange but maintain separate listing rulebooks, built on the premise that the companies seeking each market are fundamentally different in kind.
- Kospi centers on a single track demanding already-proven scale and track record — equity capital, revenue, market cap — with almost no exception path based on unproven growth potential.
- Kosdaq runs multiple tracks, including a profit-realized path alongside pre-profit exceptions like technology special listing and growth-potential special listing, letting unprofitable tech and biotech companies list.
- Technology-special-listed companies had grown to account for 52.4% of Kosdaq's total market cap as of 2025 — a sign the exception track is working as intended, though listing itself still doesn't guarantee profitability.
- Korea's 2026 delisting reform is raising the market-cap maintenance threshold on both markets in steps, and added new rules like delisting review for sub-1,000-won penny stocks.
- This structural gap carries straight through into index composition and risk profile — Kospi 200 vs. Kosdaq 150 — but the market label can't substitute for checking an individual company's actual risk.

## FAQ

### Is a Kospi stock always safer than a Kosdaq stock?
Not necessarily. Kospi does mean a company cleared a larger-scale, track-record-based filter at the time of listing, but its financial condition can still deteriorate afterward, and Kosdaq is home to plenty of consistently profitable, well-run companies. Checking an individual company's current financial statements and business outlook is a more direct way to judge risk than relying on which market it's listed on.

### What should I specifically check before investing in a tech-special-listed company?
Since the company listed without being profitable yet, traditional metrics like revenue or net income matter less than the stage of technology commercialization, the cash burn rate relative to R&D spending (how long its cash runway actually is), and the specific basis behind whatever technology-evaluation grade it received.

### If a Kosdaq company moves to Kospi, does it get re-reviewed under Kospi's requirements?
Yes. Moving to Kospi through a [transfer listing](/en/basics/transfer-listing-kosdaq-to-kospi/) requires clearing roughly the same equity-capital, revenue, and distribution requirements as a fresh Kospi listing. Even if the company originally listed on Kosdaq through an exception track like technology special listing, that exception doesn't carry over — it has to clear Kospi's general-track standards on its own.
