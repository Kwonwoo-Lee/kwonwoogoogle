## What Happened

The Federal Open Market Committee voted 10-2 on October 28 to hold its benchmark rate at 3.75%-4.00%, exactly what markets expected: Kalshi prediction-market traders had priced a roughly 93% probability of a hold going into the meeting, and the outcome matched that pricing almost to the decimal. On the surface, this looks like the least newsworthy possible result - a non-decision that confirms the consensus. The actual story is in who voted against it and why.

Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack both dissented, and both wanted a quarter-point hike rather than a hold. Hammack dissenting toward tighter policy is not new - she was one of three officials (alongside Neel Kashkari and Lorie Logan) who pushed for a hike back at the July meeting, well before the committee's actual first hike in three years, delivered unanimously in September, pushed the range from 3.50%-3.75% up to the current 3.75%-4.00%. What is new, and what separates this dissent from July's, is Cook. She has generally been read by markets as one of the more labor-market-focused, dovish voices on the committee. A governor with that reputation breaking ranks to argue for a hike - rather than a hawkish regional bank president doing it again - is a meaningfully different signal about how broadly inflation concern has spread inside the building.

Fed Chair Kevin Warsh used his press conference to address two other threads directly. Asked whether the upcoming midterm elections shaped the committee's thinking, he said politics played "no role" in the decision. And on the question of what comes next, he was unusually blunt: "I don't believe in forward guidance, absent exigent circumstances, absent a crisis." That is a deliberate break from the pattern most recent Fed chairs have followed, where some verbal signal about the likely next move gets dropped into the statement or the Q&A even when officials insist they're "data-dependent." Warsh is explicitly refusing to give markets that anchor heading into the December 9-10 meeting, which leaves each economic data print between now and then carrying more independent weight for pricing the next move than it would under a Fed willing to pre-commit.

Warsh also announced a structural change to how the committee operates going forward: one of the Fed's regularly scheduled 2027 meetings will be devoted entirely to a broad review of monetary policy strategy rather than a rate decision, and starting in 2028, two of the committee's eight annual meetings will be strategy-focused while the remaining six set policy. One strategist characterized the overall October outcome as a "hawkish hold" - a hold in name, paired with signaling that keeps the door open to further tightening rather than closing it. Equities gained modestly in the hours that followed, a muted reaction consistent with a decision that mostly matched what had already been priced in.

## Why This Matters for Markets

The dissent lineup is the part of this decision that actually moves the probability distribution for December and beyond. When hawkish dissents come only from regional bank presidents - the traditional role Hammack, Kashkari, and Logan have played all year - markets can reasonably treat it as the same small, predictable bloc restating its position. When a Fed governor seen as closer to the dovish end of the spectrum joins that dissent, it suggests the inflation data since September has been uncomfortable enough to move centrist opinion, not just reinforce the hawks who were already there. That is a harder signal to dismiss, and it is part of why some observers are reading this hold as "hawkish" rather than simply neutral.

It also matters that the dissent count actually narrowed from three in July to two in October, even as the committee's rhetoric arguably got more hawkish. Fewer dissents could be read as the committee coalescing around Warsh's view that a pause is appropriate for now - but the change in who is dissenting cuts the other way, since it shows the underlying discomfort with current inflation trends has not gone away, it has just moved to different people. Both readings are defensible, which is itself the point: this meeting didn't resolve the hawk-dove tension on the committee, it just redistributed it.

Warsh's refusal to offer forward guidance compounds that ambiguity by design. Markets built their rate-path models over the past two Fed chairs partly around reading subtle hints dropped in statements and press conferences. Removing that channel means each upcoming print - payrolls, CPI, PCE - will move December pricing by more than it otherwise would, because there's no accompanying Fed commentary to soften or confirm the market's read of the data. That raises the odds of sharper repricing around each release between now and the December meeting, and by extension, of sharper moves in rate-sensitive sectors like growth and small-cap stocks.

The timing compounds the effect further. The decision landed hours before Microsoft and Alphabet reported fiscal results after the same day's closing bell, with Amazon's own report following a day later on October 29 and Apple's on November 2. Investors had almost no time to digest the Fed outcome in isolation before mega-cap earnings and their AI-spending guidance started competing for attention, which is exactly the kind of compressed, catalyst-stacked week that tends to produce outsized volatility even when any single event, taken alone, looks as uneventful as a widely-expected hold.

## What to Take Away From This

- **Watch who dissents, not just the vote count.** A traditionally dovish governor joining a hawkish dissent is a stronger signal about how far inflation concern has spread across the committee than a simple tally of "how many officials disagreed."
- **No forward guidance means higher data-dependent volatility.** With Warsh explicitly declining to signal the likely direction of the December decision, each upcoming jobs, CPI, and PCE release is likely to move rate-path pricing more sharply than it would if the Fed were still dropping hints.
- **A governance change is a separate story from the rate decision itself.** The 2027-2028 calendar restructuring toward dedicated strategy-review meetings is a long-run institutional shift, not a signal about what the Fed does with rates in December.
- **Stacked catalysts amplify each other.** When a Fed decision lands the same week as mega-cap earnings, the market has less time to price each event independently, which tends to produce sharper short-term moves than either event would generate on its own.

## FAQ

### Why does Lisa Cook's dissent matter more than Beth Hammack's?

Hammack has dissented toward tighter policy repeatedly this year and is generally viewed as one of the committee's more hawkish voices, so her dissent confirms an existing pattern. Cook has typically been read as more focused on labor-market strength and more dovish overall. Her choosing to dissent in favor of a hike suggests the inflation data since September's hike has been uncomfortable enough to shift opinion closer to the center of the committee, not just among officials who were already leaning hawkish.

### What happens next, and when is the December meeting?

The next FOMC meeting is scheduled for December 9-10. Because Chair Warsh explicitly declined to offer forward guidance at this meeting, there is no official signal from the Fed itself about which way that decision is likely to go. Expect each major data release between now and then - payrolls, CPI, and PCE inflation in particular - to carry outsized weight in how traders price the odds of a hold versus a further hike.

### What does "no forward guidance" actually change for investors?

In practice, it removes a tool markets have relied on to anticipate Fed moves before they happen. Without hints dropped in statements or press conferences, investors have to rely more heavily on raw economic data to estimate the next decision, which tends to produce sharper market reactions to each individual data release rather than a smoother, more anticipated drift in rate expectations.
