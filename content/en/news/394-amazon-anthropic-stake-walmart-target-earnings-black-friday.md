## What Happened

Black Friday is supposed to be the one week of the year when America's biggest retailers get the benefit of the doubt. Instead, as US markets head into the holiday shopping season's kickoff day, Amazon, Walmart and Target have each had a rough few days in their stock price - and for three almost entirely unrelated reasons.

Amazon's slide is the strangest of the three, because it has almost nothing to do with retail. Amazon holds a roughly 14.6% economic stake in Anthropic, the AI company behind Claude, built up largely through AWS cloud-credit notes that converted into Anthropic equity around its IPO. When Anthropic debuted on the Nasdaq in mid-November at an $83 IPO price (raising about $81.3 billion in a record IPO for the sector, with a fully diluted valuation of roughly $1.5 trillion), the stock initially traded about 20% above that price. Amazon shares wobbled even before the debut: on November 11, an amended IPO filing detailing how those AWS notes would convert knocked Amazon down 1.7% to $266.17. On November 19, Anthropic's second trading day, Amazon fell another 3.9% to $257.37, the worst move among the Anthropic-linked megacaps that session. The real damage came on November 25, when a broad Nasdaq selloff tied to a hot October inflation reading and new tariff threats pushed the index down nearly 4% - and Anthropic's own shares slipped below their $83 IPO price for the first time. Amazon fell 5.6% that day to $238.28. Because the stake is marked to Anthropic's daily share price, and each $1 move in Anthropic reportedly swings the paper value of Amazon's position by roughly $1.9 billion, Amazon's own stock has effectively become a leveraged bet on a six-week-old IPO it doesn't control and can't vote.

Walmart and Target's problem looks completely different on paper: both companies delivered genuinely strong quarters. Walmart's November 19 report showed revenue up 5.2% to $188.8 billion, US comparable sales up 2.9%, and e-commerce surging - up 17% globally and 23% in the US, with CEO John Furner calling online sales the "primary driver again" of growth. Walmart raised its full-year guidance for the third time this year, lifting its sales-growth outlook to 4.9%-5.2% and adjusted EPS guidance to $2.84-$2.89. The stock still fell about 1.5% that morning. Target's November 18 report was arguably even stronger: comparable sales jumped 5.6% (traffic alone rose 5%), net sales rose 7.1% to $27.1 billion - the company's best quarterly sales growth in more than five years, a sharp reversal from a 2.7% comp decline a year earlier - and adjusted EPS of $2.65 crushed the $2.08 analyst estimate. Target raised its full-year sales-growth outlook to roughly 6% and lifted adjusted EPS guidance to $10.65-$11.40. Yet the stock traded flat to slightly lower in the following session.

## Why Good News Didn't Mean Good Stock Moves

Three different mechanisms are at work here, and all three are worth understanding separately because they show up constantly in earnings season, not just this week.

Amazon's case is about exposure that has nothing to do with the business you think you own. When a company holds a large equity stake in another publicly traded firm, that stake gets marked to market and flows straight into the parent's balance sheet and investor sentiment - even though it has zero effect on same-day retail sales or AWS cloud revenue. Amazon's core businesses are almost certainly having a strong Black Friday; that reality is simply being drowned out by volatility in a six-week-old stock that trades on its own news cycle entirely. The lesson generalizes: before buying a stock for its core business, check whether management has bolted on a separate, volatile source of mark-to-market risk that can move the shares for reasons that have nothing to do with the thing you're actually betting on.

Walmart and Target's situation is the "priced for perfection" problem. Walmart had already raised guidance twice this year before November 19, so a third raise - even a real, revenue-and-earnings-backed one - was partially baked into the share price already; a guide that came in more conservative than some analysts hoped for was enough to trigger a sell-the-news reaction despite the beat. Target's case adds a second layer: about $0.50 of its $2.65 adjusted EPS came from one-time tariff refunds, not recurring operations. Experienced investors routinely strip out one-time items to judge the "real," repeatable earnings power of a business, and a headline beat that leans on a non-recurring item tends to get a more skeptical reception - especially for a stock that had already rallied more than 60% year-to-date heading into the print.

## What to Take Away From This

- **A beat-and-raise doesn't guarantee a stock pop.** Check where expectations and the stock's valuation already sit before assuming good numbers equal a rally. Walmart's third guidance raise of the year and Target's blowout comps both ran into an expectations bar that had already climbed.
- **Separate one-time items from recurring earnings quality.** Target's EPS beat leaned partly on a one-time tariff refund. When a headline number looks unusually good, check the footnotes for what's driving it before assuming it repeats next quarter.
- **A stake in another company is a second, separate risk you're taking on.** Amazon's Anthropic position means Amazon shareholders are exposed to swings in a company they don't control and can't vote on. Always ask what else is riding on a stock besides the core business you think you're buying.
- **Don't assume a calendar event like Black Friday automatically drives a stock's weekly move.** All three of these stocks moved mainly on news that had nothing to do with Friday's actual shopping-day sales figures.

## FAQ

### Why did Amazon's stock fall if Anthropic is a hot AI company?
Amazon's stock isn't reacting to Anthropic's long-term prospects - it's reacting to Anthropic's day-to-day share price, since Amazon's roughly 14.6% stake is marked to that price. Anthropic's stock has actually fallen since its mid-November debut, dropping below its $83 IPO price by November 25, which is what dragged on Amazon's shares.

### What exactly is Amazon's stake in Anthropic?
It stems largely from AWS cloud-credit notes that converted into Anthropic equity around the company's IPO, giving Amazon an economic interest (reported at roughly 14.6%) through a separate share class that carries no voting control over Anthropic.

### If Walmart and Target both beat earnings, why did their stocks fall?
Both stocks had already run up a lot and had elevated expectations baked in - Walmart had raised guidance twice earlier this year, and Target shares were up more than 60% year-to-date. A real beat that doesn't clear an already-high bar, or that leans partly on one-time items like Target's tariff refunds, can still trigger profit-taking rather than a rally.
