## What Happened

After the closing bell on Wednesday, October 7, denim apparel maker Levi Strauss & Co. (NYSE: LEVI) reported fiscal third-quarter 2026 results. Adjusted earnings per share came in at $0.48, beating the Wall Street consensus of $0.36 by more than 33% - a headline beat big enough to stand out even in a quarter full of earnings surprises.

The revenue picture told a different story. Net revenue for the quarter was $1.61 billion, missing the roughly $1.62 billion analysts had modeled by about 0.6%. More notable still was what the company did with guidance in the same release: it raised its full-year adjusted EPS outlook from a prior range of $1.46-$1.52 to $1.54-$1.56, while simultaneously cutting its full-year net revenue growth guidance to 7%, the low end of its previous 7%-7.5% range. One number went up. The other went down. That's not a contradiction investors tend to shrug off.

The hidden variable behind the mismatch is a tariff refund. Levi disclosed it received roughly $80 million in IEEPA (International Emergency Economic Powers Act) tariff refunds during the quarter - which the company said represents nearly all of what it expects to receive for the full year. After redeploying a portion of that refund back into the business, the net benefit added $0.11 to adjusted EPS. Strip that out, and management itself said EPS still grew a respectable 9% year-over-year - a much less dramatic number than the 41% increase implied by the $0.48 headline figure versus $0.34 a year earlier.

Levi shares had already closed down 4.97% at $19.51 in the regular session on October 7, caught up in a broader market slide that day as the 10-year Treasury yield pushed to its highest level since 2002 and the Dow, S&P 500, and Nasdaq all finished lower. In after-hours trading following the report, shares slipped further - roughly 1-2% more - as the market digested the revenue miss alongside the mixed guidance signals. Some analysts, including UBS ahead of the print, had already flagged that consensus estimates assumed EPS guidance would land near the top of what the company could plausibly deliver - meaning the actual raise landed almost exactly where the Street had already priced it in, leaving little room for upside surprise.

## Why an EPS Beat Didn't Lift the Stock

The first key to this story is earnings quality. When investors and analysts evaluate a quarter, the first question isn't simply whether EPS cleared consensus - it's whether that EPS came from repeatable operating profit or from a one-off item unlikely to recur, like a tax or tariff refund. In Levi's case, $0.11 of the $0.48 - a meaningful chunk of the entire beat - came from exactly that kind of one-time benefit. That money almost certainly won't show up again in the same quarter next year. Once the market registers that distinction, it starts weighting the adjusted "ex-refund" growth rate (9%) far more heavily than the headline growth rate (41%).

The second key is weakness in the direct-to-consumer (DTC) channel. Levi has spent the past several years explicitly shifting its strategy away from wholesale dependence and toward DTC - its own stores and e-commerce - as a core growth pillar. This quarter, U.S. DTC sales came in softer than expected, and overall U.S. revenue fell 1% year-over-year. The broader Americas region still grew 2%, but only because strength in international markets and wholesale offset the DTC softness at home. Management guided for DTC to return to at least mid-single-digit growth in the fourth quarter, but a forecast is not the same as a result, and investors have learned to treat Levi's DTC recovery promises with some caution after prior quarters. A soft number in the exact channel the company has staked its growth story on tends to draw more scrutiny than weakness anywhere else in the business.

The third key is what the direction of the guidance revision itself signals. Raising EPS guidance while cutting revenue guidance is typically read by the market as "we can protect profitability through cost discipline, but we're losing confidence in top-line momentum." That reading lines up with what CFO Harmit Singh said on the earnings call: the company made a deliberate decision to redeploy a majority of the tariff refund benefit back into the business during the third and fourth quarters rather than simply banking it. At the same time, Levi announced a new $100 million accelerated share repurchase program and raised its quarterly dividend by 14%. That's a two-pronged approach - reinvest for growth and return cash to shareholders simultaneously - but the market reacted first and more strongly to the plain fact that revenue guidance had been trimmed.

This isn't a new pattern for Levi. Back in July, the company's fiscal second-quarter results also beat on both EPS ($0.28 versus a $0.24 estimate) and revenue ($1.56 billion versus $1.52 billion expected) - and shares still fell more than 5% in after-hours trading, dragged down by margin concerns and uncertainty about second-half profitability. Two consecutive quarters of "the numbers won in the quarter, but the stock lost anyway" is not noise. It's a signal that investors are carrying a deeper, structural skepticism about where Levi's growth is actually coming from, independent of any single quarter's print.

## What to Take Away From This

- **Always check what an EPS beat is actually made of.** A third of Levi's 33% EPS surprise traced back to a non-recurring tariff refund. Looking past the headline number to the "ex-one-time-item" growth rate reveals the real underlying trend.
- **When guidance moves in opposite directions, the stock tends to follow the weaker signal.** Raising EPS guidance while cutting revenue guidance reads to the market as "profit is being managed, but growth is slowing" - and that's usually the takeaway that sticks.
- **Weakness in a company's stated strategic priority gets weighted more heavily.** Because DTC expansion is central to Levi's growth thesis, a DTC-driven U.S. revenue decline draws more investor scrutiny than comparable softness elsewhere in the business.
- **A repeated pattern is a signal, not a coincidence.** Two straight quarters of beating estimates while the stock still falls points to eroded market confidence in the growth story itself, not a one-off misunderstanding.

## FAQ

### Levi Strauss beat EPS estimates by 33% - why didn't the stock go up?
Of the $0.48 adjusted EPS, $0.11 came from a roughly $80 million one-time tariff refund. Strip that out, and EPS grew about 9% year-over-year rather than the 41% implied by the headline number. Combined with a revenue miss and a lowered full-year revenue growth forecast, the market weighed earnings quality and slowing momentum more heavily than the headline beat.

### Why did Levi Strauss get a tariff refund, and will it happen again?
The refund relates to tariffs that had been assessed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) and were later found refundable. Levi said the roughly $80 million received this quarter represents nearly all of what it expects to receive for the full year, meaning investors shouldn't model a similar windfall recurring at the same scale next year.

### Why did Levi cut revenue guidance while raising its stock buyback and dividend?
Management said it chose to redeploy most of the tariff refund into the business - marketing and operations - during the third and fourth quarters. At the same time, it announced a new $100 million accelerated buyback and a 14% dividend increase, using the refund windfall to fund both growth investment and shareholder returns simultaneously. Investors still treated the combination cautiously because the underlying revenue growth outlook was lowered.

### How serious is the weakness in Levi's direct-to-consumer business?
U.S. DTC sales came in softer than expected this quarter, and overall U.S. revenue fell 1% year-over-year. Management expects DTC to recover to at least mid-single-digit growth in the fourth quarter, but that recovery hasn't happened yet. Since DTC expansion is central to Levi's long-term growth strategy, its trajectory over the next few quarters is likely to remain a key factor in how the stock trades.
