## Same Kind of "Maybe-Debt," But Only One Shows Up as a Number

Imagine a company gets sued by a customer for damages. The case could go either way — nothing has been decided yet. How much does this lawsuit add to the company's balance sheet? The honest answer is: it depends. Some companies will estimate the odds of losing and the likely payout, then write a specific dollar figure into their liabilities. Others won't put a single dollar into the balance sheet itself — instead, buried in the footnotes, you'll find one sentence: "the company is involved in ongoing litigation, the outcome of which cannot currently be predicted." Both situations describe the exact same thing — a potential future cash outflow tied to a past event — so why are they treated so differently? The line that separates them is the distinction between a **provision** and a **contingent liability**. This lesson covers how that line is drawn, why contingent liabilities live only in the footnotes rather than the balance sheet itself, and what risk investors miss entirely if they only skim the balance sheet's bottom-line numbers.

## The Test: "How Likely" and "How Precisely Can You Count It"

Accounting standards (IAS 37 internationally, and Korea's equivalent K-IFRS 1037) sort potential obligations arising from past events into probability bands, and treat each band completely differently. The whole framework boils down to two questions. First, is it **probable** that resources will flow out, or is it unlikely or remote? Second, can the amount be **estimated reliably**? Only when the answer to both questions is yes does the obligation get recognized as a provision — an actual dollar figure booked on the balance sheet. If the outflow is possible but not probable, or if it's probable but the amount simply can't be estimated reliably, nothing goes on the balance sheet; it's disclosed only as a contingent liability in the footnotes. If the likelihood is remote, there's typically no disclosure obligation at all.

| Category | Likelihood | Estimable? | Treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Provision | Probable | Yes | Recognized as a liability on the balance sheet |
| Contingent liability | Possible, or probable but not reliably estimable | No, or not applicable | Not booked — disclosed as text in the footnotes only |
| No disclosure needed | Remote | — | No disclosure required |

Product warranty and return obligations are a classic example of a provision: past sales data usually lets a company estimate defect rates and average repair costs with reasonable precision. An ongoing lawsuit is different — it's often hard to even gauge the odds of losing, and even when a loss looks close to certain, pinning down what a court will actually award can be nearly impossible. That "can it be reliably estimated" test is exactly why lawsuits, loan guarantees, and environmental remediation obligations — anything whose outcome hinges on an external party (a court, a borrower's solvency, a regulator) — disproportionately end up stuck as contingent liabilities rather than hard numbers.

US GAAP (ASC 450) runs on nearly the same skeleton but splits the probability band into three explicit tiers: "probable," "reasonably possible," and "remote." Probable-and-estimable gets booked as a liability; reasonably possible must be disclosed in the footnotes regardless of whether the amount is estimable; remote generally requires no disclosure at all. The wording differs slightly from IFRS, but the underlying logic is identical: the more certain something is, the more it belongs in the numbers; the less certain, the more it belongs in words. When reading a US company's 10-K, this same structure shows up under the heading "Commitments and Contingencies."

## A Worked Example: Same Lawsuit, Different Accounting

Here's why two seemingly similar lawsuits can be accounted for in completely different ways. Company A and Company B are both hit with a class-action suit seeking $100 million in damages.

Company A has faced several similar suits before and has consistently settled for roughly 30% of the amount claimed; its legal team can reliably estimate that a similar pattern will likely repeat. Company A books roughly $30 million as a provision, directly on its balance sheet. Company B, by contrast, is facing a novel type of claim with no precedent to draw on — the payout if it loses could plausibly be anywhere from $10 million to $100 million. Company B puts nothing on its balance sheet and instead adds a footnote stating the litigation is ongoing and its financial impact cannot currently be reasonably estimated.

Comparing the two balance sheets alone, Company A looks like the riskier one — it's carrying $30 million more in liabilities. In practice, the opposite read is often closer to the truth. Company A has already sized its risk and absorbed it into a number; Company B is sitting on genuinely larger, unresolved uncertainty that simply doesn't show up anywhere in its reported liabilities. That gap is exactly why the balance sheet alone can't tell you the full risk picture — you have to read the footnotes too.

## When a Contingent Liability Turns Real

Contingent liabilities sit quietly as text until a specific trigger converts them into actual cash outflows. Three triggers are the most common. The first is a final court judgment: a company can lose at trial and still carry the claim as a contingent liability while an appeal is pending, but once the final ruling lands, the award becomes a hard liability. The second is a guarantee being called. When a parent company guarantees a subsidiary's or a supplier's loan, that guarantee normally sits in the footnotes as a "commitment" — until the actual borrower fails to repay, at which point the guarantor is on the hook for the shortfall. The completion guarantees that construction contractors extend in [real estate project financing](/en/basics/real-estate-project-financing-pf-explained/) deals follow exactly this pattern: invisible contingent liabilities that turn into real debt the moment a project's pre-sales fail. The third trigger is a regulatory order — product recalls or environmental cleanup mandates — where an estimate becomes a firm liability the moment the order is issued. What all three triggers share is that the outcome, until it crystallizes, rests on a decision made by someone outside the company's control: a court, a borrower, or a regulator.

## A Checklist for Reading the Footnotes

Rather than stopping at the balance sheet's bottom line, it's worth running through a short checklist whenever you open the "contingencies and commitments" footnote in an annual report. First, check whether the total number and dollar amount of pending lawsuits have grown year over year — a steady increase can signal a structural legal-risk problem building up in the business. Second, check who the company has guaranteed and how large those guarantees are relative to shareholders' equity; if a guarantee's counterparty's credit quality deteriorates, that burden can land squarely on the guarantor. Third, check whether a contingent liability or lawsuit is flagged as a **Key Audit Matter (KAM)** or "critical audit matter" — if an external auditor singles something out this way, it's a signal the amount or the uncertainty around it is material. Finally, compare this year's language and assumptions against last year's footnote on the same issue; a sudden shift in tone or estimation approach often hints that something has changed internally even before it shows up anywhere else.

## The Catch: Judgment Creeps Into Both Tests

This whole framework has a built-in limitation: both "is it probable" and "can it be estimated reliably" ultimately rest on management's own judgment. A company facing a high probability of losing a case has every incentive to argue the outcome "isn't yet certain enough" or that the amount "can't be reliably estimated," since booking a provision immediately reduces reported net income, while leaving it as a footnote-only contingent liability leaves the numbers untouched. That's exactly why auditors and regulators keep loss contingencies, financial guarantees, and litigation on their recurring list of scrutinized accounting issues, and why investors who pay close attention to [earnings quality](/en/basics/earnings-quality-accruals/) tend to grow wary of companies whose contingency footnotes read as unusually vague or defensive year after year. Contingent-liability disclosure is a genuinely useful window into risk a balance sheet can't show — but how clearly you can see through that window still depends on how forthcoming the company choosing the wording actually is.

## Key Takeaways

- A provision is booked as a real liability on the balance sheet when a loss is probable and can be reliably estimated; a contingent liability is disclosed only in the footnotes when the loss is less than probable, or when the amount simply can't be reliably estimated.
- Lawsuits, loan guarantees, and environmental remediation obligations disproportionately end up as contingent liabilities because their outcomes hinge on a court, a borrower, or a regulator — all outside the company's control.
- A contingent liability has zero impact on reported numbers until a trigger — a final judgment, a called guarantee, or a regulatory order — converts it into an actual liability.
- Investors should pair the balance sheet with the footnotes: rising litigation counts and claim amounts, the size of guarantees relative to equity, and whether auditors flag a contingency as a Key Audit Matter all reveal risk the balance-sheet numbers alone don't show.
- Because both the probability and estimability judgments are made by management, contingent-liability disclosure is a useful signal to watch, not an absolute guarantee that all material risk has been surfaced.

## FAQ

### Does having a contingent liability in the footnotes mean a company is risky?
Not by itself. Most public companies carry routine lawsuits or standard loan guarantees in their footnotes at all times. What matters is the scale of the claims relative to equity and whether that scale is trending up year over year — context that tells you far more than the mere presence of a disclosure.

### How would an investor notice when a contingent liability gets reclassified as a real liability?
As a lawsuit progresses — a trial-court ruling comes down, or a settlement looks imminent — the company updates its quarterly or interim footnotes, reclassifies the item from contingent liability to provision, and books the estimated amount. Comparing one quarter's contingency footnote against the prior quarter's is the most direct way to catch this shift.

### Are there contingent assets too?
Yes. A potential future benefit — like a chance of winning a lawsuit and collecting damages — is called a contingent asset. Accounting's conservatism principle treats these far more restrictively than contingent liabilities, though: a contingent asset isn't recognized in the financial statements at all until its realization is virtually certain, and is only briefly mentioned in the footnotes while it remains merely possible or probable.
