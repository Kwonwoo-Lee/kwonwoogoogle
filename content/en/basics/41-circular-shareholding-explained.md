## Can 1% Ownership Control an Entire Company?

Dig into the ownership disclosures of some large conglomerates and you'll find an odd picture: a founding family owns just a few percent of Company A directly, and yet the family effectively controls the entire group. The mechanism is a loop — A holds a stake in B, B holds a stake in C, and C holds a stake back in A, closing the circle. This kind of arrangement is called **circular shareholding**, and it lets a controlling family hold voting power over an entire group that vastly exceeds the actual capital they put in. It has sat at the center of Korean corporate governance debates for decades, and understanding it is still useful today for reading the ownership structure behind any stock you're considering. This lesson covers what circular shareholding actually is, why it manufactures outsized control from a small stake, and why regulators eventually moved to restrict it.

## What Circular Shareholding Is — A Chain of Ownership That Loops Back

Circular shareholding describes a structure where three or more affiliated companies within a single group own stakes in each other in a closed loop: A owns a stake in B, B owns a stake in C, and C owns a stake back in A. The simplest version — just two companies holding stakes in each other (A↔B) — is usually called **cross-shareholding**; circular shareholding is the same idea extended to three or more companies in a chain. On the surface this looks like nothing more than affiliated companies holding each other's stock. The part that matters is what happens to capital each time it travels once around the loop.

## Why a Small Stake Buys Outsized Control

The clearest way to see the mechanism is to walk through the numbers.

A controlling family puts up ₩10 billion to found Company A. A then invests ₩5 billion of that capital into Company B, becoming B's controlling shareholder. B in turn invests ₩3 billion of that into Company C, becoming C's controlling shareholder. And C invests ₩1 billion of that back into A, closing the loop.

| Step | Investment | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Family → Company A (initial capital) | ₩10.0B |
| 2 | A → B | ₩5.0B |
| 3 | B → C | ₩3.0B |
| 4 | C → A (back to the start) | ₩1.0B |

The ₩1 billion in step 4 is money that originated from the same ₩10 billion the family put into A in the first place — it just traveled through B and C first. But the moment it lands back at A, A's book capital is recorded as ₩11 billion, not ₩10 billion. The family never contributed a single additional won, yet as the same capital circles through three affiliates, the group's combined book capital appears to grow. And along the way, the family ends up with meaningful voting control over all three companies while holding only a small direct stake in each one. What the family actually put in — its **cash flow rights** — stayed at ₩10 billion. What it can control — its **control rights**, through voting power — extends across the whole loop. The gap between these two numbers is called the **separation of ownership and control** (sometimes described as the "control-ownership wedge"), and manufacturing a large gap is the entire point of a circular shareholding structure.

## Why the Ownership-Control Gap Is a Problem

Circular shareholding isn't automatically illegal or automatically harmful. The problem lies in the incentives it creates.

**First, the inflated book capital isn't real.** In the example above, ₩1 billion of the ₩11 billion in A's recorded capital isn't new money from outside the group — it's the same capital counted a second time as it circled back. When several such loops overlap across a large group, reading the group's total reported capital tells you much less about its actual financial strength than it appears to.

**Second, trouble at one affiliate can spread through the whole chain.** If A holds a stake in B and B holds a stake in C, a major loss at C damages the value of B's stake in C, which in turn damages A's stake in B. Because the affiliates' equity stakes are chained together, distress at any single company can propagate through the entire group — a risk regulators and researchers have repeatedly flagged as a defining weakness of cross- and circular-shareholding structures.

**Third, the controlling family's incentives can diverge sharply from minority shareholders'.** The family's actual cash stake in A is only ₩10 billion worth, but its voting control spans A, B, and C. In that position, the family loses nothing in terms of overall group control by shifting profit out of an affiliate where it holds a small direct stake (say, C) toward one where it holds a larger stake. But for C's minority shareholders, that's functionally the same as watching profit they invested in flow to a different company. This dynamic is a big part of why disputes over related-party transactions and preferential deal-steering toward affiliates recur so often at conglomerates built on circular or cross-shareholding structures.

**Fourth, the structure itself gets priced into valuation.** The wider the ownership-control gap and the more opaque the web of affiliate holdings, the more likely the market is to apply a governance discount to the stock. As covered in [the holding company discount](/en/basics/holding-company-discount/) lesson, companies seen as having complex governance and weaker minority-shareholder protections tend to trade at lower multiples than peers earning the same profit. It's a pattern you can often see play out directly: stock prices at affiliates frequently move when news breaks that a group is unwinding a circular-shareholding loop or converting to a holding-company structure, because the market is repricing the improvement in governance transparency.

## How This Differs From a Holding Company Structure

It's easy to confuse circular shareholding with a [holding company structure](/en/basics/holding-company-discount/), but they're fundamentally different shapes. In a holding-company structure, one entity at the top owns stakes straight down through each affiliate — draw it out and you get a tree. Who controls what, and how much, is visible at a glance, and because no affiliate loops back to own a stake in another, there's much less of a channel for trouble at one company to spread sideways. Circular shareholding, by contrast, draws as a closed loop — governance is far less transparent, and risk has a direct path to travel sideways through the chain. This is a large part of why Korean policy has, for years, pushed conglomerates built on circular and cross-shareholding to restructure into holding-company groups.

## How Korea Regulates Circular Shareholding

Korea's Fair Trade Act introduced a **ban on new circular shareholding** starting in 2014. Affiliates belonging to a conglomerate above a certain combined asset threshold — the "cross-shareholding restricted business groups" the Fair Trade Commission designates each year — are barred from creating new circular-ownership loops, and affiliates already inside an existing loop are barred from adding further investment into that loop. The policy goals cited for the ban include preventing distress at one affiliate from dragging down healthy ones, curbing the ability to hold outsized control with a disproportionately small stake, and closing off a route sometimes used to pass control to the next generation without a proportional ownership transfer. It's worth being precise about the scope, though: the ban targets *new* loops. It doesn't require conglomerates to unwind circular-shareholding relationships that predated the law. In the years since, a number of large groups have voluntarily dissolved their remaining loops or converted to holding-company structures, but not every legacy circular-shareholding relationship in Korea has disappeared.

## Not a Uniquely Korean Phenomenon

Using a web of stakes to control far more than the capital you put in isn't specific to Korea. Academic research treats this under the broader umbrella of **pyramidal ownership structures**, which covers both top-down chains (like holding companies, where control rights get stacked and amplified layer by layer) and side-to-side arrangements like cross- and circular-shareholding. Cross-shareholding among Japan's keiretsu affiliate groups and the multi-layered ownership webs found in some European family-controlled conglomerates are commonly cited examples. Research on these structures consistently finds the same pattern: separating cash flow rights from control rights widens the potential for agency costs — the risk that a controlling shareholder or management team acts in ways that diverge from what minority shareholders would prefer. Circular shareholding, in other words, is better understood as one specific mechanism among several for separating ownership from control, not as some peculiarity found only at Korean conglomerates.

## How to Actually Use This as an Investor

Finding a circular- or cross-shareholding relationship isn't, by itself, a reason to avoid a stock. But knowing the concept changes how you read an ownership structure. Korea's Fair Trade Commission publishes an annual survey of large business groups' ownership structures, including circular- and cross-shareholding relationships, through its corporate group disclosure portal — a useful primary source if you're considering an affiliate of a specific conglomerate. Two things are worth checking directly. First, how large is the gap between the controlling family's actual ownership stake (cash flow rights) and its effective voting control? Second, does the company sit inside a shareholding loop that exposes it to distress spreading from another affiliate? A company's annual report — specifically sections detailing affiliate holdings and the largest shareholder's ownership — usually gives a rough picture of who owns what across the group, and it's worth specifically looking for a point where the ownership chain loops back on itself rather than running in one direction only. The wider that ownership-control gap, the stronger the structural incentive for controlling shareholders to make decisions that don't align with minority shareholders' interests — which is exactly why it's worth watching alongside related-party transactions and dividend policy, not treating as a standalone red flag.

## Takeaways

- Circular shareholding is a structure where three or more affiliated companies invest in each other in a closed loop (A→B→C→A), inflating recorded group capital each time the same money circles back.
- A controlling family ends up with voting control (control rights) far exceeding what it actually invested (cash flow rights) — a gap known as the ownership-control disparity.
- The main risks are inflated paper capital with little real substance, contagion of distress across affiliates in the loop, and a structural incentive for controlling shareholders' decisions to diverge from minority shareholders' interests.
- Korea has banned the creation of new circular-shareholding loops since 2014, but the law doesn't force conglomerates to dissolve loops that already existed.
- Using stake structures to control more than the capital invested isn't unique to Korea — it's studied globally under the broader concept of pyramidal ownership.

## FAQ

### Is a holding-company structure safer than circular shareholding?
Generally, yes — a holding-company structure lays out who controls what in a clear top-down chain and offers fewer channels for risk to spread between affiliates, so it's usually seen as more transparent than circular shareholding. That said, holding companies come with their own issues, like the [holding company discount](/en/basics/holding-company-discount/), so neither structure should be treated as automatically "safe."

### Do any large Korean conglomerates still have circular shareholding today?
The Fair Trade Commission's annual survey of large business groups' ownership structures discloses which groups still hold circular- or cross-shareholding relationships. Many groups have voluntarily unwound their loops or converted to holding structures since the 2014 ban on new loops, but the picture changes over time, so check the latest disclosure if you're evaluating a specific affiliate.

### Where can I check the ownership-control gap for a specific company?
Korea's Fair Trade Commission publishes annual data comparing controlling families' direct ownership stakes to their effective voting control across large business groups, available through its corporate group disclosure portal. Some brokerage research reports also cover this metric when analyzing a specific conglomerate's governance.
