## You're Invested in Something You Never Chose

A large share of people with a defined-contribution (DC) retirement account or an individual retirement pension account have never once specified what their money is actually invested in. Korea's default-option system (사전지정운용제도) took full effect in July 2023: if a participant leaves their contributions without giving investment instructions, the money is automatically routed into a pre-designated product chosen ahead of time by the pension provider. The single most common product on that default-option menu is the target date fund, or TDF. A TDF isn't just "a fund that invests for you" — it's a fund built around a specific design: it automatically cuts the share of risk assets, mainly stocks, as the participant's expected retirement year gets closer. This lesson covers exactly how that design — called a glide path — works, why it's so often chosen as the default, and where this automated design falls short.

## What a TDF Is: The Number in the Name Is the Strategy

A TDF is named after a specific target date, almost always the investor's expected retirement year. "TDF2050" targets someone expecting to retire around 2050. That number isn't a return target — it says nothing about "earning X% by 2050." It's purely a timing anchor that determines how long the fund stays aggressively invested and when it starts shifting toward a more defensive stance. Structurally, a TDF is usually not a single fund but a fund-of-funds that allocates across several underlying sleeves — domestic equity, overseas equity, domestic bonds, overseas bonds, and so on. The investor never has to decide the stock-bond split themselves; they simply ride the single allocation path the fund manager has already designed.

## How the Glide Path Works: Why Equity Exposure Falls With Age

The core of a TDF is its glide path — a pre-set route for how the asset mix changes over time. When the target date is far off, the fund holds a high equity weighting; as the target date approaches, it gradually shifts that weighting toward bonds and cash-like assets. The logic rests on a simple premise: a longer investment horizon gives you more time to recover from a downturn along the way. Someone 30 years from retirement can ride out a 50% market crash and still have decades to recover, but someone one or two years from retirement who takes the same hit has to start drawing down that money with no time left to recover. As covered in [Sequence of Returns Risk](/en/basics/sequence-of-returns-risk-safe-withdrawal-rate/), the same average return can produce wildly different retirement outcomes depending on whether a sharp drop lands right before or right after withdrawals begin — and a TDF's whole design exists to dial down that specific risk automatically, without the investor having to think about it.

To set the actual split, people often cite an old rule of thumb: hold a stock percentage equal to "100 minus your age." As life expectancy has stretched out and low-rate environments have persisted, though, that rule has increasingly been criticized as too conservative, and more aggressive variants — "110 minus age" or "120 minus age" — get cited more often now. These are nothing more than intuition-building heuristics; not every provider follows the same formula. As one concrete (illustrative, not universal) example, a major asset manager's TDF series holds roughly 90% in equities up to around age 40, glides that down to roughly 50% by the target retirement age of around 65, and then continues lowering it to roughly 30% over the decades that follow retirement. That's a single provider's example — funds sharing the same "TDF" label can have meaningfully different glide-path slopes and different ending equity levels depending on who manages them.

## "To" vs. "Through" Glide Paths

Here's a design distinction investors commonly miss. Some TDFs follow a "to" glide path that stops adjusting the moment the target date arrives and locks in the allocation from then on. Others follow a "through" glide path, which assumes the retiree will keep holding the fund and drawing it down gradually over decades, so the allocation keeps getting more conservative for years or even decades past the target date itself. Two funds can share the exact same "TDF2050" label and still carry a different actual risk level right after retirement, depending on which design they use — and marketing materials or the fund name alone rarely tell you which one you've got. The only reliable way to know whether your own TDF stops adjusting at the target date or keeps shifting afterward is to check the glide-path chart in the fund's actual prospectus.

## Korea's Default Option and TDFs

In Korea, legislation passed in late 2021 led to a one-year pilot run starting in July 2022, and the default-option system (사전지정운용제도) took full effect on July 12, 2023. If a participant in a DC-type pension or individual retirement pension account doesn't specify how their contributions should be invested within a set window, the funds are automatically routed into whichever default-option product was pre-selected before enrollment. That default-option menu typically includes principal-guaranteed products and balanced (mixed-allocation) funds alongside TDFs — and among those choices, TDFs are often singled out as the best fit for the system's actual intent, since they automatically adjust risk to match each participant's life stage. Since the system launched, both the total balance flowing into default options and the share held in TDFs specifically are widely reported to be growing steadily year over year — though a large portion of default-option money reportedly still sits in more conservative principal-guaranteed products rather than TDFs. In other words, there's still a real gap between what the system was designed to encourage and what participants are actually choosing.

## The Limitation: Same Target Year Doesn't Mean Same Risk

The most frequently raised criticism of TDFs is that the glide path keys off a single variable: the target year. Two people both planning to retire around 2050 can be in very different positions — one might have a robust public pension and real estate holdings that let them absorb losses in their retirement account comfortably, while the other might be relying on that account for nearly all of their retirement income. A TDF doesn't account for either person's individual risk tolerance or their other assets; it sets the entire allocation path off one number, "how many years until retirement." There's also a structural fee issue: when a TDF is built as a fund-of-funds, its own management fee can stack on top of the fees already charged by the underlying sleeve funds — a problem known as fee layering. That said, this has genuinely improved in recent years; as more TDFs lean on low-cost passive underlying funds, industry-wide asset-weighted average fees have come down noticeably from where they were roughly a decade and a half ago. The practical takeaway is to never pick a TDF by target year alone — compare the actual glide-path slope, the ending equity level, and the full fee structure across providers directly in the prospectus. Two funds both called "TDF2050" can differ meaningfully in underlying holdings, fees, and glide-path design, so a matching target year is never enough on its own to assume they carry the same risk.

## How a TDF Differs From a Balanced Fund

Default-option menus often list balanced funds (BF) alongside TDFs, and the two can look similar on the surface despite working very differently. A balanced fund tends to hold its stock-bond split roughly constant for the life of the investment — say, a fixed "40% stocks, 60% bonds" mix that doesn't shift much even when markets move. A TDF, by contrast, keeps changing that split over time according to its glide path, as described above. Put simply: a balanced fund maintains a constant risk level, while a TDF deliberately lowers its risk level as the time horizon shortens — two genuinely different design philosophies. An investor with decades left until retirement who's sitting in a balanced fund locked into a conservative split from day one may be giving up return potential unnecessarily for a long stretch. Conversely, someone close to retirement stuck in a TDF or balanced fund whose allocation hasn't adjusted enough is exposed to the full brunt of sequence-of-returns risk.

## A Worked Example

The table below is a simplified, illustrative glide path based on one asset manager's TDF series. Actual figures vary by provider and fund, so treat this purely as an example.

| Years to target date | Example age | Example equity weight | Example bond/cash weight |
|---|---|---|---|
| 30+ years | early 30s | ~90% | ~10% |
| 15–20 years | late 40s | ~75% | ~25% |
| 5–10 years | late 50s | ~60% | ~40% |

As the table shows, the same fund's risk-asset weighting keeps declining over time, and that adjustment happens automatically inside the fund — the investor never has to issue a new instruction for it to occur.

## Key Takeaways

- A TDF automatically reduces its equity weighting over time as its named target retirement year approaches, following a pre-set glide path.
- "100 (or 110–120) minus age" is a widely cited rule of thumb for gauging equity exposure, not a formula every provider actually follows — real glide paths vary by manager.
- A "to" design stops adjusting at the target date while a "through" design keeps shifting for years afterward — two funds with the same target-year name can carry different actual risk right after retirement.
- TDFs are a common default-option pick in Korea's system because they auto-adjust risk to life stage, but they don't account for individual risk tolerance, and fee structure and glide-path design still need to be compared directly before enrolling.

## FAQ

### Is a TDF the same thing as a default option?
No. The default-option system is the mechanism itself — contributions get auto-invested when a participant gives no instruction. A TDF is just one of several products that can sit on that default-option menu, typically alongside principal-guaranteed products and balanced funds.

### Is TDF2050 safer than TDF2030, or the other way around?
Generally, a farther-out target year (TDF2050) means a higher current equity weighting, while a nearer target year (TDF2030) means a lower, more defensive weighting right now. But that's only a general pattern within a single provider's own series — different providers can run meaningfully different glide-path slopes and ending allocations even for the identical target year, so a direct comparison is still necessary.

### If I got auto-enrolled into a TDF through the default option and don't like it, can I switch?
Yes, in most cases a participant can still change their investment instruction to a different product after being auto-enrolled through the default option. The default option is only what applies when no instruction is given — it isn't a permanent, irreversible choice.

### Is choosing a principal-guaranteed product over a TDF the safer option?
In the short run, a principal-guaranteed product feels safer because its value barely moves. But over the long run, it often barely keeps pace with inflation, or falls short of it, which matters a lot if retirement is still decades away. An overly conservative choice made too early can end up hurting retirement savings more than it protects them, so the decision should weigh your actual time horizon and risk tolerance together rather than picking whatever feels "safe" on its face.
