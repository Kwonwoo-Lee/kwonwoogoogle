## What Happened

Wall Street had its roughest session of the year on Wednesday, November 25, just ahead of the Thanksgiving holiday. The Dow Jones Industrial Average tumbled more than 1,000 points intraday - roughly 2% - while the S&P 500 fell about 3% and the tech-heavy Nasdaq Composite slid as much as 4%. The mood could not have been more different from a day earlier, when the Dow had just reclaimed the 50,000 level after a brief dip. The whiplash came from two pieces of bad news landing on investors' desks within hours of each other.

The first was an inflation reading. The Federal Reserve's preferred inflation gauge, the October personal consumption expenditures (PCE) price index, showed headline PCE up 3.8% year over year and core PCE (excluding food and energy) at 3.3%. That topped the roughly 3.2% economists had expected and marked a step back up from the 3.1%-ish pace seen in September. The number landed just days after minutes from the Fed's October meeting revealed that St. Louis Fed President Alberto Musalem had warned up to four more rate hikes could be needed by spring - a warning that suddenly looked a lot more credible. Futures markets responded by pricing in nearly a 98% probability of a rate hike at the December Fed meeting.

The second piece of news came from an unexpected direction. The White House announced new 20% tariffs - stacked on top of existing rates - targeting 14 major trading partners, including China and the European Union. Crucially, the tariffs aren't taking effect immediately: they first require a public comment period and won't be implemented until January at the earliest, meaning there's no immediate hit to corporate earnings. Markets still didn't shrug it off. Tariffs tend to feed through to consumer prices by raising the cost of imported goods, so announcing a fresh round on the same morning inflation was already running hotter than expected read, to traders, as one combined signal that price pressures aren't going away anytime soon - further complicating an already hawkish-leaning Fed.

The combined shock hit semiconductor and AI-related stocks hardest. Nvidia (NASDAQ: NVDA) dropped roughly 8% intraday, and the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index (SOX) fell about 6%. The swing is especially jarring given that Nvidia had surged as much as 33% in a single session just two days earlier, on November 23, after its board reinstated Jensen Huang as CEO. The Nasdaq 100 gave back a significant chunk of its recent record closing highs on the day, underscoring just how fragile the foundation of the chip-driven AI rally has looked over the past week.

## Why Inflation and Tariffs Together Hit Markets So Much Harder Than Either Alone

Taken individually, neither piece of news looks dramatic enough to explain a 1,000-point Dow decline. Core PCE rose just 0.1 percentage point, from roughly 3.2% to 3.3%, and the new tariffs haven't even taken effect yet - they're still at the "announced" stage. But three mechanisms combined to make the market's reaction far larger than the sum of its parts.

First, there was a confirmation effect on an already-hawkish Fed narrative. Markets already knew about Musalem's warning that up to four more hikes could be needed. What they were waiting for was a data point that would either validate or undercut that warning. A core PCE print running slightly hot did exactly the former. Once a single data point completes a narrative - "this isn't a blip, it's a trend" - markets tend to react more to the conviction that narrative provides than to the raw size of the number itself. The jump in December rate-hike odds to nearly 98% is a direct reflection of that narrative becoming conventional wisdom overnight.

Second, tariffs get priced in at the announcement, not the implementation date. The 20% tariffs won't actually show up in consumer prices until sometime after January, but financial markets price assets based on discounted future cash flows - meaning expectations about future costs get baked into prices today, long before those costs materialize. If a tariff looks likely to actually go into effect, the expected hit to corporate margins and consumer prices gets priced in immediately. The fact that this announcement landed on the very same morning as the hot inflation data compounded the effect: instead of being read as two separate stories, markets folded them into a single narrative that inflation is likely to keep running hot for longer.

Third, semiconductor and AI stocks were hit disproportionately hard because of how their valuations work. Nvidia and other AI megacaps often trade on the basis of years of future growth priced in today. Rising rate expectations increase the discount rate used to value those distant future cash flows, and a higher discount rate mathematically shrinks the present value of earnings further out on the horizon. That's why growth and technology stocks consistently react more violently than value stocks whenever rate-hike expectations rise - and it's exactly the dynamic behind Nvidia's roughly 8% drop and the semiconductor index's 6% slide on November 25.

## What to Take Away From This

- **A data point that beats expectations by a small margin can still trigger an outsized market reaction.** Core PCE rising just 0.1 percentage point, from about 3.2% to 3.3%, is a tiny change in absolute terms - but because it confirmed an already-hawkish market narrative, it helped trigger a 1,000-point Dow decline. What matters most for market reactions is often not the absolute size of a data surprise, but whether it confirms or contradicts the story investors were already telling themselves.
- **Policy announcements get priced in before they take effect.** The new 20% tariffs won't be implemented until January at the earliest, yet markets reacted immediately on the announcement itself. Always distinguish between when a policy is announced, when it's priced in, and when it actually takes effect - these are three different moments that can be separated by weeks or months.
- **High-valuation growth stocks carry outsized rate sensitivity.** Nvidia and other AI megacaps fell harder than the broader market not by coincidence, but because stocks priced on distant future growth take a mathematically larger hit when discount rates rise. If your portfolio is concentrated in high-valuation growth names, expect larger swings whenever rate expectations shift.
- **When multiple pieces of bad news land on the same day, the market reaction can exceed the sum of the individual shocks.** Either the inflation data or the tariff announcement alone likely wouldn't have triggered a move this large. It was the combination - read by markets as one unified story about persistent inflation - that amplified the sell-off. On days when several headlines break at once, it's worth asking what single narrative the market is folding them into, rather than evaluating each headline in isolation.

The size of the increase itself (from about 3.2% to 3.3%) was small, but the timing mattered enormously: it landed just days after St. Louis Fed President Alberto Musalem warned that up to four more rate hikes could be needed by spring. The data was read as confirmation of that hawkish warning, pushing the probability of a December rate hike to nearly 98% and hitting high-valuation growth and tech stocks especially hard through worsening discount-rate expectations.

They require a public comment period first and won't be implemented until January at the earliest. Financial markets, however, tend to price in the expected impact of a policy as soon as it's announced rather than waiting for its implementation date, which is why stocks reacted the same day.

### Why did Nvidia fall more than the broader market?

When rate-hike expectations rise, the discount rate used to value those future earnings rises too, which mathematically reduces the present value of growth-heavy stocks far more than it does for value stocks. That's why Nvidia fell roughly 8% and the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index dropped about 6% on November 25, both steeper than the broader market's decline.
