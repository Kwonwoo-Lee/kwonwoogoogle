## A Bond With No Maturity Date That Trades Like It Has One

In early November 2022, a single bond issued by a mid-sized Korean life insurer rattled the entire domestic credit market. The amount involved — $500 million — was modest next to the size of Korea's bond market as a whole. Yet the announcement alone triggered a cascade of canceled overseas bond offerings from other Korean financial institutions and a sharp selloff in related securities. The trigger was Heungkuk Life's decision not to exercise the "call option" (early redemption right) on this particular bond. What makes this strange is that skipping the call was perfectly legal. It wasn't a default. It didn't breach any contract term. So why did markets react as if something had broken? The answer lies in what kind of security this was — a **perpetual bond**, sometimes called a hybrid capital security — and in an unwritten market convention investors had quietly assumed would always hold. This lesson covers how a bond with no maturity date gets treated as equity in the first place, and why breaking that convention can shake an entire market.

## What a Perpetual Bond Actually Is — Three Conditions That Turn Debt Into Capital

A perpetual bond is exactly what the name suggests: a bond issued with no fixed maturity date, or with a maturity so long (30, 50, even 60 years) that it functions the same way in practice. But having no maturity alone isn't enough to earn equity treatment under accounting and credit-rating standards. Three features typically need to be present together. First, the maturity has to be absent or extremely long, weakening the core debt characteristic of "money that must be repaid." Second, coupon payments have to be deferrable at the issuer's discretion — unlike an ordinary corporate bond, where missing even one interest payment triggers default, a perpetual bond can skip a coupon without being in default at all. Third, in a bankruptcy, the security has to rank behind ordinary creditors, sitting just ahead of common equity in the payout order. Put those three together — no maturity, discretionary coupons, deep subordination — and rating agencies and accounting standards will treat the instrument as something much closer to equity than debt. In the way [RCPS (redeemable convertible preferred stock)](/en/basics/rcps-redeemable-convertible-preferred-stock/) lets startups design a security that sits between debt and equity, perpetual bonds let listed companies and financial institutions do something similar at a much larger scale. The appeal to the issuer is obvious: raise capital that improves balance-sheet leverage ratios and, for an insurer, solvency ratios — without diluting existing shareholders the way a rights offering would. That's why issuers ranging from utilities and heavy manufacturers to insurance companies have used perpetual bonds for years.

## How Much "Equity Credit" Do Rating Agencies Actually Give It?

Just because a perpetual bond sits on the balance sheet as capital doesn't mean credit rating agencies treat it as 100% equity. Moody's and S&P assess hybrid securities against those same three features — maturity, coupon discretion, subordination — and assign what they call "equity credit." Even a well-structured perpetual bond that checks every box typically earns only partial equity credit, often around half, with the rest still counted as debt for leverage calculations. In other words, even when an issuer markets the security as "pure capital," rating agencies view it as something closer to half capital, half debt. The call option matters here too: if the coupon step-up after the call date is steep enough, agencies may read it as a de facto mechanism forcing redemption at that date, and discount the equity credit accordingly. How "real" the equity treatment actually is isn't settled by one line in the prospectus — it emerges from how maturity, coupon terms, subordination, and the call structure interact.

## Why Investors Treat the Call Date as the Real Maturity

Here's the catch: almost no investor buying a perpetual bond actually expects to hold it forever. Most are issued with a call option — the issuer's right, not obligation, to redeem the bond at par after five or ten years. And most also carry a **coupon step-up** clause: if the issuer skips the call, the interest rate jumps sharply from that date forward. From the issuer's perspective, once that step-up kicks in, it's usually cheaper to simply issue a new perpetual bond and use the proceeds to redeem the old one than to keep paying the elevated rate. Repeated across the market enough times, this pattern hardened into an unwritten convention: perpetual bonds are expected to be redeemed at the call date even though nothing in the contract requires it. Investors price accordingly — a bond with a legal maturity of 30 or 60 years gets valued almost entirely on the yield between issuance and the call date, as if that date were the real maturity. The contract says "issuer's discretion." The price embeds "it will obviously be called." That gap between legal right and market expectation is exactly where perpetual bonds become dangerous.

## The 2022 Heungkuk Life Episode — What Happens When the Convention Breaks

That gap turned real in November 2022. Heungkuk Life had issued a $500 million perpetual bond in 2017 at a 4.475% coupon, with a call date of November 9, 2022. On October 31 — just over a week before that date — the company's board decided not to exercise the call. The reasoning was straightforward: aggressive rate hikes through 2022 had made issuing a replacement perpetual bond prohibitively expensive, and Korean insurance regulations required that solvency ratios stay above a supervisory guideline of 150% even after early redemption — a constraint Heungkuk Life was reportedly close to. Legally, nothing was wrong. Skipping the call wasn't a default, and the contract's step-up clause meant Heungkuk Life was simply choosing to keep paying a now much higher rate — one expected to jump from 4.475% to roughly the high-6% range — rather than redeem.

The market's reaction said otherwise. The bond's price fell more than 30% in the aftermath, and the news that this was the first time a Korean financial institution had skipped a call since Woori Bank in 2009 — thirteen years earlier — amplified the shock. Investors who had assumed the "call date equals maturity" convention was ironclad suddenly had to ask whether other issuers might do the same thing. That question spread into broader selling pressure across Korean-issued dollar bonds (KP bonds) and a wave of canceled overseas bond issuance plans from other Korean financial firms. The timing made it worse: the episode landed within weeks of the Legoland-triggered [commercial paper and short-term funding market freeze](/en/basics/commercial-paper-abcp-explained/) that was separately gripping Korean credit markets in the fourth quarter of 2022 — together, the two events are often cited as compounding the credit crunch of that period. Facing intensifying backlash and fearing lasting damage to market trust, Heungkuk Life reversed its decision within a week, announcing on November 9 that it would redeem the bond as originally expected. A decision that was entirely legal had to be unwound within days — not because any law was broken, but because it violated a convention the market had quietly priced in as fact.

## How This Differs From Bank CoCo Bonds (AT1)

Perpetual bonds are often confused with [CoCo bonds (AT1)](/en/basics/coco-bonds-at1-explained/), and the two do share surface features — no maturity, equity-like treatment, hybrid status. But the risk each one carries works in fundamentally different ways. CoCo bonds exist specifically to satisfy Basel III bank capital requirements, and they carry a loss-absorption trigger written directly into the contract: if a bank's core capital ratio falls below a preset threshold, the bond automatically converts to equity or gets written down to zero, regardless of what the investor wants. That's a mechanical, forced trigger that only activates in an extreme crisis. The risk in an ordinary perpetual bond like Heungkuk Life's isn't a forced trigger at all — it's that the issuer retains full discretion over whether to honor a market convention that was never a legal promise in the first place. A CoCo bond's danger is a tail risk that only bites when a bank is genuinely failing. A perpetual bond's call-skip risk can surface in far more ordinary circumstances — a difficult refinancing environment, a regulatory ratio sitting close to its floor — long before the issuer is anywhere near collapse.

## Why Perpetual Bonds Keep Getting Issued — and What to Watch For

Despite this risk, perpetual bond issuance hasn't slowed. The benefit to issuers — improving leverage or solvency ratios without diluting shareholders — remains real, and investors keep buying as long as the yield premium over ordinary corporate bonds compensates them for the risk. What the Heungkuk Life episode made clear is that treating the call date as a guaranteed maturity is a convention-based assumption, not a legal one. The risk of a skipped call rises in exactly the conditions you'd expect: when rates have climbed sharply, when refinancing markets have tightened, or — for an insurer — when a regulatory solvency ratio is sitting close to its floor. The 2012 dispute over whether Doosan Infracore's perpetual bond, issued by a manufacturer rather than a financial institution, should even count as equity on the balance sheet is a reminder that this question isn't limited to insurers either. Assessing a perpetual bond's real credit risk means looking past the issuer's general financial health to ask a narrower question: when the call date arrives, will this issuer actually be able to refinance, or is it cornered enough that breaking the convention becomes the cheaper option? It's also worth checking whether a portfolio is concentrated in perpetual bonds from issuers whose call dates cluster around the same period — as Heungkuk Life showed, one issuer's decision can spread through trust damage to the pricing of an entire sector's bonds.

## Key Takeaways

- A perpetual bond earns equity-like accounting and credit treatment by combining three features: no (or extremely long) maturity, discretionary coupon payments, and deep subordination in a bankruptcy — but rating agencies typically grant only partial equity credit, often around half.
- Issuers usually attach a call option at year five or ten along with a coupon step-up clause if that call is skipped, and the market has priced in the convention that the call date functions as the real maturity — even though nothing legally requires redemption then.
- In 2022, Heungkuk Life skipped its call citing rate conditions and a solvency-ratio constraint — a fully legal move that nonetheless triggered a 30%+ price drop and spread distrust across Korean credit markets until the decision was reversed a week later.
- CoCo bonds (AT1) carry a forced, mechanical loss-absorption trigger tied to bank crises; ordinary perpetual bonds carry a different risk entirely — an issuer's discretionary choice to break a market convention under far more routine financial pressure.

## FAQ

### Does skipping the call option mean the issuer defaulted?
No. Perpetual bonds are designed with no maturity or an extremely long one, so declining to exercise a call option is a contractually permitted choice, not a default. The cost isn't legal liability — it's the price decline and trust damage that follow from breaking a market convention.

### Are CoCo bonds and perpetual bonds the same thing?
CoCo bonds are a specific type of perpetual bond built to satisfy bank regulatory capital rules, with a forced conversion or write-down trigger embedded in the contract. Most perpetual bonds issued by insurers or ordinary corporations carry no such trigger — their main risk is simply the possibility that the issuer skips the call.

### How should an investor think about the coupon step-up clause?
The step-up is designed to give the issuer a financial incentive to redeem on schedule, since the alternative is paying a much higher rate. But as Heungkuk Life showed, when refinancing conditions or regulatory constraints become more pressing than the extra coupon cost, that incentive alone doesn't guarantee the call will actually be exercised.
