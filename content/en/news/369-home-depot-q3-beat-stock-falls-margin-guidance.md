## What Happened

On Tuesday, November 17, Home Depot (NYSE: HD), the largest home improvement retailer in the United States, reported fiscal 2026 third-quarter results. Revenue came in at $43.4 billion, up 4.9% year over year, and comparable sales - the retail industry's key growth metric, measuring sales at existing stores and online channels excluding new store openings - rose 2.2%, clearing the roughly 1.7% consensus tracked by FactSet. Adjusted earnings per share reached $3.86, up from $3.74 a year earlier and slightly ahead of the roughly $3.84 Wall Street expected. Revenue, comparable sales, and EPS all beat estimates - a clean triple beat on paper.

Yet the stock moved the opposite direction. Shares fell more than 2% intraday following the release, with some readings showing a drop closer to 3%. On the surface, this was a good quarter. In practice, it became a textbook case of a company beating every headline number and still watching its stock sell off, because the market doesn't price a quarter that already happened - it prices what the company says is coming next.

That "what's coming next" came through in Home Depot's updated full-year guidance. The company raised its full-year sales growth outlook to roughly 4.5%, signaling confidence that the comparable-sales momentum from Q3 would carry into the holiday-season fourth quarter. The catch was profitability. Home Depot guided full-year gross margin to roughly 33.0%, a figure that landed below what investors had been modeling, and guided full-year adjusted EPS growth to only about 2% year over year. Back that growth rate out into dollar terms, and it implies fourth-quarter EPS of roughly $2.77 - below the $2.81 the Street had been expecting. In other words, the company that just delivered a strong Q3 told investors, in the same breath, that its profitability is likely to soften in the very quarter that matters most for a home improvement retailer: the run-up to year-end.

## Why "Good Numbers" Produced a Falling Stock

The core reason is that investors weighed margin trajectory more heavily than the sales beat. Revenue growth is relatively easy to generate when underlying demand cooperates; margin is a far more comprehensive signal of a company's cost structure, pricing power, and sales mix. By raising sales guidance while simultaneously cutting margin guidance, Home Depot effectively told the market it expects to sell more while keeping less of every dollar as profit. That combination - "growing revenue, shrinking profitability" - tends to move a stock far more decisively than a same-quarter sales beat, because it raises questions about the quality, not just the size, of future growth.

The second factor is the implied fourth-quarter EPS gap. A strong third quarter is, by definition, already in the past. Markets are forward-looking by nature, and once analysts backed out an implied Q4 EPS of $2.77 from the percentage-based guidance and compared it against the $2.81 consensus, that gap got priced into the stock almost immediately. Companies that guide using year-over-year growth percentages rather than explicit dollar ranges - as Home Depot did here - often create exactly this kind of mismatch once analysts convert the percentage into real numbers and compare it to what they had already modeled.

The third factor is cost structure. Home Depot has spent recent years expanding aggressively into the professional contractor ("Pro") market through acquisitions of large wholesale distributors such as SRS Distribution. Wholesale-style revenue of that kind structurally carries thinner margins than traditional retail sales, so as that mix grows as a share of total revenue, it mechanically dilutes the company's blended margin even when the underlying business is performing well. Layer on tariff-related input cost pressure, wage growth, and volatile freight and energy costs, and the picture becomes one where generating top-line growth and defending margins simultaneously has gotten structurally harder, not easier.

Timing mattered too. Home Depot's report landed just one trading session before Nvidia's fiscal third-quarter results (released after the close on November 17), and in the same stretch as heightened Federal Reserve uncertainty, including rising odds of a December half-point rate hike and the release of FOMC minutes. With macro nerves already elevated across the market, investors had less patience than usual for anything short of a clean guide. Home improvement spending is also tightly linked to mortgage rates, and with the 30-year Treasury yield holding near multi-decade highs, the longer-run concern about remodeling demand staying soft added another layer of caution to how the quarter was read.

## What to Take Away From This

- **The word "beat" alone tells you less than you'd think.** Revenue, comparable sales, and EPS can all top estimates and the stock can still fall if forward guidance disappoints. Always separate "last quarter's numbers" from "next quarter's guidance" when reading an earnings report.
- **Convert percentage-based guidance into dollar terms before trusting it.** When a company guides to "about 2% EPS growth" rather than a specific dollar range, do the math yourself. In Home Depot's case, that conversion revealed a fourth-quarter EPS implied by guidance that sat below what the Street expected - the real story behind the sell-off.
- **Margin direction can matter more to a stock than the sales growth rate.** Rising revenue paired with falling margin raises questions about growth quality, and the market frequently punishes that combination more severely than it rewards a same-quarter sales beat.
- **Acquisition-driven expansion can dilute margins before it pays off.** Buying large wholesale distributors like SRS extends a retailer's addressable market and can be the right long-term move, but the near-term effect is often a lower blended margin - a trade-off best judged over several quarters, not one earnings print.
- **Earnings released in the middle of a dense macro calendar face a tougher audience.** When Fed policy uncertainty and other mega-cap earnings are clustering around the same days, the market's tolerance for anything less than a flawless guide tends to drop, and otherwise-ordinary guidance can draw a sharper reaction than it would in a quieter week.

## FAQ

### Did Home Depot's stock fall because of bad earnings?
No. Third-quarter revenue ($43.4 billion, up 4.9%), comparable sales (up 2.2%), and adjusted EPS ($3.86) all beat Wall Street estimates. Shares fell because the company's accompanying full-year guidance on margins and EPS growth came in below what investors had modeled - specifically, an implied fourth-quarter EPS of about $2.77 versus the $2.81 consensus.

### What exactly is "comparable sales" and why does it matter so much?
Comparable sales (also called same-store sales) measure revenue growth at stores and online channels that have been open for at least a year, stripping out the effect of newly opened locations. It matters because a retailer can grow total revenue simply by opening new stores even if its existing business is stagnant - comparable sales show whether the core, already-established business is actually getting stronger.

### Does Home Depot's margin guidance signal trouble for other home improvement retailers?
Not directly, but it's a useful data point. Cost pressures like tariffs, rising wages, and a growing mix of lower-margin wholesale/Pro revenue are industry-wide dynamics that competitors such as Lowe's also contend with to varying degrees. That said, each retailer's product mix and customer base (DIY shoppers versus professional contractors) differ enough that Home Depot's specific margin trajectory shouldn't be assumed to apply identically elsewhere.
