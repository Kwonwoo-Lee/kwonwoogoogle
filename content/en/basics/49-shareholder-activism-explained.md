## They Don't Want to Take Over the Company — So Why Can It Still Feel Threatened?

In [the anti-takeover defenses lesson](/en/basics/anti-takeover-defenses-explained/), poison pills and golden parachutes were described as tools built to stop one specific kind of threat: someone trying to seize control of the entire company. But most of the funds that show up in headlines every proxy season aren't after control at all. They buy a modest stake — often in the single digits — and then push the board to raise the dividend, buy back stock, or add an outside director. No one is threatening to take the company away, so why can't the board just ignore them? Part of the answer, at least in Korea, is a legal shift: a July 2025 amendment to the Commercial Act expanded directors' fiduciary duty from "the company" alone to "the company and its shareholders," giving boards a fresh legal reason to weigh minority shareholders' interests fairly. Add the Korea Exchange's "value-up" push for better shareholder returns and governance disclosure, and activist funds now have more leverage than they've had in years. This lesson walks through exactly how an activist campaign is run, how it differs from a hostile takeover, and what to check when one shows up in a stock you own.

## What Activism Actually Is — Influence Without Control

Shareholder activism is an investment strategy in which a hedge fund or asset manager buys a meaningful stake in a company and then uses that stake as leverage to push for changes in how the company is run or how it allocates capital. The defining feature is influence without control. A hostile acquirer typically needs to cross 50% ownership, or launch a tender offer, to actually take the company; an activist fund can often get what it wants with a stake in the single digits — commonly somewhere around 5–10%. That's not enough to control a company outright, but it's enough to be heard, and enough to serve as a rallying point for other institutional and retail shareholders who might vote the same way. The asks look nothing like a takeover, either: bigger dividends or buybacks, spinning off or selling an underperforming division, restructuring to close a holding-company discount, nominating independent directors to check the board, or simply pushing out management seen as underperforming. Activism, in short, isn't about taking the company away — it's about finding the loudest channel a minority stake can use, and using it.

## How a Campaign Actually Unfolds — From Quiet Buying to a Proxy Fight

Activist campaigns tend to follow a fairly predictable sequence. The first stage is quiet accumulation: the fund buys shares on the open market without tipping its hand. In Korea, crossing 5% ownership of a listed company triggers a mandatory disclosure — filed with the Financial Supervisory Service and the exchange within five business days — that must state the purpose of the holding, either "simple investment" or "intent to influence management." This filing, commonly called the "5% rule," is the market's first real signal: once a fund checks the "intent to influence management" box, everyone assumes this isn't a passive position. Stage two is private engagement — most funds approach the board and management privately, well before any public letter, and try to negotiate. A large share of activist campaigns are actually settled quietly at this stage and never become public at all. If the company stalls or the talks break down, stage three is a public campaign: open letters, media interviews, and direct appeals to other institutional shareholders for support. If none of that moves the board, the fund escalates to the final stage — a formal shareholder proposal and a proxy fight. Under Korean corporate law, any shareholder holding at least 3% of outstanding shares has the right to formally place an item — a dividend increase, a director nomination — on the shareholder meeting agenda. Once that item is on the ballot, the activist fund is effectively asking every other shareholder to hand over their proxy vote in its favor.

## The Math Behind a Proxy Fight — Why 5% Can Still Move a Company

A hypothetical case shows how the arithmetic actually works. Say Company Z has 100 million shares outstanding, and Activist Fund C has accumulated 5% (5 million shares), then puts a dividend-increase proposal on the ballot. Under Korean corporate law, an ordinary resolution like this needs two things at once: a majority of the votes actually cast at the meeting, and support from at least one-quarter of all outstanding shares.

| | Stake | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Activist Fund C | 5% (5,000,000 shares) | Proposed the resolution |
| Controlling shareholder & affiliates | 25% (25,000,000 shares) | Expected to vote against |
| National Pension Service & other institutions | 20% (20,000,000 shares) | The real swing vote |
| Retail and other institutional holders | 50% (50,000,000 shares) | Outcome depends on turnout |

Fund C's own 5% can never pass this resolution on its own. But once large institutional holders — a national pension fund, say — and enough of the retail base line up behind the proposal, the math changes completely. What actually decides most proxy fights isn't the activist's own stake; it's which side domestic or international proxy advisors (Korea's own governance research firms, or ISS and Glass Lewis internationally) recommend, and whether the institutions that lean on those recommendations actually vote that way. In other words, an activist fund doesn't win with its 5% — it wins by using that 5% as the seed that persuades the other 95%.

## How This Differs From a Hostile Takeover and Its Defenses

The poison pills and white-knight defenses covered in [the anti-takeover defenses lesson](/en/basics/anti-takeover-defenses-explained/) exist to stop someone from actually seizing control of the company. Most activist funds never want control in the first place, and that changes how a company responds. Diluting a genuine control-seeking acquirer with a poison pill makes sense; triggering the same weapon against a fund that only wants a bigger dividend is much harder to justify, and risks being read as the board crushing a legitimate shareholder request by force. In practice, companies facing activist campaigns respond far more often by partially conceding — adjusting dividend policy, accepting one or two board nominees — than by reaching for a formal defense. The two categories aren't entirely separate, though. If a campaign escalates all the way to demanding a management shake-up or an outright sale of the company, the board may start treating it as a genuine control threat and reach for defensive tools like a white knight after all — meaning activism and hostile M&A sit on the same spectrum, even if they usually stay at opposite ends of it.

| | Activist fund | Hostile acquirer |
|---|---|---|
| Typical target stake | Roughly 5–10% | Above 50%, or enough for control |
| Ultimate goal | Push capital allocation / governance change | Take control of the company itself |
| Main tools | Open letters, shareholder proposals, proxy fights | Tender offers, open-market buying, mergers |
| Company's usual response | Negotiate, partially concede | Poison pill, white knight, golden parachute |
| Disclosure trigger | 5% ownership filing (check the stated purpose) | Tender-offer filing, 5% ownership filing |

## Why Activism Is Picking Up in Korea Right Now

A viable activist campaign generally needs two things at once: a company that genuinely has room to improve — undervalued assets, thin shareholder returns, an inefficient corporate structure — and a legal or institutional environment where the board can't easily brush off minority shareholders. Korea has recently seen both conditions strengthen together. As covered in [the holding company discount](/en/basics/holding-company-discount/) and [circular shareholding](/en/basics/circular-shareholding-explained/) lessons, many large Korean business groups control an entire group through a relatively small direct stake — a structure that tends to build in exactly the kind of undervaluation activist funds look for. On top of that, the Commercial Act amendment that took effect on July 22, 2025 broadened directors' fiduciary duty from "the company" to "the company and its shareholders," giving boards a new legal basis for weighing all shareholders' interests fairly, not just the controlling shareholder's. The Korea Exchange's value-up program adds another layer of pressure by encouraging listed companies to publicly disclose their own dividend, buyback, and governance-improvement plans — companies with a thin or missing plan become easier targets. How far and how fast this trend continues, though, depends on further legislation and on how actual proxy votes play out, so it's worth reading this as a snapshot of where things stand as of this writing rather than a settled trend.

## What Investors Should Check

If a headline says an activist fund has taken a position in a stock you follow, the first thing worth checking is the stated purpose in its 5% ownership filing — "simple investment" versus "intent to influence management." The latter is a real signal that a public campaign could follow. From there, check whether the company actually has the kind of undervaluation activists target — an oversized holding-company discount, a low payout ratio, an inefficient subsidiary structure — and whether it has already made a value-up or dividend-policy disclosure of its own. If the campaign goes public, keep an eye on which way domestic and international proxy advisors recommend voting, and where large institutional holders such as the national pension fund land — those two factors decide most proxy fights. Finally, remember that a campaign starting is no guarantee the demands get met or the stock rises. If the controlling shareholder's stake is overwhelming, a proxy fight may never have realistic odds in the first place, and plenty of campaigns simply fizzle out with nothing achieved.

## Takeaways

- Shareholder activism isn't about gaining control — it's a strategy that uses a minority stake as leverage to push for bigger dividends/buybacks or better governance.
- A typical campaign runs from quiet accumulation, to a mandatory 5% ownership disclosure, to private negotiation, to a public letter campaign, to a formal shareholder proposal and proxy fight.
- Because activists usually don't want control, companies respond far more often with negotiation and partial concessions than with formal defenses like poison pills.
- Korea's July 2025 expansion of directors' fiduciary duty, combined with the value-up program, has meaningfully increased the legal and institutional leverage activist campaigns can use.
- Checking the stated purpose in the 5% filing, the company's actual undervaluation case, and where proxy advisors and institutional holders stand is the starting point for judging whether a campaign is likely to succeed.

## FAQ

### Does a stock always rise once an activist fund buys in?
No. Prices often rise early on excitement alone, but whether the demands actually get met depends on the controlling shareholder's stake size, whether other institutions join in, and how willing the company is to negotiate. If the controlling shareholder's stake is overwhelming, a proxy fight may never really be viable, and a campaign that ends with nothing achieved can send the stock right back down as the excitement fades.

### How do you tell an activist fund apart from a hostile acquirer?
The clearest signal is the stated purpose and target stake in the ownership disclosure filing. "Simple investment" paired with a request for better dividends or governance looks like activism; "intent to influence management" paired with a stake climbing toward 50% or a push for board control looks like a hostile takeover. That line can blur, though, if a campaign escalates far enough.

### Can an individual retail investor take part in an activist campaign?
Launching a public letter campaign or leading a proxy fight yourself isn't realistic, but you can take part in one already underway by submitting your proxy in favor of the resolution an activist fund has put forward. Reading the shareholder meeting notice and the proxy advisors' published recommendations is a good way to understand what each proposal is actually about.
