## How Can Winning Also Mean Losing?

Every year around free-agent season, sports headlines repeat a familiar story: a team pays a "record-breaking" contract to land a star player, only to watch that same contract drag the team down a few seasons later once the player underperforms. Markets news tells a similar story about takeovers — the company that outbids every rival to win a prized acquisition target ends up, a few years on, struggling under the debt load that same acquisition created. How can "winning" a competition and "losing money" from that same win coexist in the same event? Economists have a specific name for this: the **winner's curse**. Understanding how it works makes M&A headlines and hot IPO allocation results much easier to read correctly. This lesson isn't about deciding whether to bid on a specific deal or stock right now — it's about a structural trap hidden inside the act of winning a competition itself.

## Common Value Auctions: Everyone Is Guessing at the Same Answer

The winner's curse shows up in what economists call a **common value auction** — not an auction like fine art, where value is genuinely subjective, but one where the "true value" is exactly the same for every bidder, yet nobody actually knows what that number is. The size of an offshore oil deposit, the real future cash flow a takeover target will generate, the fair value of a company about to go public — all of these share that structure. There's one correct answer, and every participant is merely *estimating* it using their own data and analysis.

Here's the key statistical fact. When several people independently estimate the same unknown value, their guesses scatter above and below the true number — some too low, some too high, averaging out close to the truth. But an auction doesn't award the deal to whoever guessed closest to the average. It awards it to whoever bid **highest**. As the number of bidders grows, the odds increase that at least one of them happened to estimate wildly optimistically — and that bidder is exactly the one who wins. The winning bid ends up reflecting not "the most accurate estimate of true value" but "the most optimistic estimate among all the participants," and that systematic gap between the winning bid and the true value is the winner's curse. Winning the auction is, ironically, evidence that your own estimate was probably too high relative to everyone else's.

## Why Uncertainty and Competitor Count Make It Worse

The phenomenon was first documented systematically in a 1971 paper by three petroleum engineers at Atlantic Richfield — Capen, Clapp, and Campbell — published in the Journal of Petroleum Technology. They set out to statistically explain something oil companies were experiencing firsthand: winners of offshore drilling-rights auctions in the Gulf of Mexico kept earning lower-than-expected returns, year after year. With no way to know a reservoir's true size in advance, competing oil companies each estimated its value from their own exploration data and bid aggressively, and the company with the most optimistic estimate kept winning — and kept losing money on the deal. Economist Richard Thaler later brought the term to a much wider audience with his 1992 book *The Winner's Curse*, extending it well beyond formal auction theory into everyday economic behavior.

Management researchers Max Bazerman and William Samuelson later ran experiments identifying two conditions that make the curse worse. The first is the **degree of uncertainty** in the value being estimated: the more widely participants' estimates scatter around the true value, the larger the gap between the most optimistic guess and reality. The second is the **number of bidders**: as competition grows, the statistical odds rise that the pool includes someone with an extreme, overly optimistic estimate — and that their bid will be the one that wins. In other words, the less anyone truly knows about what they're bidding on, and the fiercer the competition, the greater the risk that winning itself becomes the losing move. That's precisely what makes it dangerous — it cuts against intuition, since fiercer competition should call for more caution, but in practice it tends to produce the opposite reaction.

## M&A: Acquirers Who Overestimate Synergy

The arena where this idea gets cited most often is corporate mergers and acquisitions. When multiple bidders compete for the same target, the future cash flow and [synergies](/en/basics/leveraged-buyout-lbo-explained/) the target will actually generate are a common value — a single true number nobody can observe directly. Each bidder runs its own due diligence to estimate that value, but the deal goes to whichever bidder offers the highest price. The problem: that bidder is more likely to be the one who estimated synergies most optimistically, not the one who estimated them most accurately. A recurring finding across multiple research studies on large M&A deals is that a substantial share of them end up destroying value for the acquirer's own shareholders rather than creating it. Paying a premium and "winning" the bidding war often turns out, after the fact, to have been evidence that the synergies needed to justify that premium never really existed.

Korean markets have seen this pattern repeat as well — a conglomerate wins a fiercely contested bidding war by offering the highest price, only to have the whole group destabilized years later under the debt burden that acquisition required. The common thread is a bidding process where the pressure to not lose pushes the offer price away from a careful estimate of underlying value and toward whatever number it takes to beat the competition. The stronger the internal organizational determination to "win this deal no matter what" during negotiations over price or a [merger exchange ratio](/en/basics/merger-swap-ratio-explained/), the weaker the objectivity of the valuation — and the greater the risk of falling into the winner's curse.

## IPOs: The Curse Falls Hardest on Less-Informed Investors

The winner's curse shows up in IPO allocation too, though through a different mechanism than a traditional auction. In a 1986 paper, economist Kevin Rock modeled the IPO market as an "auction" split between well-informed institutional investors and relatively uninformed retail investors. Informed investors do careful homework and concentrate their subscriptions on the IPOs they've identified as genuinely underpriced, while skipping the ones they judge to be overpriced. Uninformed retail investors, lacking the same ability to tell good deals from bad, tend to subscribe fairly evenly across nearly every IPO on offer.

The result: underpriced, genuinely attractive IPOs draw heavy competition from informed investors too, making shares hard for retail investors to get allocated in large quantities. Overpriced IPOs, by contrast, see informed investors step away entirely — leaving retail investors to fill the gap and receive their full requested allocation. Retail investors haven't made any particular mistake here, yet they systematically end up under-allocated in the good deals and fully allocated in the bad ones — a structural form of adverse selection. This is a core reason why the [IPO book-building process](/en/basics/ipo-pricing-bookbuilding/) tends to price new issues somewhat below estimated fair value on average: pricing has to leave enough money on the table that uninformed investors, despite facing this adverse selection, still come out ahead on average and keep participating in the IPO market at all.

## Why the Mistake Keeps Repeating: Confirmation Bias During Due Diligence

The winner's curse has been documented since the 1970s, yet the same mistake keeps recurring in M&A because competition itself distorts how organizations make decisions. As a bidding war heats up, deal teams face implicit pressure to "find the synergy numbers that justify this deal," and due-diligence staff, once committed to that frame, tend to weight favorable evidence more heavily than they should — a classic case of confirmation bias, the kind of behavior covered in [efficient market hypothesis and behavioral finance](/en/basics/efficient-market-hypothesis-behavioral-finance/). People are prone to selectively trust information that justifies a decision they've already leaned toward. Add in escalation of commitment — the determination not to let a rival walk away with "our" deal — and the original value estimate keeps drifting upward under the pressure of the competition itself. Seen this way, the winner's curse isn't just a statistical coincidence; it's also a structural problem in which competition itself systematically biases human judgment in one direction.

## Bidding Lower Is the Rational Response

If the winner's curse is a statistically unavoidable feature of common value competition, the rational response for any individual participant is to bid below their own value estimate — a practice called **bid shading**. Rather than reasoning "if my estimate is right, this price is a good deal," a bidder who shades their bid reasons instead that "the very fact I won tells me my estimate was probably too high" — and builds that correction into the price upfront. As Bazerman and Samuelson's experiments showed, the wider the uncertainty and the larger the pool of competitors, the deeper that discount needs to be to make the economics work out. In practice, this translates into setting a conservative ceiling on an acquisition price before negotiations begin and refusing to let competitive pressure push past it, or sizing positions more cautiously in IPOs where an investor's own research is thin. Ultimately, the most realistic defense against the winner's curse is separating the pressure to "win this competition" from the process of estimating value in the first place.

Many companies build this directly into their M&A playbooks: the board pre-approves a maximum walk-away price before negotiations start, with a procedure that prevents even a senior deal lead from unilaterally bidding past it once talks are underway. That ceiling only works if it's set before anyone is actually competing — once negotiations are live, the atmosphere in the room and news of a rival's latest bid can sway judgment in real time, which is exactly why the limit has to be locked in while everyone involved is still calm and uncommitted. Retail investors can apply a similar discipline to IPO subscriptions, sizing down their request specifically for sectors or business models they aren't equipped to analyze. A pre-set ceiling like this functions as a real safeguard against the late-stage temptation to think "we've come this far — a little more and we win."

## Takeaway

- The winner's curse describes a common value auction — where the true value is identical for all bidders but unknown to anyone — in which the highest bidder tends to be the one who overestimated most optimistically, making the winning bid systematically overpay on average.
- First documented statistically in a 1971 study of offshore oil-lease auctions, the curse grows larger as uncertainty about the true value increases and as the number of competing bidders increases.
- In M&A, it shows up as acquirers who win bidding wars by overestimating synergy and paying too much; in IPO markets, it shows up as adverse selection, where less-informed retail investors end up disproportionately allocated the overpriced deals that informed investors avoided.
- The rational defense is bid shading — bidding below your own value estimate, with a deeper discount as uncertainty and competitor count both increase.

## FAQ

### Does the winner's curse only happen in formal auctions?
The concept originated in auction theory, but it applies broadly to any fierce competition where simply "winning" is itself a signal that the winner's estimate or bid may have been too aggressive. Free-agent sports bidding wars, competitive bids for contracts, and real estate auctions all share the same common-value structure and can exhibit the same effect.

### Does paying a high price in an acquisition always mean the winner's curse occurred?
No. A high price can still be a good deal if the acquirer actually delivers the synergy and growth needed to justify it. The winner's curse is really a retrospective judgment — it applies specifically when a value estimate that was inflated by competitive pressure to win fails to be borne out by actual results afterward.

### Does this mean retail investors shouldn't subscribe to IPOs?
No. The adverse-selection structure of the IPO market mainly explains why IPO prices tend to be set at a discount to estimated fair value on average — it isn't an argument against investing in IPOs altogether. It does suggest that the less research information an investor has about a specific offering, the greater the risk of ending up with a disproportionate share of the overpriced ones.
