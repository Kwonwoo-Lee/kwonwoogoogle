## The "Steward" Behind the Shares

If you've read about [beneficial ownership disclosure rules](/en/basics/beneficial-ownership-report-5-rule/) or [ESG investing](/en/basics/esg-investing-rating-explained/), you've probably seen a line like "the National Pension Service voted against the proposal under its stewardship code." Often the article moves on without ever explaining what that code actually requires. The word steward originally means someone who manages a household's property without owning it, but is still bound to look after it faithfully on the owner's behalf. Pension funds, asset managers, and insurers occupy exactly that position. The money they invest ultimately belongs to pensioners and fund holders, not to the institution itself, so the shares that money buys are, in a real sense, owned by those people — not by the fund manager. A stewardship code is a voluntary set of principles telling institutional investors: since you're holding someone else's money, you also need to exercise the rights and responsibilities of being a shareholder, not just collect a management fee and look away. This lesson covers why the code exists, what it concretely asks institutions to do, how Korea and its national pension fund adopted it, and why it draws criticism from two opposite directions at once.

## Why It Exists — A Question the 2008 Crisis Left Behind

The stewardship code traces back to the UK in the aftermath of the 2008 financial crisis. The Walker Review, commissioned to examine what went wrong, flagged an uncomfortable fact: while financial firms were taking on excessive risk, the pension funds and asset managers that held large blocks of their shares did essentially nothing to push back. In theory these institutions were the largest voting blocs at annual meetings; in practice, many behaved as quiet shareholders who simply sold and walked away when something displeased them. Out of that criticism, the UK's Financial Reporting Council published the world's first Stewardship Code in 2010. The logic is straightforward: if a corporate governance code tells boards how to behave, a stewardship code is its mirror image — it tells the shareholders, especially large institutional ones, how to behave. Watching the board alone isn't enough; the shareholders who are supposed to watch the board also need to do their job. The UK experiment was judged successful enough that the Netherlands, Japan, Hong Kong, Malaysia, and other markets adopted similar voluntary codes, and Korea later joined that same wave.

## What It Actually Requires — Comply or Explain

In practice, a stewardship code asks signatories to do a handful of concrete things. First, publish a clear policy on how they vote. Second, disclose how they manage conflicts of interest — for example, when an affiliated asset manager has to vote on a proposal from its own parent group. Third, go beyond just casting a vote on meeting day: periodically review the long-term value and risk factors at the companies they invest in, and engage privately with management when something looks off. Fourth, publish an annual report summarizing all of this activity. Crucially, none of this is backed by law. Instead, codes run on a mechanism called **comply or explain**. A signatory either follows each principle, or publicly explains why it didn't. There's no fine and no regulator standing over anyone, yet the mechanism works through reputational pressure: having to publish, in writing, "we didn't follow this principle and we won't say why" is embarrassing enough that most institutions choose to comply instead. This is a different posture from [shareholder activism](/en/basics/shareholder-activism-explained/), which buys stakes specifically to force management change. Stewardship leans toward ongoing dialogue with existing management and routine voting, aiming for gradual improvement rather than a change of control.

## How Korea Adopted It

Korea's own code grew out of controversy surrounding the 2015 Samsung C&T–Cheil Industries merger vote, where the National Pension Service's approval drew heavy criticism. An academic drafting committee responded by publishing the formally named "Principles on Stewardship Responsibilities of Institutional Investors" in December 2016. Like the UK version, it's voluntary rather than legally mandated, and the number of signatory institutions has grown from 16 in 2017 to well over 200 in recent years. Separately from that private-sector code, the National Pension Service's own fund management committee voted in July 2018 to formally adopt stewardship principles for its own operations. Because the NPS holds such enormous stakes across the market, its vote on any given proposal is itself market-moving news. Since adopting the code, the fund has noticeably increased how often it votes against director nominations and executive pay packages — though, as the next section covers, whether that's been applied consistently is a separate question. This shift has also coincided with a related regulatory change covered in the [5% rule lesson](/en/basics/beneficial-ownership-report-5-rule/): a new "general investment" disclosure category was carved out so that routine shareholder actions, like opposing a director nominee, wouldn't automatically get classified under the much stricter "management participation" disclosure bucket — a change that made it easier for institutions to exercise ordinary stewardship rights without tripping heavier reporting requirements.

## How Engagement Actually Escalates

Stewardship in practice is rarely just "cast one no vote at the annual meeting." Institutions typically follow an escalating ladder, starting with the least confrontational step and only moving up if nothing changes. Picture a hypothetical: Asset Manager A holds shares in Company B, and an independent director nominee's actual independence looks questionable. Step one is **private engagement** — raising the concern directly with the board or management and asking for an explanation or a change. If that resolves things, there's no public friction at all. If not, step two is **joint engagement**, teaming up with other institutional shareholders to send a joint letter or otherwise raise the pressure collectively. If that still goes nowhere, step three is **a public vote against the proposal**, with the institution's reasoning spelled out in its published voting record. The last resort is **exit** — simply selling the stock and walking away. What stewardship codes changed from pre-2008 practice is precisely this sequence. Previously, jumping straight to step four (sell and leave) was the default reaction to any disagreement. The code asks institutions to show they genuinely tried steps one through three first, and to keep a transparent record of the attempt. That whole process is what "engagement" means in this context, and documenting it honestly is the core obligation the code imposes.

## The Criticism — "Hidden Power" and "Box-Ticking," at the Same Time

Stewardship codes draw fire from two opposite directions simultaneously. One camp says they have too little bite. Korea's Board of Audit and Inspection has flagged inconsistent standards in how the NPS casts its votes, and civil-society critics argue many signatories simply sign on and then rubber-stamp management proposals anyway — publishing a polished annual report while staying passive on the votes that actually matter. The other camp says the opposite: that stewardship concentrates too much power. As covered in the lesson on [passive investing's concentration risk](/en/basics/passive-investing-concentration-risk/), when a handful of giant asset managers simultaneously hold large stakes in companies that compete with each other, their stewardship teams effectively control the voting outcomes at tens of thousands of companies — a different kind of concentrated power than regulators originally set out to fix. The fact that both criticisms coexist says something about where stewardship codes actually sit: because they carry no legal force, "too passive" and "too powerful" can both be true descriptions of different institutions, or even the same institution on different issues.

There's also a structural conflict-of-interest problem that keeps resurfacing. Many asset managers depend on pension funds or large institutional clients for fee revenue, while simultaneously being required to vote on proposals from companies affiliated with those same clients. Voting against an affiliated company's management can strain a lucrative business relationship, creating a built-in incentive to stay quiet. Codes try to address exactly this by requiring disclosure of conflict-of-interest policies, but publishing a policy and actually letting it change voting behavior are two different things, and critics argue the gap between them remains wide. Korean regulators have acknowledged this and are working on tightening how compliance with the code is actually monitored, which is a reminder that the framework is still evolving rather than settled.

## Why This Matters Even If You Never Sign a Code

Individual investors never sign a stewardship code themselves, but understanding the concept changes how you read certain headlines. "The National Pension Service voted against a director nomination at Company X" isn't a random news item — it's a signal that a major institutional holder is publicly flagging a governance concern through the exact channel the code was designed to create. Conversely, if an institution keeps approving proposals that clearly raise red flags, that's a reasonable basis to question whether its published stewardship report matches its actual voting behavior. Many large asset managers and the NPS itself publish annual stewardship reports, and some break voting records down by individual company and individual agenda item. Checking whether your own pension or fund actually does this — and whether the report contains concrete examples of votes against management, not just general statements of principle — is one way to judge whether an institution treats stewardship as a real practice or a compliance formality. In the end, a stewardship code isn't a trading strategy or a return driver; it's closer to a literacy skill for judging how responsibly the institution managing your money actually exercises the ownership rights that money buys.

## FAQ

### Is there a penalty for not following a stewardship code?
No. These are voluntary codes with no fines or legal sanctions attached. The enforcement mechanism is comply-or-explain: a signatory that departs from a principle has to publicly explain why, and the reputational cost of that disclosure is what drives compliance in practice.

### Are Korea's private-sector code and the NPS's own code the same thing?
They share the same roots but aren't identical. The "Principles on Stewardship Responsibilities of Institutional Investors," published in December 2016 by an academic drafting committee, is the voluntary private-sector code that any institutional investor can sign. The National Pension Service separately adopted its own version of these principles through its fund management committee in July 2018.

### How is stewardship different from shareholder activism?
[Shareholder activism](/en/basics/shareholder-activism-explained/) involves buying a stake specifically to push for management change or other aggressive intervention. Stewardship leans toward ongoing dialogue with existing management, periodic monitoring, and routine voting — it isn't aimed at forcing a change of control.
