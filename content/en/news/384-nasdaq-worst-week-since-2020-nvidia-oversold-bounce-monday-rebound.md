## What Happened

Wall Street closed out last week in the kind of session that gets remembered. On Friday, November 20, the S&P 500 fell 3.70% to 6,910.39, the Dow Jones Industrial Average dropped 1.93% to 48,147.33, and the Nasdaq Composite was hit hardest, sinking 5.28% to 22,617.73. Zoom out to the full week and the damage is even starker: the Nasdaq 100 lost roughly 10%, its worst five-day stretch since March 2020, while the S&P 500 shed about 8.6%, its worst week in more than six years. Both the S&P 500 and the Dow slid into correction territory - more than 10% below their respective highs - and the Nasdaq Composite turned negative for the year, erasing every point of its 2026 gain.

The proximate trigger was Nvidia. On Friday alone, its shares plunged roughly 14% after Reuters reported that the SEC had opened an inquiry into the company's accounting practices, wiping out close to $500 billion in market value in a single session (see our earlier coverage of [Nvidia's 14% SEC-probe plunge](/en/news/nvidia-sec-accounting-probe-14-percent-drop-sp500-dow-correction/)). Across the full week, Nvidia fell about 23%, its worst five-day performance since 2008. Layered on top of that single-stock shock was a broader loss of conviction in the AI trade: Anthropic's record-breaking IPO, rather than reigniting enthusiasm, left Wall Street questioning whether the easy money in AI stocks had already been made. Bloomberg Economics' Anna Wong went as far as to suggest the Anthropic listing could mark "the end of the AI trade," while AI critic Gary Marcus calculated that AI-linked stocks had lost more than half their value over five months.

Then Monday, November 23 arrived, and the tape flipped hard. Futures pointed to a sharp rebound from the opening bell: S&P 500 futures rose roughly 2% to 2.2%, Nasdaq 100 futures climbed about 2.75% to 3%, and Dow futures gained close to 1.4% to 1.6%. Nvidia led the bounce by a wide margin, with futures jumping as much as 5.7% toward the $132-$135 range. By the time cash markets were trading, shares were changing hands near $134.56, up roughly 3.4% - with the daily Relative Strength Index (RSI) sitting around 26.8, a textbook oversold reading. Crucially, the stock remained below its 7-day, 20-day, 50-day, and 200-day moving averages even after the bounce, meaning Monday's move relieved an extreme condition without repairing the underlying technical damage.

## Why This Rebound Doesn't Settle the Debate

The split in how Wall Street is reading Monday's bounce tells you almost everything about where sentiment stands. Fundstrat's Tom Lee characterized the prior week's rout as a "healthy reset" rather than the start of something worse. JPMorgan's take was more technical: it described the episode as mostly an "AI de-leveraging event" - meaning leveraged positions and margin-driven selling amplified the drop more than any actual change in AI's long-term earnings outlook - and kept its broader multi-year view on the AI capital-spending cycle intact. On the other side, Haverford Trust's Hank Smith cautioned that a quick, durable recovery shouldn't be assumed, warning that AI-linked stocks could stay out of favor well into next year.

That disagreement maps directly onto the mechanics of an oversold bounce versus a genuine trend reversal. An RSI reading near 27 signals that a stock has fallen so far, so fast, that even a modest wave of buying - short sellers covering positions, bargain hunters stepping in, or both - can produce an outsized one-day pop. None of that requires a change in the underlying story. What it does not do is confirm that the selling is finished: Nvidia's stock sitting below all four of its key moving averages after a 3.4% bounce is the market's way of saying the longer-term trend is still pointed down until proven otherwise. Traders who study technical analysis call this distinction a "dead-cat bounce" risk - a sharp recovery inside an intact downtrend, as opposed to the start of a new uptrend, which typically requires the stock to reclaim its moving averages on rising volume over multiple sessions, not just one.

There's also a second, separate pressure stacked underneath the AI-specific story: rising Fed rate-hike expectations. Just as the AI-trade doubts were building, St. Louis Fed President Alberto Musalem warned that up to four additional rate hikes could be needed by next spring, reigniting a debate Governor Christopher Waller had started days earlier over a possible half-point "jumbo" hike in December (see our coverage of the [Fed's October minutes and Musalem's warning](/en/news/fed-october-minutes-waller-50bp-musalem-four-hikes-december-fomc/)). Higher rates matter enormously for AI infrastructure spending specifically, because so much of the data-center and chip buildout that Wall Street has been pricing into AI earnings estimates is funded with borrowed money. Goldman Sachs Research has estimated that AI capital spending accounted for roughly half of S&P 500 earnings growth this year - which means a rate shock and an AI-sentiment shock hitting in the same week isn't a coincidence of timing so much as two channels reinforcing the same underlying risk: expensive, debt-funded AI buildouts becoming harder to justify if both financing costs and investor patience are rising together.

## What to Take Away From This

The most important discipline here is not confusing relief with resolution. A single sharp rebound after an oversold reading - even one accompanied by genuinely upbeat commentary from respected strategists - is not the same signal as a confirmed trend change. Professional traders typically look for a stock or index to reclaim its key moving averages, hold those levels on a pullback, and show rising volume on the advance before treating a bounce as anything more than a bounce. Nvidia doing none of that yet, even after jumping more than 3%, is the clearest evidence that last week's downtrend is still technically intact.

A second lesson is about concentration risk. When a single company's accounting headline can knock roughly $500 billion off the market and drag the Dow and S&P 500 into correction territory in the same session, it's a reminder of how much of this market's recent gains have been concentrated in a small handful of AI-linked names. Portfolios that look diversified on paper - spread across a broad index fund, for instance - can carry far more single-stock risk than investors realize if that index's gains have been disproportionately driven by one or two companies. Rebalancing or simply being aware of that concentration before volatility hits is far more useful than trying to time the exact bottom of a selloff.

Finally, this episode is a useful case study in reading analyst disagreement itself as information. When well-regarded voices like Fundstrat and JPMorgan describe the same event as a healthy, largely technical de-leveraging, while others like Haverford Trust see a longer road back, that spread of opinion usually means the market genuinely doesn't know yet - and that's exactly the environment where position sizing and risk management matter more than conviction in either direction.

## FAQ

### Is the AI stock selloff over after Monday's bounce?
Not based on the evidence so far. Monday's rally relieved an extremely oversold technical condition - Nvidia's RSI near 27 is a classic signal that a stock fell too far, too fast - but the stock remained below its 7-, 20-, 50-, and 200-day moving averages even after bouncing. Traders generally want to see several sessions of the stock reclaiming and holding above those averages, on rising volume, before treating a bounce as a genuine trend reversal rather than a temporary relief rally within a larger downtrend.

### Why did Nvidia bounce if the SEC accounting probe hasn't been resolved?
Technical bounces and fundamental resolutions are different things. The SEC inquiry that triggered Friday's 14% plunge remains unresolved - neither Nvidia nor the SEC has confirmed details publicly - so Monday's move wasn't a signal that the investigation's overhang had lifted. Instead, strategists described it largely as short covering and bargain hunting after an oversold reading, which JPMorgan characterized as an "AI de-leveraging event" rather than a fundamental shift in the AI investment case.

### How are rising Fed rate-hike bets connected to the AI stock selloff?
The two pressures are separate in origin but reinforcing in effect. St. Louis Fed President Alberto Musalem's warning of up to four more hikes by spring 2027 raises the cost of capital broadly, which matters disproportionately for AI infrastructure because so much of the data-center and chip buildout driving AI earnings estimates is financed with debt. Goldman Sachs Research estimates AI capital spending drove roughly half of S&P 500 earnings growth this year, so higher borrowing costs and fading AI-trade enthusiasm hitting in the same week compound each other rather than being purely coincidental.
