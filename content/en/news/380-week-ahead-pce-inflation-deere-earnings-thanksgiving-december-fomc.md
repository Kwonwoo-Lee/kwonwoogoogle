## What Happened

The trading week starting Monday, November 23 is a Thanksgiving-shortened one. U.S. markets are fully closed Thursday, November 26 for the holiday, and close three hours early, at 1 p.m. Eastern, on Friday, November 27. That leaves effectively three full trading days plus a half-day Friday - and into that compressed window, Wall Street has crammed some of the most consequential data and earnings releases of the quarter.

The single most important day is Wednesday, November 25. At 8:30 a.m. Eastern that morning, the Commerce Department releases the second estimate of third-quarter GDP alongside the October Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) price index - the inflation gauge the Federal Reserve weights most heavily in setting policy. Consensus estimates point to headline PCE rising to roughly 3.6%-3.8% year over year, up from September's reading, with core PCE (excluding food and energy) coming in around 3.1%-3.2% year over year and about 0.3% month over month. The Fed's Beige Book, a qualitative survey of regional economic conditions ahead of the December FOMC meeting, is also scheduled for release that afternoon.

On the earnings side, farm and construction equipment maker Deere & Co. reports before the opening bell on Wednesday. Analysts are modeling EPS of roughly $4.06, up from $3.93 in the same quarter a year ago, on revenue of about $10.3 billion, down slightly from roughly $10.6 billion. If the EPS estimate holds, it would mark Deere's first year-over-year earnings increase since the fourth quarter of 2023 - notable after a long stretch of declines. Deere shares are already up close to 30% year-to-date. A day earlier, on Tuesday, November 24, Dell Technologies, HP, Best Buy, and Workday are among the companies lined up to report, giving investors a read on PC demand, consumer electronics, and enterprise software spending heading into the holiday season.

## Why This Week Matters

What separates this week from a routine holiday lull is that the compressed calendar concentrates, rather than dilutes, the amount of information markets have to process. Last week, Nvidia shares fell 14% in a single session on a Reuters report of an SEC accounting inquiry, dragging the S&P 500 and Dow Jones Industrial Average both more than 10% below their highs and into a formal correction. The same day, bitcoin broke below $57,000, roughly 55% off last October's peak. Against that risk-off backdrop, Wednesday's PCE and GDP releases aren't just routine data points - they're being treated as close to the last major inputs the Fed will see before its December 9-10 meeting, where futures markets have already priced in a meaningful probability of a quarter-point rate hike.

PCE matters less for its absolute level and more for whether it reinforces or undercuts those already-hawkish expectations. Following a hotter-than-expected October CPI report, several Fed officials - including Boston Fed President Susan Collins - have recently backed a "steady, gradual" path of further rate increases rather than a pause. If core PCE comes in at or above the 3.1%-3.2% consensus, it would support the case that inflation pressure is broad-based rather than a one-off energy spike, likely hardening the case for a December hike. A softer-than-expected print, on the other hand, could hand the already-correcting market a near-term reason to bounce.

Deere's results carry their own signal. As a bellwether for farm income and heavy capital spending, a return to year-over-year earnings growth would suggest industrial demand is stabilizing after a prolonged high-rate squeeze. Meanwhile, Dell, HP, and Best Buy's results function as an early read on the health of the consumer heading straight into Black Friday. If industrial and consumer signals diverge - one strengthening while the other weakens - investors may need to rethink broader assumptions about which parts of the economy are actually carrying growth right now.

Finally, don't discount the mechanical effect of fewer trading sessions. A full holiday closure plus an early Friday close typically mean thinner volume, and thin markets tend to amplify reactions to data surprises rather than dampen them. Combined with the elevated volatility already in place since last week's correction, this week has the ingredients for outsized moves packed into very few sessions.

## What to Take Away From This

- **Shortened trading weeks can produce bigger moves, not smaller ones.** Lower holiday volume means a single data surprise can move prices more than it would in a normal week - don't assume a holiday calendar means a quiet market.
- **Judge PCE by whether it confirms or breaks the existing rate-hike narrative**, not by the headline number alone. A print in line with or above consensus reinforces hawkish Fed expectations; a softer one could spark a relief bounce.
- **Pair industrial and consumer data for the full picture.** Deere represents capital-spending demand; Dell, HP, and Best Buy represent consumer demand. Reading only one side risks a distorted view of where the U.S. economy actually stands.
- **Always check the consensus estimate before reacting to a headline figure.** A core PCE reading of 3.2% is only meaningful relative to what the market already expected going in.
- **Price action during a low-volume holiday week often gets reassessed** once normal trading volume resumes - treat moves made on thin volume with some caution rather than extrapolating a new trend.

## FAQ

### Why does the Fed watch PCE more closely than CPI?
The Federal Reserve's official inflation target is defined in terms of PCE, not CPI. PCE uses a formula that adjusts for changes in consumer buying patterns, which many economists view as a more accurate real-time read on the cost of living. That's part of why PCE prints tend to move markets more than CPI releases in the run-up to an FOMC meeting.

### Does Deere's earnings report matter to investors outside the agriculture sector?
Yes. As a major seller of large farm and construction equipment, Deere's results are widely used as a proxy for business capital-spending appetite - whether companies are willing to commit to big-ticket purchases rather than defer them. That makes it a useful read on rate-sensitive industrial demand more broadly, not just agriculture.

### Do stocks typically rise during Thanksgiving week?
Historical data is sometimes cited showing a mild seasonal tendency for gains around the Thanksgiving holiday, but that's a statistical pattern, not a guarantee that repeats every year. With the market already in a correction heading into this particular week, actual data outcomes like PCE and GDP are likely to matter far more than any seasonal tailwind.
