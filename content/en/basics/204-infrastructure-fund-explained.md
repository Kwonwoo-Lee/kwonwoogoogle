## It Trades Like a Dividend Stock, But It Isn't One

Investors scanning Korean markets for high, stable yields eventually run into names like Macquarie Korea Infrastructure Fund (MKIF). It trades on the exchange like any other stock, and its dividend yield sits reliably higher than most blue chips. But open its financial reports and something unusual shows up: it isn't a semiconductor company, a cosmetics brand, or a bank. It's a vehicle that holds stakes in the operating rights to toll roads, ports, and tunnels — social overhead capital (SOC) — and collects toll and usage fees from the people who drive on them. On the surface it looks like a high-yield blue chip. Underneath, the way it earns money and the risks it carries are fundamentally different from both REITs and ordinary manufacturing stocks. This lesson covers what an infrastructure fund actually owns, how toll revenue becomes a dividend, and the two risks — government renegotiation and interest-rate sensitivity — that matter most when evaluating this asset class.

## What an Infrastructure Fund Actually Is: A Mix of Equity and Debt

An infrastructure fund invests in the project companies (SPCs) that build and operate public infrastructure — roads, railways, ports, tunnels — typically under a public-private partnership framework. In Korea's version of this model, a private sponsor finances and builds the facility, operates it for a fixed concession period (often several decades) to recover its investment through tolls or usage fees, and then transfers ownership back to the government or local authority once the contract ends. An infrastructure fund buys stakes across a handful of these SPCs — both equity and debt — and passes through the tolls and fees those projects collect as dividends and interest income to its own investors.

This points to a defining feature: unlike a typical shareholder, an infrastructure fund rarely holds pure equity. It often invests in the same SPC through senior loans, subordinated loans, and equity all at once — acting as both lender and owner in the same project. That blended structure lets the fund collect interest payments on its loans relatively early and predictably in the cash waterfall, while equity holders — including the fund itself in its equity tranche — take whatever is left over. Mixing debt and equity this way is designed to balance the stability of a lender's claim against the higher, more variable upside of ownership, and it's one of the sharpest structural differences from how REITs are typically built, covered below.

## Where the Cash Flow Comes From: Why Tolls Tend to Rise With Inflation

The core pitch for infrastructure funds is predictable cash flow. Drivers don't stop using a toll road and shippers don't stop using a port just because the economy softens a little — usage of essential infrastructure tends to be far less cyclical than discretionary spending. More importantly, the fee structure itself is usually written into the concession contract: tolls and usage fees are frequently indexed to inflation or a fixed escalation formula, adjusted on a set schedule. That means rising prices tend to push nominal toll revenue up along with them, rather than eroding it — which is why infrastructure assets are often described as carrying a built-in inflation hedge.

That description shouldn't be taken too literally, though. In practice, scheduled toll increases aren't always executed automatically just because the contract allows it. They go through negotiation with local governments, public opinion, and political pressure, and get delayed or scaled back more often than the contract language alone would suggest. Korean toll-road and light-rail projects have repeatedly settled for smaller fee increases than originally planned after public backlash. That gap — contractually predictable on paper, politically exposed in practice — is the defining tension of this asset class.

## Infrastructure Funds vs. REITs: User Fees, Not Rent

Because both promise stable, income-driven payouts, infrastructure funds are often compared to REITs, covered in [the lesson on REIT FFO valuation](/en/basics/reit-ffo-valuation/). The resemblance is real, but the two generate income through fundamentally different mechanisms. A REIT's income is rent paid by tenants under multi-year leases; vacancies require finding new tenants, and the value of the underlying property tracks real-estate transaction prices. An infrastructure fund's income is the sum of countless small usage fees paid by an unidentified, rotating set of users — and critically, the fee itself is often capped or set by contract rather than freely negotiated the way rent is.

The more decisive difference is capital structure and the nature of the asset itself. REITs typically invest through pure equity and own real property outright, which is in principle a permanent asset. Infrastructure funds, as described above, routinely blend equity with senior and subordinated debt — and the operating rights they hold are finite: once the concession period (often 20 to 30 years) ends, ownership reverts to the government. An infrastructure fund's portfolio is, in a sense, a depleting asset — its remaining concession life shortens every year — which means the fund has to keep adding new projects just to keep its asset base and earnings power from shrinking over time.

## A Worked Example: From Toll Revenue to Dividend

Here's a simplified, hypothetical example to trace the cash flow. Suppose a toll-road SPC collects 100 billion won a year in toll revenue. After 30 billion won in operating costs (maintenance, staff), operating profit is 70 billion won. An infrastructure fund has lent this SPC 200 billion won in senior debt (5% interest, 10 billion won a year) and 100 billion won in subordinated debt (8% interest, 8 billion won a year), with the rest of the project financed through equity. After paying the 10 billion won in senior interest and 8 billion won in subordinated interest out of the 70 billion won in operating profit, 52 billion won is left for equity holders — including the fund's own equity stake — some of which gets retained for reinvestment or debt service, with the rest flowing out as a dividend.

The key point is that the 18 billion won in interest income comes in almost regardless of moderate swings in traffic, as long as operating profit stays above that threshold — the debt tranches put a floor under the cash flow, while the equity tranche captures the upside when traffic outperforms. If traffic falls hard enough that operating profit drops below 18 billion won, the equity dividend disappears first, and in an extreme case even subordinated interest could go unpaid. This example uses simplified, hypothetical numbers; real projects set interest rates and payout ratios differently depending on the deal and the era it was structured in.

## Why Government Renegotiation Is the Biggest Variable

Looking back at Korea's public-private partnership history, the single biggest swing factor in infrastructure fund returns hasn't been traffic volume — it's been the relationship with government. After the 1997 Asian financial crisis, Korea introduced a minimum revenue guarantee (MRG) to attract private capital into public infrastructure: if actual toll revenue fell short of the level projected at the time the project was designed, the government would cover the shortfall. The policy was discontinued for new contracts around 2009, but it wasn't applied retroactively, so projects signed before then kept the guarantee. The catch is that "the government covers the losses" became politically toxic whenever operators tried to raise fares — Seoul's Line 9 subway fare-hike controversy is the case most often cited — and the backlash pushed many toll-road and light-rail projects into scaled-back fee plans or full financial restructurings (refinancing, adjusted return targets).

The lesson for investors is straightforward: however favorable a concession contract looks on paper, as long as its revenue is tied to a public-facing fee, it stays exposed to political and public-opinion pressure. The gap between the contracted rate of return and what investors actually collect is a structural, recurring risk in this asset class — far more so than typical contract risk in an ordinary manufacturing business.

## Two More Risks: Interest Rates and Concentration

Beyond government renegotiation, two other risks deserve attention. The first is interest-rate sensitivity. Infrastructure funds value their holdings by discounting decades of future cash flow back to today. As covered in [the lesson on bond duration](/en/basics/bond-duration-and-convexity/), the farther out a cash flow stream stretches, the more its present value falls when the discount rate (market interest rates) rises. The longer the remaining concession life in a fund's portfolio, the greater this rate sensitivity — which is why listed infrastructure funds often see their share prices (relative to net asset value) compress when rates rise, even though their underlying dividend story hasn't changed. The second risk is concentration. Most listed infrastructure funds hold a handful of SPCs, not dozens or hundreds — and it's common for one or two large projects to generate a disproportionate share of total income. If a traffic shortfall or renegotiation dispute hits one of those core assets, the impact on the fund's overall payout can be severe in a way that a REIT spread across hundreds of buildings would rarely experience.

## How Retail Investors Actually Access This Asset Class

There are two broad paths into infrastructure investing. The first is listed infrastructure funds — MKIF and KB Balhae Infrastructure Fund are the names most familiar in Korea — which trade on the exchange like ordinary stocks, giving retail investors easy access and liquidity. The tradeoff is that their share prices can swing with overall market sentiment and interest-rate moves, sometimes independent of what's actually happening to the underlying assets' net asset value. The second path is private infrastructure funds aimed at institutional and professional investors, where pension funds and similar large pools of capital invest directly in major assets like airports, ports, or renewable-energy projects. These are effectively locked up until maturity, but in exchange they're insulated from the exchange's short-term price swings. For most individual investors, the listed route is the realistic entry point, which makes it worth actually reading the fund's quarterly asset management reports — checking the list of underlying projects, each one's remaining concession life, and its recent history of fee adjustments — before treating the yield as a simple, stable number.

## Takeaways

- Infrastructure funds invest equity and debt — often senior and subordinated loans — in the operating rights to roads, ports, and tunnels, passing through toll and usage-fee revenue as dividends and interest.
- Inflation-indexed fee contracts give these funds a reputation for predictable, inflation-resistant cash flow, but actual fee increases are frequently delayed or reduced by political and public pressure.
- They resemble REITs but differ fundamentally: usage fees instead of rent, a blended equity-debt capital structure, and concession rights that expire rather than a permanent physical asset.
- Korea's minimum revenue guarantee controversy illustrates why government renegotiation risk is the dominant variable, on top of long-duration interest-rate sensitivity and concentration in a small number of underlying projects.

## FAQ

### Are infrastructure funds safer than REITs?
Neither is categorically safer. REITs carry lease and property-valuation risk; infrastructure funds carry usage-volume and government renegotiation risk. Because the risks stem from different sources, holding both can diversify a portfolio rather than simply duplicating the same exposure.

### Does a high dividend yield mean an infrastructure fund is safe?
No. A high yield only means the current payout is large relative to the share price — it says nothing about whether that payout will hold up. Check the renegotiation history, remaining concession life, and concentration of the underlying projects before treating the yield as durable.

### How do falling interest rates affect infrastructure funds?
Generally, falling rates raise the present value of long-dated cash flows, which tends to support these funds' valuations. That's a general tendency holding other variables constant — actual share prices move on a combination of factors, including traffic trends and any renegotiation news.
