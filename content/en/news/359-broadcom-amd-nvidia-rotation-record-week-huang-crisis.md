## What Happened

In the week ending Friday, November 13, the real story inside the U.S. semiconductor sector was more dramatic than any single index number let on. While Nvidia (NASDAQ: NVDA) fell 12.75% for the week amid a leadership crisis - its sixth straight weekly decline - rival chipmakers Broadcom (NASDAQ: AVGO) and AMD (NASDAQ: AMD) moved sharply in the opposite direction. The clearest way to see that divergence is the Broadcom-to-Nvidia price ratio, which measures Broadcom's share price relative to Nvidia's. That ratio jumped 35.4% this week - the largest weekly gain in data going back to Broadcom's 2009 Nasdaq listing, beating the previous record of nearly 33% set in the week ended December 13, 2024, right after one of Broadcom's own earnings reports.

AMD had its own wild ride over the same stretch. Shares plunged 17.3% on Thursday, November 5, after Democratic midterm election wins raised fears about restrictions on new data center construction. Just one session later, on Friday, November 6, AMD rebounded 11.04% (up $44.64) to close at $449.04, immediately clawing back a large chunk of the prior day's loss. The stock kept climbing from there as rotation flows out of Nvidia found their way into chip alternatives; some market trackers put AMD briefly topping $580 intraday on Thursday, November 12 - more than 40% above its November 5 low. Broadcom's move was smoother but still striking: shares rose 8.78% to $319.48 on Monday, November 9, then climbed further to $331.98 by Tuesday, November 10.

All of this unfolded against the backdrop of nearly a month of leadership chaos at Nvidia. On November 8, Nvidia's board announced an independent investigation into CEO Jensen Huang's personal relationships and whether he had been a target of approach by a foreign intelligence service. On November 11, The Wall Street Journal reported exclusively that the board was moving to oust him. By November 12, Nvidia confirmed Huang had resigned immediately as president, CEO, and board member, with CFO Colette Kress stepping in as interim CEO. Then on Friday, Yahoo Finance reported a third affair allegation tied to Huang, extending a story that had already dominated the week's headlines. CNBC summed up the fallout by noting that "Nvidia's turmoil is prompting a rotation across chip stocks," with investors turning toward Broadcom, AMD, and TSMC as alternative ways to stay invested in AI hardware.

## Why the Money Moved, and What the Ratio Actually Tells You

Start with what a price ratio measures. Dividing Broadcom's share price by Nvidia's isolates how much better Broadcom performed relative to Nvidia specifically - not just that the two moved in opposite directions, but by how much, stripped of whatever the broader market or sector was doing that week. That kind of relative metric is especially useful for separating sector-wide forces (interest rates, overall sentiment on the AI capital-spending theme) from company-specific ones, which in this case means Nvidia's governance crisis. Two stocks that sit in the same industry and ride the same AI theme diverging to a record degree is a strong signal that this week's chip-sector action wasn't a reassessment of the AI investment theme itself - it was money reshuffling within that theme.

There's a concrete business logic behind why investors gravitated specifically toward Broadcom and AMD. Broadcom occupies a different niche than Nvidia, building custom AI chips (ASICs) for major hyperscaler customers like Google and Meta, while AMD competes directly with Nvidia's data center GPUs through its MI-series accelerators, particularly for inference workloads. Both companies stand to benefit from the same underlying thesis - that AI chip demand remains intact - without carrying Nvidia's company-specific baggage of a CEO change and an active board investigation. Read that way, this rotation isn't a signal that conviction in AI hardware weakened; it's a signal that money holding the same conviction searched for a lower-risk way to express it.

Not every analyst takes the move entirely at face value, though. Some describe it as a rotation within the AI trade rather than a new verdict on it, pointing out that actual demand for AI chips is set by hyperscaler capital-expenditure budgets at Google, Meta, Microsoft, and Amazon - not by headlines about Nvidia's leadership drama. Crucially, none of those major customers has said it's dropping Nvidia as a supplier. In other words, this week's surge in AMD and Broadcom may reflect less about those companies' fundamentals suddenly improving and more about Nvidia-focused money temporarily parking elsewhere. Rotations driven by a specific catalyst - here, uncertainty over Nvidia's leadership - can reverse once that catalyst resolves, which makes Nvidia's November 17 third-quarter earnings report, and any subsequent news on a permanent CEO, the key events to watch for whether this divergence holds or unwinds.

## What to Take Away From This

- **Relative-performance ratios reveal sector rotation that looking at absolute stock prices alone can miss.** Tracking each stock's weekly return tells you the direction, but comparing Broadcom's price directly against Nvidia's captured something neither number alone would: that the gap hit a record. When comparing direct competitors, it's worth charting the ratio between them, not just each stock separately.
- **A rotation within a sector and a loss of conviction in that sector are two very different things.** This week's flows read far more like investors searching for lower-risk exposure to the same AI hardware theme than like broad skepticism about AI chip demand. Conflating the two can lead to the wrong conclusion about what a move actually means.
- **A governance crisis at one company can hand a direct, measurable benefit to its competitors - without those competitors' own fundamentals having changed at all.** AMD's and Broadcom's gains this week likely reflect money temporarily leaving Nvidia rather than a genuine improvement in either company's business. That money can just as easily flow back once the catalyst behind it resolves.
- **Identify who actually sets end demand before reacting to a supplier's headline risk.** Hyperscaler capital-expenditure guidance, not a chipmaker's leadership scandal, is what ultimately drives AI chip demand. Customer capex announcements are a more reliable forward signal than any single supplier's news cycle.
- **When a scheduled catalyst is coming, mark the date that will test whether a rotation is durable or temporary.** Here, Nvidia's November 17 earnings report and any further clarity on a permanent CEO are likely to be the events that determine whether this week's divergence holds or reverses.

## FAQ

### Does the Broadcom-to-Nvidia ratio hitting a record mean Broadcom is now the better investment?
Not necessarily. The ratio captures one week of relative performance, not a verdict on either company's long-term fundamentals or valuation. Nvidia's weakness is widely attributed to governance and leadership uncertainty rather than a deterioration in its underlying business, so the ratio could reverse once that uncertainty clears. It's worth comparing each company's revenue growth, margins, and customer base independently before drawing investment conclusions from a single week's ratio move.

### Does this rotation suggest the broader AI spending boom is slowing down?
Most current analysis says no. There's no sign that major hyperscalers have cut their data center investment budgets, and none has indicated it's removing Nvidia from its supply chain. The more common read is that this was a rotation within the AI hardware theme - money moving toward stocks seen as carrying less company-specific risk - rather than a sign that the broader theme itself is weakening.

### Were AMD's and Broadcom's gains driven by their own earnings or news?
This week's surge in both stocks appears tied more closely to rotation flows away from Nvidia's leadership crisis than to company-specific earnings beats or major news of their own. AMD's week was also shaped by its own sharp swing after the November 5 midterm election results raised data-center regulatory concerns, so multiple forces were at work in the chip sector simultaneously rather than a single clean narrative.
