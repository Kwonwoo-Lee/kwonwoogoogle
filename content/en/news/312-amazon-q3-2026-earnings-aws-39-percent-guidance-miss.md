## What Happened

After the closing bell on Thursday, October 29, Amazon (Nasdaq: AMZN) reported third-quarter earnings - the last of the week's three biggest Big Tech reports, following Microsoft and Alphabet's starkly divergent reactions the night before (see the related article below). The headline result can be summed up in one sentence: the cloud business grew at its fastest pace in five years, and the stock still fell. Amazon Web Services (AWS) revenue rose 39% year over year to roughly $46 billion, beating the roughly $45.7 billion analysts expected and marking its fastest growth rate since 2021. Despite that, shares dropped 7% to 9% in after-hours trading. The stock had already slid more than 4% during the regular session amid a broader tech selloff, so the post-earnings decline piled on top of an already rough day.

The numbers behind the disconnect tell the real story. Total third-quarter revenue came in at $201.9 billion, up 12% year over year but just short of the roughly $202.3 billion consensus. Earnings per share landed at $1.89, down from $1.95 a year earlier and below the $1.96 analysts had modeled. Operating income hit a record $28.1 billion, translating to a healthy 13.9% operating margin - a genuinely strong result that got almost no attention once investors moved on to the number that actually moved the stock: fourth-quarter guidance. Amazon guided Q4 revenue to a range of $240 billion to $247 billion, with a midpoint around $243.5 billion - slightly below the roughly $244.2 billion consensus. Coming right before the holiday shopping season, a guide that lands even marginally short of expectations reads as a warning sign to investors.

AWS's own underlying numbers, by contrast, were genuinely encouraging. The business exited the quarter at a $184 billion annualized revenue run rate, and CEO Andy Jassy told analysts on the earnings call that he expects AWS growth to stay above 40% in both 2027 and 2028 - a signal that cloud demand is accelerating, not cooling. The catch is what it costs to sustain that growth. Amazon held its 2026 capital expenditure guidance at roughly $220 billion, and that scale of spending has pushed trailing-twelve-month free cash flow into negative territory. Revenue and profit are both growing - just not as fast as the spending required to fund that growth.

## Why Strong Growth Didn't Save the Stock

First, investors react more to the gap between results and expectations than to the growth rate in isolation. A 39% AWS growth rate is excellent by almost any standard, but what the market actually wanted to see was how that strength flowed through to the company's consolidated numbers and forward guidance. When total revenue and EPS both miss and guidance comes in soft, a single strong segment usually isn't enough to carry the entire stock - especially at a conglomerate like Amazon, where retail, advertising, and cloud sit side by side and investors tend to fixate on whichever piece looks weakest.

Second, the timing of the Q4 guidance miss made it sting more than it otherwise would have. The fourth quarter, anchored by Black Friday and the holiday shopping season, is Amazon's single most important quarter of the year. Even a slightly soft revenue guide for that period raises the question of whether core consumer demand is cooling going into Amazon's biggest selling season - a worry that's separate from, and arguably more concerning than, anything happening at AWS.

Third, this earnings week as a whole was dominated by one question: is all the AI capital spending actually converting into revenue? Alphabet's selloff the night before - driven partly by a quarter that turned free-cash-flow negative under the weight of its own capex - had already primed investors to scrutinize exactly this pattern. When Amazon disclosed that it, too, was holding roughly $220 billion in annual capex while trailing-twelve-month free cash flow had turned negative, it confirmed investors' suspicion that the pattern of "revenue growth outpaced by spending growth" is showing up across Big Tech this earnings season, not just at one company.

## What to Take Away From This

- **Segment growth and consolidated guidance have to be read together.** AWS grew 39%, but the stock still fell because total revenue, EPS, and Q4 guidance all came in soft. A standout segment doesn't automatically offset weakness elsewhere in a multi-segment company.
- **Guidance for a seasonally critical quarter gets judged more harshly than guidance for an ordinary one.** Q4 carries outsized weight for Amazon's full-year results. Even a modest miss on the consensus for that specific quarter tends to get read as a signal about core demand, not just a rounding error.
- **A shift to negative free cash flow can rattle investors more than an unchanged capex plan.** Amazon didn't raise its 2026 capex guidance - it simply held it at roughly $220 billion. But the disclosure that trailing-twelve-month free cash flow had actually turned negative landed as a more concrete, immediate warning than any forward-looking spending figure could.
- **Earnings reported in the same week by comparable companies shape how each one gets read.** Alphabet's capex-driven selloff the night before primed investors to watch for the exact same pattern at Amazon - growth paired with spending that's growing even faster. When evaluating one company's results, it's worth checking how peers reporting in the same window performed and why, since investor sentiment often carries over.

## FAQ

### Why did Amazon's stock fall when AWS grew 39%?

AWS's growth was genuinely its fastest since 2021, but Amazon's consolidated revenue and EPS both missed consensus, and fourth-quarter guidance came in slightly below expectations. Investors weighted the weaker companywide numbers and guidance more heavily than the strength in a single segment.

### Why did Amazon's free cash flow turn negative?

Heavy capital spending to support AWS and broader AI infrastructure investment is the main driver. Amazon held its 2026 capex guidance at roughly $220 billion, and that spending has outpaced operating cash generation enough to push trailing-twelve-month free cash flow into negative territory.

### Why does Amazon's Q4 guidance matter so much?

The fourth quarter, which includes Black Friday and the holiday shopping season, represents the largest share of Amazon's annual revenue. Even a small guidance miss for that specific quarter tends to get interpreted as a signal about softening core consumer demand, which is why it moves the stock more than guidance misses in other quarters.

### What did CEO Andy Jassy say about AWS's growth outlook?

Jassy told analysts on the earnings call that he expects AWS growth to stay above 40% in both 2027 and 2028. AWS's annualized revenue run rate already stands at $184 billion, suggesting cloud demand is accelerating rather than slowing, even as the stock reaction on earnings night focused on other parts of the report.
