## Holding Several Products in One Account, Taxed as One Combined Result

Hold several stocks, ETFs, or funds in an ordinary brokerage account in Korea, and each one gets taxed separately. Capital gains on domestically listed shares are tax-exempt for most investors anyway, so that part rarely matters. But the moment a portfolio mixes in dividend income, gains on foreign stocks, or returns from bond or mixed funds — all of which are taxable — the picture changes. In an ordinary account, a gain on one holding gets taxed on its own, and a loss on another holding inside the same account simply disappears; it never offsets that gain. Make 4 million won on Fund A and lose 1.5 million won on Fund B, and the real net gain in your pocket is only 2.5 million won — but tax still falls on the full 4 million won gain. Korea's ISA (개인종합자산관리계좌, literally "individual comprehensive asset management account," commonly rendered as Individual Savings Account) works differently at exactly this point. Wrap multiple holdings inside one ISA, and at settlement, every gain and loss across the whole account over the full holding period gets combined first — tax applies only to the net result. The ISA itself isn't an investment product; it's a wrapper you can hold stocks, ETFs, funds, and deposits inside, and that wrapper changes how the tax bill itself gets calculated.

## How Loss-Netting Actually Plays Out — A Worked Example

Put a number on it. Say an investor splits the same amount of money evenly between a foreign-index ETF (A) and a domestic bond fund (B), running the position inside an ordinary account on one side and an ISA on the other. Over the holding period, ETF A gains 4 million won and Fund B loses 1.5 million won. In the ordinary account, tax applies to ETF A's full 4 million won gain, and Fund B's 1.5 million won loss just vanishes without offsetting anything. Inside the ISA, the two results get combined first: `4,000,000 - 1,500,000 = 2,500,000` won, and that 2.5 million won — not the 4 million won — is the figure tax rules actually apply to. Put identical money into identical holdings, and the taxable amount nearly halves purely because of which wrapper held them. The more an investor spreads money across several products inside one account, the more this netting effect tends to matter whenever some of those holdings land in the red.

## Three Account Types: Trust, Discretionary, and Brokerage-Linked

ISAs come in three operating types, and an investor picks one at sign-up. A **trust-type (신탁형)** account has the investor direct exactly what to buy and how much, with the financial institution simply executing those instructions. A **discretionary-type (일임형)** account flips that: the investor sets a general risk preference, and the institution builds and rebalances the portfolio on its own. A **brokerage-linked type (중개형)**, introduced in 2021, is the one that lets investors directly buy and sell domestically listed stocks and ETFs through a securities account, much like an ordinary trading account. The trust and discretionary types were originally built around funds and deposit-type products and couldn't hold individual stocks directly; the brokerage-linked type opened the door to picking stocks yourself while still getting the loss-netting and tax benefits. Which type you choose determines both what you can hold and whether you trade it yourself, so it's worth settling on this before signing up.

## Who Can Open One — Eligibility and Limits

An ISA isn't a wrapper anyone can open without limit. Anyone 19 or older can open one regardless of income, and those with earned income can open one from age 15. Each person, however, can generally hold only one ISA at a time. There's also an income-based gate: anyone who was subject to the comprehensive financial income tax (financial income over 20 million won in a year — covered in the [dividend income tax lesson](/en/basics/dividend-income-tax-explained/)) in any of the prior three tax years is barred from opening an ISA. That restriction reflects the policy intent behind the account: it's built to help ordinary-income investors build tax-advantaged, longer-term savings, not primarily to serve high-income or high-net-worth investors who already clear that comprehensive-tax threshold.

## Tax-Free Thresholds and Reduced-Rate Taxation — How Much Actually Escapes Tax

Loss-netting doesn't mean the whole net gain goes untaxed. At settlement — typically after a minimum three-year holding period — only a portion of the net gain is tax-free, with the excess taxed at a reduced rate rather than the standard one. The general type (일반형) shields up to 5 million won of net gain from tax; the lower-income and farming/fishing-household types (서민형·농어민형) shield up to 10 million won. Anything above that threshold is taxed at a reduced 9.9% rate instead of the standard 15.4% rate that would otherwise apply to interest and dividend income. In other words, an ISA is less a "tax-free account" and more an account with both a tax-free band and a reduced-rate band stacked on top of each other. Worth flagging: as of this writing, Korean media have reported proposals under discussion as part of 2026 tax-law revisions that would roughly double both the annual contribution cap (currently 20 million won) and the lifetime cap (currently 100 million won) — to roughly 40 million won and 200 million won, respectively. Whether and when that actually takes legal effect isn't settled at the time of writing, so anyone planning contributions around these limits should check the current figures directly with the National Tax Service or their brokerage before acting.

## The Pension-Conversion Bonus After Maturity

The ISA's tax benefit can extend one step further after the account matures. Transfer the maturity proceeds — or funds from an early withdrawal made after the three-year minimum holding period — into a pension account such as a 연금저축 (pension savings account) or IRP (individual retirement pension) within 60 days, and the transferred amount earns an additional tax credit worth 10% of that sum, up to a cap of 3 million won, applied in the year of the transfer. What makes this notable is that it works through a separate channel from the pension account's own annual contribution cap of 18 million won. Funds moved over from a matured ISA can be deposited into a pension account on top of that 18-million-won cap — one of the few legal ways to exceed it. Principal that didn't receive a tax credit can generally be withdrawn from the pension account relatively freely afterward, while amounts actually paid out as a pension are taxed at a reduced pension-income tax rate (roughly 3.3%-5.5%). The ISA's own annual contribution room also carries forward: unused room from one year rolls into the next, which lets some investors contribute less in early years and then front-load a larger deposit later using accumulated unused limits.

## The Minimum Holding Period — Why the Money Is Effectively Locked Up

All of this comes with a catch: the account generally needs to stay open for at least three years to unlock the full benefit. Close it before that point, and the loss-netting-based tax-free and reduced-rate treatment doesn't apply — gains accrued inside the account instead get taxed under the ordinary rules that would have applied without an ISA at all. In practical terms, an ISA is designed on the assumption that the money inside it can stay put for three years or more. Put money you might need back within a year or two into an ISA, and an early withdrawal forced by some urgent need could mean the funds sat locked up without ever delivering the tax benefit that was the whole point of opening the account. Money that genuinely can sit for three-plus years, on the other hand, gets to accumulate the loss-netting effect over that stretch — often producing a better after-tax result than running the same holdings through an ordinary account.

## What an ISA Doesn't Solve — Its Real Limits

Most explanations of the ISA lean hard on the upside, but a few limits matter before deciding to open one. First, since capital gains on domestically listed shares are already tax-exempt for most investors, the loss-netting and tax-free benefits mainly show up when a portfolio includes dividend income, foreign assets, or taxable fund returns — a portfolio that's purely domestic stocks won't feel much of the effect. Second, even the brokerage-linked type can't buy foreign stocks directly; foreign market exposure only comes indirectly, through domestically listed ETFs that track foreign indexes. Third, the three-year minimum holding period is a real constraint — better products or a change in personal financial plans during that window aren't easy to act on without giving up the tax benefit. Fourth, the one-account-per-person rule rules out any strategy of spreading ISAs across multiple institutions. Taken together, an ISA isn't a universally superior account for every investor — it's a tool that pays off most clearly for investors who hold taxable assets and can afford to lock money up for three years or longer.

## How This Compares to Similar Accounts Abroad — The Loss-Netting Distinction

Tax-advantaged account wrappers aren't a uniquely Korean idea. The US's [401(k) and IRA, and Canada's RRSP and TFSA](/en/basics/us-canada-tax-advantaged-accounts/), start from the same basic premise, and the UK runs its own account also called an "ISA" (Individual Savings Account) under the same acronym. Most of these foreign structures work by exempting gains inside the account from tax, or by granting a tax credit on contributions — benefits applied product-by-product within the wrapper. Where Korea's ISA departs from that pattern is that it doesn't stop at a tax break: it first combines the gains and losses across every holding in the account into one net figure. A loss on one holding doesn't just disappear — it reduces the taxable gain on another. That loss-netting mechanism, more than the tax-free threshold itself, is the feature that sets Korea's ISA apart from similarly named accounts elsewhere.

## Takeaway

- An ISA is a wrapper, not a product. Its defining feature is combining every holding's gains and losses inside the account over the full holding period into one net figure before tax applies — loss-netting, not just a flat tax exemption.
- Three account types exist — trust, discretionary, and brokerage-linked — and only the brokerage-linked type, introduced in 2021, lets investors directly trade domestically listed stocks and ETFs.
- Eligibility runs from age 19 (or 15 with earned income), limited to one account per person, and barred for anyone who hit the comprehensive financial income tax threshold in any of the prior three tax years.
- The general type shields up to 5 million won of net gain from tax, and the lower-income/farming-household type up to 10 million won, with any excess taxed at a reduced 9.9% rate — though contribution-cap increases are reportedly under policy discussion and should be verified against current law before acting.
- Transferring maturity proceeds into a pension account within 60 days earns an extra tax credit worth 10% of the transferred amount (capped at 3 million won) on top of the pension account's own annual contribution cap — but all of this depends on meeting the three-year minimum holding period.

## FAQ

### If I sell a losing holding partway through, does that loss still get netted?
Netting is based on the gains and losses actually realized inside the account by the time it's settled at maturity (or qualifying early withdrawal). Swapping holdings along the way doesn't change that — whatever gains and losses actually occurred inside the account by the final settlement date all get combined.

### Do I have to choose between an ISA and a pension account like an IRP?
No — they're complementary, not an either-or choice. An ISA suits medium-term money (three-plus years) held alongside taxable products, where the loss-netting benefit matters most. A pension account suits long-term retirement savings with its own annual tax credit. The ISA-to-pension-account conversion rule is specifically designed to let investors use both in sequence.

### If I already crossed the comprehensive financial income tax threshold once, am I permanently barred from opening an ISA?
No. The eligibility test looks at the prior three tax years only. A one-time spike in financial income that triggered the comprehensive tax in a given year doesn't permanently disqualify you — if income stays under the threshold for the following three tax years, eligibility returns. Confirm the exact rule in effect at the time of application with current tax law or your brokerage.
