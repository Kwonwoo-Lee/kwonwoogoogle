## What Happened

The long-running takeover battle between Italy's largest bank, UniCredit, and Germany's second-largest lender, Commerzbank, passed a significant milestone in the past few days. In late July, Germany's financial regulator BaFin confirmed that UniCredit's application to hold more than 30% of Commerzbank met the necessary documentation requirements, and referred the matter to the European Central Bank for formal review and final approval. The ECB now has up to 60 working days to complete its assessment, with markets expecting a decision sometime this autumn. UniCredit has said that, assuming the review clears, it is targeting full control of Commerzbank by the fourth quarter of 2026.

A second notable shift came on August 6, when Commerzbank reported second-quarter earnings. CEO Bettina Orlopp, who had until recently characterized UniCredit's approach as effectively hostile, struck a noticeably more conciliatory tone. In an interview with Bloomberg TV, she said: "We have started talks on this, which is in the genuine interest of both UniCredit and Commerzbank, as we are both determined to create value for our shareholders, clients and employees," adding that she was "optimistic that, step by step, we can find common ground on governance and also on the business model." At the same time, she was careful to note that "the German federal government, employee representatives, and our supervisory board have all made clear that they now expect UniCredit to engage with Commerzbank constructively" - a reminder that the start of talks doesn't mean German resistance has fully dissolved. On the same day, Commerzbank also announced a €1.2 billion share buyback program, layering a shareholder-friendly signal on top of an already news-heavy earnings day.

The market's reaction has been clear. Commerzbank shares gained 6.78% over the past seven trading sessions and are up 23.55% over the past twelve months, making it one of the stronger performers among European bank stocks. UniCredit, for its part, has built its position to an economic interest of 47.6% and voting rights of 49.65% in Commerzbank through a combination of open-market purchases, derivatives, and a tender offer that ran from May through July 3. Converting that stake into actual control, however, still requires sign-off not just from the ECB but from regulators in Poland and the United States as well - meaning several procedural hurdles remain even after the current milestone.

## Why This Deal Is Being Watched as a Test Case for European Banking

The structure of the offer itself is part of what makes this standoff so complicated. Back in May, UniCredit proposed exchanging 0.485 of its own shares for each Commerzbank share, valuing the deal at roughly €35 billion, or about €30.8 per Commerzbank share. That amounted to a premium of only about 4% over Commerzbank's prior closing price, and Commerzbank rejected the offer, saying it lacked the key terms needed to serve as a basis for negotiation and effectively offered no real premium to shareholders. One detail worth understanding is how the offer was deliberately engineered: under German takeover law, crossing the 30% ownership threshold triggers a "cliff-edge" requirement to launch a mandatory offer for all outstanding shares. UniCredit structured its bid to stay just under that line while preserving room to keep building its stake later - which is part of why the bank has publicly insisted it has "no expectation to achieve control," even as its actual holdings have crept up toward half of the company.

German political opposition has been the other defining feature of this saga. Chancellor Friedrich Merz has publicly described UniCredit's approach as "uncoordinated and unfriendly," and the federal government - which owns a 12.7% stake in Commerzbank - has opposed the takeover on the grounds that it threatens the bank's systemic importance and its role in financing Germany's Mittelstand, the small and mid-sized businesses that form the backbone of the German economy. That's why market participants increasingly view this fight as a broader test of whether the European Union's long-stated ambition for a genuine cross-border Banking Union can actually function in practice. Large cross-border bank mergers have been rare in Europe over the past decade-plus, and a big reason is precisely this dynamic: beyond the usual regulatory and antitrust review, an additional layer of national political approval can override sound economic logic entirely. If this deal ultimately closes, it would create the eurozone's largest bank by assets and stand as the most significant cross-border bank merger in Europe in more than a decade.

## What the BBVA-Sabadell Precedent Warns About the Gate That Comes After Regulatory Approval

There's a sobering recent parallel for how the UniCredit-Commerzbank standoff could still play out: Spain's BBVA and its hostile pursuit of domestic rival Banco Sabadell, launched in May 2024. That deal cleared fierce initial opposition from the Spanish government and won conditional approval from Spain's Council of Ministers in June 2025. The conditions were substantial - Madrid required Sabadell to keep operating as a separate legal entity for at least three years and barred layoffs directly tied to the merger. On the regulatory front alone, the deal looked like it was heading toward completion.

The actual outcome was the opposite. Despite clearing that major government hurdle, only 25.47% of Sabadell shareholders tendered their shares when the offer came to a vote on October 16, 2025 - far short of the roughly 50% BBVA needed to declare the deal a success. A takeover valued at close to $19 billion collapsed at the very last gate - not regulatory approval, but the actual choice made by shareholders - after clearing every government checkpoint along the way. BBVA's stock jumped 5.7% on the news as it announced an immediate resumption of share buybacks, while Sabadell shares fell 6.5%. Few recent cases illustrate more clearly that a government's political blessing and shareholders' economic judgment don't automatically point in the same direction.

The lesson for UniCredit-Commerzbank is direct: clearing BaFin and ECB approval is no guarantee the deal actually closes. The broader trend still favors more attempts like this one - cross-border bank M&A within the EU reached roughly €17 billion (about $20 billion) over the past year, up nearly fivefold from €3.4 billion the year before, signaling that European banking is entering a new consolidation phase after a long stagnant stretch. But as BBVA-Sabadell shows, a regulatory green light and an actual completed deal remain two very different things. UniCredit's 47.6% stake looks commanding on paper, but how Commerzbank's largest single shareholder - the German federal government, at 12.7% - and the remaining minority holders ultimately choose to act is a variable that stays open even after the ECB rules.

## What to Take Away From This

- **Read M&A headlines by asking "what's the next regulatory gate, and when does it open?" rather than "will this deal close?"** Deals requiring sequential approvals across multiple jurisdictions - here, BaFin, then the ECB, then Poland and the U.S. - tend to see fresh price action around each individual milestone. Marking those dates on a calendar is more useful than trying to predict the final outcome upfront.
- **A shift in management's tone is itself a signal.** Commerzbank's CEO moving from adversarial language to talk of "finding common ground" changes how the market recalculates deal odds, even well before any formal agreement is reached. Tracking the tone of official statements matters as much as tracking the raw numbers.
- **Stake percentage and actual control are two different things.** UniCredit holding a 47.6% economic interest doesn't automatically translate into the power to run the company. Understanding the gap between regulatory "control" thresholds and actual ownership stakes helps avoid drawing conclusions from headline numbers alone.
- **Political risk looms especially large in cross-border M&A.** Even an attractively priced deal with real strategic synergy can stall or collapse if the target country's government or labor unions push back hard enough. When evaluating stocks tied to foreign takeover attempts, weigh political feasibility alongside the financial case.
