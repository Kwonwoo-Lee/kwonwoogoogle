## The Same 9 Million Won Doesn't Refund the Same Amount

Every tax season, Korean financial media repeats some version of the same line: "max out your pension savings (연금저축) and IRP at 9 million won and get back 1.485 million won." That's true for some people and not for others. Cross a certain income line and the same 9 million won contribution refunds noticeably less. Keep earning income past age 55 while already drawing a pension, and the credit keeps applying. Pull the money out early for an unapproved reason, and the tax office claws back exactly what it gave. Where Korea's [ISA account](/en/basics/isa-account-korea/) cuts taxes by netting gains against losses inside one wrapper, pension savings accounts and the IRP (Individual Retirement Pension, 개인형퇴직연금) work through an entirely different mechanism: a tax credit the moment money goes in, followed by a lower tax rate — not zero — the moment it comes back out as a pension. This lesson walks through how that credit rate, the contribution caps, and the withdrawal-stage tax rules all fit together.

## Two Accounts, One Shared Benefit — Pension Savings vs. the IRP

Pension savings and the IRP earn the tax credit the same way, but they're built for different people. A **pension savings account (연금저축, usually structured as a fund or an insurance product)** can be opened by anyone, employed or not, with no restriction on what it can hold. The **IRP**, by contrast, was originally designed as a vehicle for parking severance pay, so only people with earned income — employees, the self-employed, freelancers — can open one, and it comes with an operating constraint: at most 70% of the account's total balance can sit in risk assets such as equity funds or ETFs, with the remainder required in safer holdings like deposits, insurance products, or government bonds. That constraint is why [target-date funds (TDFs)](/en/basics/target-date-fund-glide-path/), which automatically dial down risk exposure over time, are commonly used as the IRP's default holding. The two accounts aren't competing options — they're two buckets that share the same tax-credit ceiling, and most savers end up using both to fill it.

## How the Credit Actually Works — the 9 Million Won Cap and 16.5%/13.2%

A tax credit is more direct than a deduction: it subtracts a fixed percentage straight off the tax bill rather than off taxable income. Contributions to pension savings and the IRP combined are eligible for the credit up to 9 million won per year. There's a catch inside that number, though — a pension savings account on its own is only eligible up to 6 million won. To use the full 9 million won, the remaining 3 million won has to go into the IRP. Put all 9 million won into pension savings alone, and only 6 million won of it actually earns the credit.

The credit rate itself splits by income into two tiers: 16.5% (inclusive of local income tax) for anyone with total wage income of 55 million won or less (or comprehensive income of 45 million won or less), and 13.2% above that line.

| | Wage income ≤ ₩55M | Wage income > ₩55M |
|---|---|---|
| Credit rate | 16.5% | 13.2% |
| Refund on a full ₩9M contribution | ₩1,485,000 | ₩1,188,000 |

That's the opposite of how most tax breaks scale with income — lower earners get the higher percentage back, not the higher earners. That's only possible because this is a credit rather than a deduction: a deduction's value rises with the taxpayer's marginal rate, so high earners benefit more automatically, but a credit pays out a flat percentage regardless of income — and the government deliberately set the lower-income rate (16.5%) above the higher-income rate (13.2%) to tilt the benefit toward middle- and lower-income savers.

One more number worth separating from the 9 million won figure: the account's actual contribution ceiling is 18 million won a year, double the credit-eligible amount. Contributions between 9 million and 18 million won are allowed but earn no credit. Even so, that excess isn't contributed for nothing — gains inside the account, whether or not the contribution that generated them earned a credit, still grow tax-deferred until withdrawal, so pushing past the credit cap can still make sense for savers with room to spare.

## The IRP's 70% Risk-Asset Cap and Default Investment Option

The IRP's operating limit deserves a closer look on its own. At most 70% of the account's total balance — calculated across the whole account, not holding by holding — can sit in assets without principal protection, such as equity funds and ETFs; the rest must sit in safer instruments. As that 70% line is approached, further purchases of risk assets get restricted until more safe-asset balance is added. Since 2023, Korea has also required a default investment option (사전지정운용제도) for any IRP holder who hasn't given investment instructions — in practice, a life-cycle TDF that automatically lowers its risk-asset share as retirement approaches is one of the most common defaults chosen for this purpose. Pension savings accounts carry no such risk-asset ceiling; a saver can put the entire balance into equity funds or ETFs if they choose. That difference in investment freedom is worth weighing alongside the tax math when deciding how to split contributions between the two accounts.

## The Effect That Outlasts the Credit — Compounding on Deferred Tax

Fixating on the 1.485 million won refund misses the larger effect running underneath it. In an ordinary brokerage account, every realized gain or dividend gets taxed right away, shrinking the capital available for the next round of reinvestment. Inside a pension account, that same gain isn't taxed until the money is actually withdrawn as a pension — so the money that would have gone to tax stays invested and keeps compounding alongside everything else, the same mechanism covered in [why compounding matters](/en/basics/why-invest-compound-interest/). Run two otherwise identical portfolios side by side for 20 or 30 years — one where roughly 15.4% leaks out in tax each time a gain is realized, one where that tax is deferred until withdrawal — and the deferred account ends up larger, because the money that would have left each year kept compounding instead. That gap narrows somewhat once the pension is finally taxed at withdrawal, even at the reduced rate, but the upfront credit itself can also be reinvested rather than spent, stacking a second compounding effect on top of the first. The real payoff of a pension account, in other words, isn't the one-time refund — it's what that deferred tax does to the account's growth curve over decades.

## Missed the Credit This Year? Carrying It Forward

Money contributed beyond the annual cap, or a credit that wasn't claimed for some reason in a given year, isn't simply lost. Korea's tax authority allows a conversion request (전환신청) that reclassifies an already-made, uncredited contribution as if it had been made the following year, letting it count toward that year's cap instead. A saver whose income dropped unexpectedly, for instance, might skip claiming the credit in a low-income year and roll the contribution forward to claim it against a higher-income year instead. This isn't automatic — it has to be requested — so anyone who didn't max out a year's cap, or skipped filing for the credit, should check with their financial institution or the National Tax Service before the next year-end filing.

## The Credit Doesn't Stop at Age 55

A common misconception is that the credit disappears once pension payments start. What actually gates it is earned income, not age. A saver who starts drawing a pension past 55 but still has wage or business income can keep contributing and keep claiming the credit during that same period — which is why the 55-to-59 window, when many Koreans still have earned income after their main career winds down, is often singled out as a prime stretch for maximizing the credit. On the other end, someone with no income at all — a student, for instance, or someone not in paid work — can't open an IRP in the first place, though pension savings remains open to them regardless.

## Taxed on the Way Out — Five Years, Age 55, and the 15 Million Won Line

The credit at contribution doesn't mean the money escapes tax entirely — it means the tax bill is deferred and, eventually, reduced. Much like the [gross-up mechanism](/en/basics/dividend-income-tax-explained/) that adjusts for double taxation on dividends, the pension account's credit works on a "give it back now, collect it later at a lower rate" logic. Qualifying for that lower rate at withdrawal requires two conditions together: at least five years since the account was opened, and the holder being 55 or older (severance pay rolled directly into an IRP is exempt from the five-year rule and can be drawn as a pension from age 55 regardless of how long the account has existed).

Even once those conditions are met, the amount withdrawn in a given year still determines the rate. Annual pension withdrawals of 15 million won or less (counting only the portion built from credited contributions and their investment gains) are taxed at a flat, reduced rate based on age — 5.5% from 55 to 69, 4.4% from 70 to 79, and 3.3% from 80 onward, so the later the withdrawal starts, the lower the rate. Cross 15 million won in a year, and the amount above that threshold (or in some structures, the full amount) must either be combined with other income and taxed under the comprehensive income tax schedule (6.6%-49.5%) or taxed separately at a flat 16.5%, whichever works out lower for that saver. That 15 million won line applies per individual, not per household — a detail that trips people up in practice.

## The Early-Termination Trap — Giving the Credit Back

Because these accounts are built to lock up retirement savings for the long haul, closing one early reverses the benefit rather than simply forfeiting future ones. Outside the legally recognized exceptions (the holder's death, emigration, bankruptcy, a serious illness, and similar hardship cases), an ordinary early termination triggers a 16.5% miscellaneous income tax on the entire amount built from credited contributions plus whatever investment gains accrued on them. Contributions made above the 18 million won ceiling, or any portion never claimed for a credit in the first place, can generally be withdrawn without that penalty. The money inside one of these accounts isn't a single undifferentiated pool, in other words — it sits in distinct layers, and which layer a withdrawal comes from determines what tax applies.

## Where This Connects to the ISA

As covered in the lesson on [Korea's ISA account](/en/basics/isa-account-korea/), proceeds from a matured ISA moved into a pension savings account or IRP within 60 days earn an additional credit — 10% of the transferred amount, capped at 3 million won — on top of, not counted against, the 9 million won cap covered here. The two accounts aren't rival choices; the system is built so money can run through an ISA for mid-term, loss-netted growth and then roll into a pension account at maturity to pick up this longer-term tax treatment.

## Takeaway

- Pension savings and the IRP share a combined 9 million won annual tax-credit cap, but pension savings alone only counts up to 6 million won of it — the remaining 3 million won has to go through an IRP to claim the full amount.
- The credit rate splits at 55 million won in wage income: 16.5% below that line, 13.2% above it, refunding 1.485 million won or 1.188 million won respectively on a full 9 million won contribution.
- The 9 million won credit cap and the account's actual 18 million won contribution ceiling are different numbers; contributions between them earn no credit but still grow tax-deferred.
- The credit doesn't stop once pension payments begin — only earned income matters — and withdrawals of 15 million won or less a year, after five years and age 55, are taxed at a flat 3.3%-5.5% depending on age.
- An ordinary early termination reverses the benefit: it triggers a 16.5% tax on credited contributions and their gains, effectively handing back what the credit gave.

## FAQ

### Which account should I fill first, pension savings or the IRP?
The credit rate is identical either way, so the order itself doesn't change how much credit is earned. But because pension savings alone only counts toward 6 million won of the cap, reaching the full 9 million won requires putting the remaining 3 million won into an IRP regardless of sequence. Many savers also split contributions based on each account's investment flexibility — unrestricted for pension savings, capped at 70% risk assets for the IRP.

### If I don't use the full credit this year, does it carry over automatically?
No — the credit itself applies only to that year's contributions and doesn't roll forward on its own. What can roll forward is an uncredited contribution itself, through a conversion request that reclassifies it as a contribution made in a later year, so it can be claimed against that year's cap instead.

### What if the rates or thresholds change before I actually start drawing my pension?
The rates, caps, and thresholds in this lesson reflect the law as of this writing and can change with future tax reform. Anyone filing for the credit or planning a withdrawal should confirm the figures in effect at that time through Korea's National Tax Service (Hometax) or their financial institution.
