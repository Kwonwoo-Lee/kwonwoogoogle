## The Land Is Still Priced Like It's Thirty Years Ago

Every so often a listed company files a disclosure saying it has revalued a piece of real estate it owns, and the stock jumps to near the daily limit the same day — even though the company didn't buy new land or build a new plant. It just happened, concretely, in the first half of 2026. Daewon Hwasung, a company holding an industrial site in Osan, Gyeonggi Province, disclosed that an independent appraisal had revalued land carried on its books at ₩36.3 billion up to ₩91.0 billion — and its stock rose 29.98% that day. Mercury, which holds land in Incheon's Seo-gu district, disclosed a revaluation from a ₩10.0 billion book value to ₩68.5 billion, and its stock rose 9.06% the same day. In neither case did the land's real value suddenly appear out of nowhere — it had been there all along, quietly larger than what the balance sheet said. What finally changed was the number on the page. Understanding why that gap opens up in the first place, and why some companies choose to close it while most don't, requires understanding two different ways accounting can price an asset: the **cost model** and **fair-value accounting**.

## Why the Cost Model Is the Default — Conservative, but Increasingly Out of Date

When a company buys land or a building, accounting's default rule keeps the asset on the books at "what was paid for it (historical cost), minus accumulated depreciation." This is the **cost model**. A building depreciates over time, so its book value gradually shrinks, but land isn't depreciated at all — barring an impairment event, it simply stays at whatever price was paid decades ago. This has been the default for a reason: conservatism. Market prices swing daily, but the purchase price is a fixed, verifiable number, so keeping the books anchored to it minimizes subjective judgment calls. The catch is that this conservatism has a cost that compounds over time. A decades-old manufacturer or retailer that bought an industrial park or a headquarters site back in the 1980s or '90s may be sitting on land whose real market value is several times — in extreme cases, tens of times — what the books say. That gap between a company's face (its financial statements) and its real substance (market prices) is exactly where this phenomenon comes from.

## The Revaluation Model — Choosing to Catch the Books Up to Reality

The IFRS standard Korea follows for property, plant and equipment (IFRS, formerly "IAS 16") offers a way to close that gap. A company can elect, by asset class — land, buildings, and so on — to apply the **revaluation model** instead: getting an independent appraisal and resetting the book value to that fair value at a point in time. Two details matter here. First, this is a choice, not an obligation. Staying on the cost model is not a violation of any accounting rule, and in practice most Korean listed companies simply stick with it — appraisals cost money, and once a company elects the revaluation model for an asset class, it commits to periodic re-appraisals going forward. Second, and more important for investors: the increase in value from a revaluation does **not** flow through net income. It goes directly into equity, through other comprehensive income, as a **revaluation surplus**. Profit doesn't appear to improve. Instead, total equity on the balance sheet grows by that amount, which directly lifts the denominator used to calculate book value per share (BPS) and, in turn, price-to-book (P/B). The company doesn't suddenly look like it's "performing better" — what shows up instead is the fact that it was already sitting on undervalued assets. Worth noting: US GAAP doesn't allow the revaluation model at all — only the cost model — so the exact same type of asset can be carried very differently on a Korean or European company's books versus a US one.

## Why Not Every 'Undervalued Asset' Stock Jumps on Revaluation

A revaluation disclosure, though, is no guarantee of a stock pop. Of 18 Korean listed companies that disclosed an asset revaluation in the first half of 2026, only 8 saw their stock price rise; 9 fell, and 1 barely moved. The reason is straightforward: if the market had already priced in some expectation of that hidden land value, the disclosure is merely confirmation of what was already assumed — not new information. If, on the other hand, the market had never paid attention to a company's quietly appreciated land, the surfacing of that gap triggers a one-time re-rating of the stock. In other words, the revaluation disclosure itself doesn't create the value — it's the moment the information asymmetry between an already-existing value and the market price gets resolved, nothing more. That's part of why companies like Lotte Chilsung, whose large Seocho-dong site in Seoul has been reported to be worth several trillion won amid ongoing redevelopment discussions, or long-time "asset-rich stock" names like Taekwang Industrial and Seobu T&D, keep coming up in investor conversations even before any formal revaluation filing — the gap itself has functioned as an investment thesis long before it shows up as an accounting entry. The key thing to remember is that owning land worth more than its book value doesn't, by itself, mean a stock is undervalued. A hidden asset is simply one factor that can widen the cushion covered in [What Is Margin of Safety?](/en/basics/margin-of-safety-explained/) — the gap between intrinsic value and market price — not a buy signal in its own right.

## The Same Choice Shows Up for Stocks, Too — Fair-Value Accounting for Equity Holdings

Land and buildings aren't the only assets where this kind of choice exists. Companies also hold shares of other companies, and a structurally similar decision governs how those are measured. A stake held below 20%, without control or significant influence — not the kind covered by [What Is the Equity Method?](/en/basics/equity-method-accounting-explained/) — must be measured at fair value (market price) every quarter. IFRS 9 opens up two different paths for where that fair-value gain or loss goes. The default is **FVTPL (Fair Value through Profit or Loss)**: swings in the held stock's market price flow straight into net income every quarter, which means a company's reported profit can swing noticeably just because a stock it holds moved. Alternatively, at the moment of initial acquisition — and only then, as an **irrevocable** choice — a company can elect **FVOCI (Fair Value through Other Comprehensive Income)** for that specific holding. Under this election, the fair-value gain or loss bypasses net income entirely and goes straight into equity, as accumulated other comprehensive income. And here's the rule that matters most: even when a company eventually sells a stock classified as FVOCI, the accumulated gain is never reclassified into net income at the point of sale. Under IFRS 9, that's a deliberate, permanent rule — gains on FVOCI equity simply don't recycle to profit the way gains on some FVOCI debt instruments do. Only dividends received along the way ever touch net income. In other words, the moment a company elects FVOCI, it's choosing to let that stock's price gains forever show up only as a larger balance sheet — never as reported "performance."

## A Real Case That Shows the Weight of This Choice — Samsung Fire & Marine and Its Samsung Electronics Stake

How much this single accounting election can move a set of financial statements is clear in Samsung Fire & Marine Insurance's case. The insurer holds roughly a 1.31% stake in Samsung Electronics, classified as FVOCI. At the end of 2025, the accumulated FVOCI valuation gain on that stake stood at ₩7.6957 trillion — up 125% year over year — which pushed the company's core capital up 34% year over year to ₩19.0293 trillion. Over that same period, though, Samsung Fire & Marine's standalone net income actually fell 17%, to ₩1.6909 trillion. At a glance this looks contradictory — equity surging while profit shrinks — but they are simply two different numbers answering two different questions. The equity increase reflects Samsung Electronics' rising share price feeding an accumulated FVOCI gain; the profit decline reflects something else entirely — the performance of the insurer's actual underwriting and investment business. Reading total equity alone and concluding "the company is making more money" would be a mistake: in this case, a held stock's market price lifted the balance sheet, which has nothing to do with the strength of the core business. In a related wrinkle, Samsung Life's roughly 15.43% stake in Samsung Fire & Marine is also classified as FVOCI — and it has been the subject of ongoing debate over whether that classification actually understates the real relationship, given that board representation and other forms of influence could arguably push it past the threshold for the equity method or something closer to it. It's a useful illustration that the line between FVTPL/FVOCI classification and equity-method treatment isn't drawn mechanically by an ownership percentage alone.

## How to Check This Yourself in a Company's Financial Statements

Once the mechanism is clear, knowing what to look for becomes straightforward. Start with **accumulated other comprehensive income (AOCI)** on the balance sheet. A steadily growing AOCI balance is a signal that gains are building up either in FVOCI-classified equity holdings or in revalued property under the revaluation model. Next, check the footnotes in the annual report. The property, plant and equipment note states whether the company uses the cost model or revaluation model — and if revalued, when and by which appraiser — while the financial-instruments note shows how held equity stakes are split between FVTPL and FVOCI, along with the size of the valuation gain or loss on each. One timely detail worth tracking: starting with fiscal year 2026 annual reports, companies still using the cost model will be required to disclose the government-assessed land value (공시지가) for their land holdings in the footnotes — which should make it considerably easier for ordinary investors to compare book value against a real-world benchmark going forward. Finally, when a company's net income and total equity are growing at very different rates, it's worth asking whether that gap reflects the core business or an asset revaluation effect — both numbers describe "the company's value," but they're answering different questions. As covered in [PER, PBR, and PSR: Picking the Right Valuation Multiple](/en/basics/valuation-multiples-per-pbr-psr/), price-to-book divides share price by book value per share — and if that denominator is inflated by a revaluation surplus or an FVOCI gain, a P/B of 1.0x can mean something quite different from one company to another, even at the exact same multiple.

## Key Takeaways

- Property, plant and equipment is carried by default at historical cost minus depreciation (the cost model), but a company can elect, by asset class, the revaluation model — resetting land or buildings to fair value, with the gain flowing directly into equity as a revaluation surplus rather than into net income.
- A revaluation disclosure doesn't guarantee a stock will rally — the reaction depends on whether the market had already priced in that hidden value.
- Equity stakes held below 20% must be measured at fair value, but whether the resulting gains or losses hit net income (FVTPL, the default) or bypass it entirely into equity (FVOCI, an irrevocable election made only at initial acquisition) completely changes what the financial statements show.
- Gains on FVOCI-classified stock are never reclassified into net income even when the stock is sold — only dividends received along the way touch profit.
- Checking accumulated other comprehensive income on the balance sheet alongside the revaluation and fair-value classification notes lets an investor separate genuine earnings growth from an asset-revaluation effect.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does electing the revaluation model also increase a company's tax bill?
The increase in book value from a revaluation isn't, by itself, taxable income. Corporate tax is generally triggered only when the asset is actually sold and a realized gain is confirmed, so at the moment of revaluation, what grows is accounting equity — not an immediate tax liability. That said, the specific tax treatment can vary by asset type and jurisdiction, so company-specific cases are worth confirming with a tax professional.

### Does an FVOCI gain on a stock never show up in the company's performance at all?
It's true that the accumulated gain is never reclassified into net income, even when the stock is eventually sold. But dividends received while holding the stake do hit net income every period, and the larger equity base keeps showing up in other metrics like BPS and P/B. The more accurate way to think about it: it doesn't show up in net income, but it never stops showing up in the company's overall value.

### Can an ordinary investor realistically spot these hidden assets in advance?
To some extent, yes — by reading the property and financial-instruments footnotes in the annual report directly. But pinning down a piece of land's real market value to appraisal-level precision is genuinely difficult for an outsider. Government-assessed land values or nearby transaction prices can serve as rough reference points, but they don't necessarily match what the asset would actually fetch in a sale.
