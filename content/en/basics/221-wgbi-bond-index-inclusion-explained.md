## Three Days, 4.4 Trillion Won: What Happened in April 2026

In early April 2026, something unusual showed up in Korea's government bond market. Over just three trading days, foreign investors bought a net 4.4 trillion won worth of Korean treasury bonds. Nobody suddenly got more bullish on the Korean economy that week. This wasn't a judgment call at all — it was closer to a compliance obligation. That was the date Korean government bonds started being phased into FTSE Russell's **World Government Bond Index (WGBI)**. Funds that track that index don't get to decide whether they like Korean bonds; once the index assigns Korea a weight, they're required to hold that weight, full stop. No analysis, no persuasion needed — inclusion in the index converts directly into buy orders. This lesson covers what WGBI actually is, the bar a country has to clear to get in, what Korea changed to clear it, and what actually happens to yields and the currency once the buying starts.

## What WGBI Is — One of Three Major Global Bond Benchmarks

WGBI is a global government bond benchmark that FTSE Russell, the UK-based index provider, has published since 1986. It pools sovereign bonds issued by major governments, denominated in their own currencies, and weights each country by the outstanding size of its bond market. Alongside WGBI, the bond world treats Bloomberg's Global Aggregate Index and JPMorgan's GBI-EM (an emerging-market sovereign bond index) as the other two major global benchmarks. If [MSCI's developed-market classification](/en/basics/msci-developed-market-classification-korea/) decides how the world's equity passive money gets allocated by country, WGBI plays the equivalent role for bonds. The pool of pension funds, central banks, insurers, and asset managers that track or benchmark against WGBI is typically estimated in the trillions of dollars, and the combined government bond markets it covers add up to well over $30 trillion outstanding. Getting onto that list, in other words, is the dividing line between being a default holding for the largest pools of fixed-income capital on earth and simply not existing on their radar.

## The Bar to Clear — Market Size and "Accessibility Level 2"

FTSE Russell's review has two distinct tracks. One is quantitative: a country's government bond market needs to clear minimum thresholds for issuance size and trading liquidity. The other is qualitative — market accessibility — and FTSE Russell scores every country's bond market on a scale of 0, 1, or 2. Only markets rated the top tier, Level 2, are even eligible for WGBI inclusion. Level 2 essentially asks whether a foreign investor can trade and settle that country's bonds on terms that are practically equivalent to a domestic investor's. The review happens twice a year, in March and September; a country that falls short typically lands on a watch list first and gets re-evaluated at the next cycle. The framework echoes [MSCI's three-pillar approach](/en/basics/msci-developed-market-classification-korea/) to classifying equity markets — development level, size, and accessibility — but the specific checklist differs because the underlying asset is government debt, not equities. What matters here is currency-conversion and settlement infrastructure, the tax treatment of non-resident bondholders, and whether trades can clear through international central securities depositories rather than requiring a purely domestic setup.

Korea landed on the watch list in September 2022, but repeatedly fell short of the Level 2 bar in subsequent reviews. The breakthrough came at the October 2024 review, when FTSE Russell formally confirmed Korea's inclusion.

## What Korea Changed — Tax Exemptions, a Scrapped Registration System, and a Link to Euroclear

Three reforms did most of the work in getting Korea to Level 2. First, starting in January 2023, the government exempted foreign investors from tax on interest and capital-gains income earned from Korean treasury bonds and monetary stabilization bonds. Before that, investors from countries without a tax treaty faced a withholding tax, which made the real return on Korean bonds harder to calculate upfront. Second, in December 2023, Korea abolished the Investor Registration Certificate (IRC) system, which had previously required foreign investors to obtain a separate ID number before they could trade Korean bonds at all — a procedural hurdle that global asset managers had flagged for years as a deterrent. Third, in June 2024, Korea opened an integrated government bond account system that lets investors trade and settle Korean treasury bonds offshore through international central securities depositories such as Euroclear and Clearstream — the same settlement rails many global funds already use for other markets, without needing to set up a separate domestic account. On top of those three, Korea also extended onshore FX trading hours, from 9 a.m.–3:30 p.m. to running until 2 a.m. the next day, widening the overlap with London and New York trading sessions. Together, those four changes were enough for FTSE Russell to finally rate Korea's accessibility at Level 2.

## What Inclusion Actually Does — Mechanical Buying by Passive Funds

When the inclusion was announced in October 2024, Korea's expected WGBI weight was estimated at roughly 2.1%, the ninth-largest among all member countries. The original plan called for a quarterly ramp-up starting November 2025 over the following year. In April 2025, though, FTSE Russell revised that timeline after hearing from global investors, and the actual phase-in ran instead from April through November 2026 — eight months, with Korea's weight stepped up in equal monthly increments. As covered in [the index inclusion/exclusion effect](/en/basics/index-rebalancing-effect/), a fund that fully replicates an index has to hold whatever weight the index assigns, regardless of its own manager's view. The same logic applies to WGBI: funds tracking it had to mechanically buy more Korean treasury bonds every month as Korea's weight increased. The Korean government estimated this process could bring in anywhere from roughly 70 to 90 trillion won (on the order of $50–60 billion) in foreign capital — though reported estimates vary enough across sources that this should be read as a rough range rather than a fixed number. In practice, reports put net foreign buying at about 4.4 trillion won over the first three days after the phase-in began in April 2026, and around 6.9 trillion won cumulatively ten days in.

It's also not just strict index-replicating passive funds that move on this. Plenty of global active bond funds manage their portfolios against WGBI as a performance benchmark without copying it exactly, and those funds tend to trim large gaps between their own country weights and the benchmark's whenever one opens up too far. So "only passive money moves mechanically" is an oversimplification — passive funds are obligated to move, and benchmark-aware active funds tend to move too, just with more discretion about timing and size.

## The Effect on Yields and the Won — It Depends on Currency Hedging

WGBI inclusion's impact on domestic yields and the currency runs through two separate channels. The yield channel is the more straightforward one: steady buying pressure from foreign passive funds pushes bond prices up, which mechanically pushes yields down. Domestic brokerage estimates have floated a 20–50bp decline concentrated in longer-dated maturities, but that's an estimate, not a settled number — the effect is spread across eight months of phase-in and overlaps with whatever else is happening to global rates at the same time, which makes isolating the WGBI-specific piece difficult.

The currency channel comes with an extra condition attached. In principle, a foreign investor buying won-denominated bonds first needs to convert dollars into won, and that conversion demand should put downward pressure on the exchange rate (i.e., strengthen the won). But a large share of the money tracking WGBI — Japanese institutional money alone is estimated at around 30% of total WGBI-tracking assets — typically pairs its bond purchases with an [FX hedge](/en/basics/currency-hedged-vs-unhedged-etf/) that locks in the currency rate at the time of the trade. A hedged purchase doesn't generate the same real won-conversion demand that an unhedged one does, so the won-strengthening effect can end up smaller than the headline inflow number would suggest. In practical terms, the same dollar amount of inflow can move the exchange rate very differently depending on how much of it comes in hedged versus unhedged — which is exactly why the Bank of Korea's own public comments after the inclusion announcement stayed cautious, flagging "how sustained the inflow is" and "the hedge ratio" as the two things that actually determine the outcome.

## Inclusion Isn't the Finish Line — What China's Experience Shows

Before treating WGBI inclusion as an automatic, permanent win, it's worth looking at a precedent. China was added to WGBI in 2021, but foreign holdings of Chinese government bonds didn't simply climb indefinitely afterward. Once the one-time mechanical buying from the inclusion itself ran its course, other variables — a narrowing US-China rate differential, geopolitical risk — ended up mattering more, and foreign holdings flows slowed or pulled back for stretches in the following years, according to various market analyses. The lesson isn't that index inclusion doesn't matter; it's that the inflow it triggers is largely a one-time, mechanical event. Whether foreign investors keep holding a country's bonds afterward is a separate question, decided by ordinary fundamentals — relative yields, currency outlook, credit quality — not by the index membership itself. The same distinction applies to Korea: the reforms that earned WGBI inclusion are a real and durable improvement to market infrastructure, but inclusion itself doesn't guarantee that foreign capital stays in Korean bonds once the mechanical buying phase ends.

## Key Takeaways

- WGBI is FTSE Russell's global government bond benchmark. Inclusion requires clearing both a quantitative bar (market size and liquidity) and a qualitative one — "accessibility Level 2" — reviewed twice a year, every March and September.
- Korea cleared that bar through a sequence of reforms: tax-exempting foreign bondholders' income (2023), scrapping the Investor Registration Certificate system (2023), and linking up with Euroclear/Clearstream settlement plus extending FX trading hours (2024) — leading to formal inclusion in October 2024 and an actual phase-in from April through November 2026.
- Passive funds tracking the index were mechanically required to buy more Korean treasury bonds each month as Korea's weight increased, with the government estimating total inflows of roughly 70–90 trillion won.
- Both the yield-decline and won-strengthening effects are estimates, not certainties — and the currency effect in particular depends heavily on how much of the inflow comes in FX-hedged. China's post-2021 experience shows that index inclusion doesn't guarantee inflows keep growing afterward.

## FAQ

### Is WGBI inclusion the same thing as MSCI developed-market inclusion?
No. Both work on the same underlying logic — get into the index, and passive money is mechanically required to buy — but [MSCI's developed-market index](/en/basics/msci-developed-market-classification-korea/) evaluates equity markets, while WGBI evaluates government bond markets. Korea has been added to WGBI but still hasn't been added to the MSCI developed-market index, which shows the two reviews don't necessarily move in lockstep.

### Does WGBI inclusion guarantee lower bond yields?
The direction is widely expected to lean toward lower yields (higher prices), but the magnitude is hard to pin down precisely. Because the inclusion was phased in over eight months and overlapped with broader global rate movements, brokerage estimates for the effect vary across a fairly wide range.

### If the inflow is FX-hedged, does it have zero effect on the exchange rate?
Not exactly zero — the hedging transaction itself can still generate some activity in the FX market. But a higher share of hedged (versus unhedged) inflow generally means the actual won-strengthening effect ends up smaller than what you'd estimate just by looking at the headline inflow figure alone.
