## The Buyback Was Never the End of the Story

As covered in [stock splits and buybacks](/en/basics/stock-splits-and-buybacks/), a buyback reduces the share count and mechanically lifts earnings per share (EPS), which is why it's usually announced as "shareholder-friendly." But in the Korean market, buybacks carried an uncomfortable asterisk for years. A company could announce it had spent hundreds of billions of won buying back its own stock, and the shares would simply sit in the corporate vault rather than being destroyed. Years later, that same stock would often resurface for an entirely different purpose — a takeover defense, or a share swap between affiliated companies. This pattern earned Korean treasury stock a cynical nickname among foreign institutional investors: a controlling shareholder's "rainy-day fund." A set of Commercial Act amendments finalized across 2026 answered the obvious question — "if you bought it back, why aren't you destroying it?" — with a legal mandate. As a rule, treasury stock must now be retired within a year of being acquired.

## What Changed: Retirement Within a Year Is Now the Default

The core of the amended Commercial Act (Article 341-4 and the proviso to Article 343, Paragraph 1) is straightforward: once a company acquires its own shares, it must retire them by board resolution within one year of the acquisition date, as a matter of default. Previously, a company could essentially choose freely whether to retire, hold indefinitely, or resell treasury stock to a third party. The amendment flips that default: retirement is now the baseline, and holding the shares requires the company to demonstrate a specific legal justification and go through a formal process. The law was promulgated and took effect in early March 2026, and the one-year clock applies immediately to any treasury stock acquired from that point forward. It also applies retroactively to stock the company already held before the law took effect — but to soften the shock of an immediate deadline, the clock for existing holdings only starts running six months after the law's effective date, giving companies a grace period of up to a year and a half in total.

The policy motivation is explicit. The amendment is tied directly to the current government's "KOSPI 5000" pledge, and framed as a legal fix for the problem covered in [the Korea discount and the Value-up Program](/en/basics/korea-discount-value-up-program/): shareholder-return promises that stayed promises and never turned into action. Unlike the Value-up Program, which relies on voluntary participation, this amendment makes retirement a legal obligation rather than a choice.

## Why "Holding" Was the Real Problem

To understand why this mattered, it helps to see why a buyback and a retirement are fundamentally different events. The moment a company buys back its own shares, voting rights on those shares are immediately suspended — the EPS boost comes purely from shrinking the denominator, and the market usually reads the announcement as good news. The catch is what happens next: if the company resells those treasury shares to a third party, the suspended voting rights instantly come back to life. As covered in [the magic of treasury stock](/en/basics/treasury-stock-magic-explained/), this "resurrection" of voting power has been used to expand a controlling family's control during spinoffs, and more broadly as an [anti-takeover defense](/en/basics/anti-takeover-defenses-explained/) — handing treasury shares to a friendly third party (a "white knight") to instantly manufacture votes on management's side during a proxy fight. Treasury stock, in other words, has always had a split identity: shares bought "for shareholders" and a reserve card for expanding control whenever it was convenient. From an investor's perspective, there was simply no way to tell from a buyback announcement whether the stock would eventually be destroyed for good or come back to life as a friendly stake years later. Mandatory retirement is aimed squarely at removing that possibility of resurrection in the first place.

## Why the Numbers Behave Differently

The difference between buying back and retiring becomes concrete with numbers. A company earning 10 billion won in net income with 10 million shares outstanding has EPS of 1,000 won. If it buys back 1 million shares (10%), the shares counted as outstanding drop to 9 million and EPS immediately rises to roughly 1,111 won. But at this stage, the EPS improvement comes from a shrinking *float*, not a shrinking share *count* — if the company later resells those shares, the float bounces back to 10 million and the EPS gain disappears with it. Retire the same 1 million shares instead, and the total share count permanently drops to 9 million; there's no stock left to resell, so the EPS improvement becomes a structural change that can't be undone. Under Korean corporate law, this kind of retirement is generally designed to be funded out of distributable profit and executed by board resolution alone, which lets it move faster than a formal capital reduction (which changes paid-in capital and requires creditor-protection procedures). Even so, much of the EPS and book-value-per-share improvement from retirement is a mechanical result of a shrinking denominator rather than the company earning anything new — and if the retired portion is only 1–2% of shares outstanding, the improvement will be correspondingly small.

## Where the Exceptions Lie

The mandate isn't absolute. The law carves out room to hold or dispose of treasury shares when there's a specific statutory reason — compensating employees through stock options, or distributing shares proportionally across existing shareholders — provided the board unanimously signs a holding-and-disposal plan and gets shareholder approval for it. Vague "business necessity" no longer suffices as justification; the bar has moved toward spelling out concrete reasons in the articles of incorporation and clearing a special shareholder resolution. There's also a sector-specific carve-out: industries subject to foreign-ownership caps — airlines, broadcasting, and telecom among them — were given until early March 2029 to dispose of treasury shares, since selling them to foreign buyers risks breaching ownership limits unique to those sectors.

## Why This Law Arrived Now

The fact that this is aimed at the whole market, not just an accounting technicality, is clear from its backstory. The gap between Korean companies' [total shareholder yield](/en/basics/total-shareholder-yield-explained/) and the global average has been a recurring complaint in the Korea-discount debate, and buying back stock without retiring it was often cited as the textbook example of that gap. At the same time, the fact that treasury stock could double as a takeover shield was a point where controlling shareholders' interests and minority shareholders' interests diverged sharply. Since the amendment passed, reports suggest retirement announcements by listed companies have jumped noticeably — some reports describe the number of companies announcing retirements more than doubling within a single month. Some of that is straightforward compliance; some of it looks like companies rushing to restructure their defenses before the white-knight playbook closes off entirely. Industry groups have pushed back, arguing the law effectively removes a tool for responding to hostile takeover attempts, while minority shareholders and foreign institutional investors tend to view it as simply redirecting treasury stock back to its stated purpose. That disagreement is itself the clearest sign that this is a rule about who controls the company, not just how it's booked. It's also not a uniquely Korean experiment: many large U.S. companies retire the bulk of their buybacks fairly quickly and don't let treasury stock accumulate beyond what's needed for compensation plans, while Japan has faced similar criticism for letting treasury stock pile up unretired — and the Tokyo Stock Exchange has only recently started pushing harder on retirement as part of a broader capital-efficiency campaign. Korea's version goes a step further by making retirement a legal obligation rather than a recommendation.

## What Investors Should Actually Check

The question worth asking about treasury stock disclosures has shifted. It used to be "did this company actually retire what it said it bought back?" Now that retirement is the default, the more useful question is "under what exception is this company choosing *not* to retire shares, and how much?" It's worth checking whether a holding-and-disposal plan has cleared shareholder approval, whether the stated reason is routine (employee compensation) or one that might raise control-related concerns, and tracking which companies are still sitting inside the grace period with large pre-existing treasury-stock balances — those are the likely candidates for a large retirement announcement over the next year or two. Still, a retirement announcement by itself isn't a buy signal: the EPS lift it produces is mechanical and unrelated to actual earnings growth, so the company's underlying profitability and cash flow still need to be judged separately. Governance-wise, there's a new angle to watch too — companies with low controlling-shareholder ownership and large treasury-stock balances are losing a defensive card they used to be able to play in a crisis, which some analysts argue makes them more exposed to tender offers or control contests going forward, and potentially more interesting to the market for exactly that reason.

## Key Takeaways

- Under Korea's 2026 Commercial Act amendment, treasury stock a company acquires must, as a rule, be retired by board resolution within one year; holding it instead requires a statutory exception and shareholder approval.
- A buyback alone only suspends voting rights, which come back to life the moment the shares are resold — a loophole that let treasury stock serve as a takeover defense or friendly-stake tool. Retirement permanently shrinks the share count and removes that possibility.
- The EPS and book-value improvement from retirement is a mechanical effect of a shrinking denominator, not real earnings growth, and is proportionally small when the retired stake is small.
- Foreign-ownership-regulated industries have until March 2029, and pre-existing holdings get up to 18 months of grace — so it's worth checking exactly which exception and approval process a company's holding-and-disposal plan relies on.

## FAQ

### What happens to treasury stock a company already held before this law took effect?
It's covered retroactively, but to avoid an abrupt deadline, the one-year retirement clock for pre-existing holdings only starts six months after the law's effective date — giving companies up to 18 months in total.

### Does retiring treasury stock pay cash to shareholders?
No. Unlike a dividend, retirement doesn't distribute cash directly. It only produces an indirect effect: each remaining shareholder's ownership percentage, EPS, and book value per share rise because the total share count has shrunk.

### Does this rule apply equally to every listed company?
The principle applies broadly, but companies with a board-approved, shareholder-ratified holding-and-disposal plan under a recognized statutory exception — or those in foreign-ownership-regulated sectors like airlines, broadcasting, and telecom — get separate exceptions or extended grace periods.

### Does this apply to unlisted companies too?
The statutory text applies to stock companies generally, listed or not. In practice, though, unlisted companies face far less disclosure and market scrutiny, so verifying actual compliance from the outside is harder, and enforcement in practice may differ from how it plays out at listed companies.
