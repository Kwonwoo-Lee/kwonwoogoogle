## Indices Up, Apple Down

The same week produced two opposite pictures. When the Federal Reserve decided to hold interest rates steady, the Dow Jones rose 1.2%, the S&P 500 climbed 1.7%, and the Nasdaq jumped 2.8% — all three major U.S. indices higher, led by Microsoft and semiconductor stocks.

Apple went the opposite direction. After reporting fiscal Q3 earnings, its stock fell 7.35% in a single day, wiping out roughly $358 billion in market value. What makes this striking is that the earnings report itself was, on paper, a **beat** — revenue hit an all-time record of $109.4 billion, and the stock still cratered.

## "Good Earnings, So Why Did It Fall": The Tariff Refund Catch

The key to this apparent contradiction is looking at **where** the earnings beat actually came from. It turned out that most of the earnings-per-share (EPS) beat over Wall Street's expectations came from **tariff refunds**. Specifically, tariff refunds contributed $0.11 per share — about 85% of the total 13-cent beat.

In other words, this wasn't a case of "the core business outperforming" — **a one-time refund inflated the headline number far more than underlying operations did.** This is exactly why professional investors never stop at the headline revenue/earnings figures — they always ask "where, specifically, did this number come from."

## The Guidance Hurt More Than the Numbers

What actually rattled the market more than the current quarter's numbers was the forward outlook.

- **Gross margin guidance lowered**: down to 47%-48% from 50.1% the prior quarter, partly reflecting fading tariff-related benefits.
- **Next-quarter revenue growth guidance**: set at 9%-11%, missing Wall Street's forecast of 12%.
- **Weak Greater China revenue**: $18.8 billion, short of the $19.5 billion analysts expected.
- Concerns over supply constraints and slowing Services growth compounded the selloff.

## What to Take Away From This

- **"Beat earnings" doesn't automatically mean "stock goes up."** Apple's case shows precisely how a company can post record revenue and still see its stock crater — because markets react far more to **forward guidance** than to backward-looking results.
- **Always separate one-time gains from sustainable earnings.** Whenever an earnings beat includes a one-off item like a tariff refund, you need to check separately what the "real" performance looks like once that item is stripped out.
- **Rate policy and individual stocks don't move in lockstep.** Even when the Fed sends a broadly favorable signal to the whole market by holding rates, a company-specific issue (here, tariffs and guidance) can drag a single stock in the exact opposite direction. That's exactly why you can't judge an index and an individual stock by the same yardstick.
