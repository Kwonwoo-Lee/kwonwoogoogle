## What Happened

On Thursday, October 29, Alphabet (Nasdaq: GOOGL) shares fell roughly 12% during the regular trading session (reports ranged from 11.9% to 12.6%), closing around $283. The one-day decline wiped out roughly $500 billion in market value, and CNBC reported it as the largest single-day drop in Alphabet's history as a public company, surpassing the 11.63% decline set during the panic selling of March 2020.

The selloff traces back to third-quarter earnings released after the close the previous evening, Wednesday, October 28. Alphabet reported revenue of $127.6 billion, up 25% year over year - a solid growth number on its face, but one that still fell about $330 million to $620 million short of the roughly $128 billion consensus. The bigger surprise was earnings per share: EPS came in at $2.12, down roughly 26% year over year and well below the roughly $3.01 analysts expected. Much of that EPS miss traced to an $8.7 billion to $8.9 billion markdown on Alphabet's equity stake in SpaceX - a non-cash charge that alone shaved an estimated $0.55 off EPS. Shares initially fell about 9% in after-hours trading following the release - the same night Microsoft jumped on 48% Azure growth, a split already covered in a related article below.

The real surprise came the next day. Overnight, that 9% after-hours decline deepened into a roughly 12% drop once the regular session opened. After-hours trading is thin and illiquid, which can either exaggerate or understate a stock's true reaction; in this case, the initial move actually understated the selling pressure that followed. By the time the earnings call transcript circulated and analysts published their notes, investor attention had shifted away from the EPS miss itself and toward a heavier number: 2027 capital expenditures.

On the earnings call, CFO Anat Ashkenazi said 2027 capex would increase significantly, but declined to give a specific figure, saying more detail would come on the Q4 call. CNBC and other outlets have projected annual capex topping $200 billion in 2026 before exceeding $300 billion in 2027. Third-quarter capex alone had already reached $51.4 billion, and that spending pushed quarterly free cash flow to negative $9.1 billion. Seeing the company burn billions of dollars in a single quarter, on top of a guidance number that kept climbing, deepened investors' unease about when - or whether - that spending converts into returns. The Nasdaq Composite fell about 1.4% that day, the Dow slipped 133 points (0.25%) to 52,342.56, and the S&P 500 lost about 0.3%, as the broader market absorbed the shock.

What makes the reaction more striking is that Google Cloud itself delivered a genuinely excellent quarter. Cloud revenue surged 103% year over year to $30.7 billion, comfortably beating the roughly $29.1 billion consensus. By contrast, Alphabet's traditional cash cows lagged: Search advertising revenue (about $63.4 billion) and YouTube ad revenue (about $11.2 billion) both missed estimates. In other words, this report delivered three contradictory signals at once - a cloud business that's accelerating, a core ad business that's cooling, and a spending bill that keeps climbing faster than either.

## Why the Same Pattern Repeated Within Three Months

What stands out most is that this wasn't Alphabet's first capex-driven shock this year. Just three months earlier, on July 23, Alphabet raised its full-year 2026 capex guidance from a range of $180-190 billion to $195-205 billion at its second-quarter earnings report. That single disclosure sent the stock down 7.1% in a single day, wiping out roughly $300 billion in market value - at the time, the largest one-day loss in the company's history. In other words, Alphabet set and then broke its own "worst day ever" record twice within three months, both times triggered by a capex guidance increase. The pattern is consistent: the cloud and AI businesses keep growing at or above expectations, but the spending required to sustain that growth keeps crossing whatever line investors were previously willing to tolerate.

This repeating cycle is one of the clearest illustrations of the "AI capex fatigue" now spreading across Wall Street. Back in 2023-2024, investors largely welcomed hyperscalers' aggressive spending plans as a necessary bet to secure AI leadership. But as that spending kept getting revised upward quarter after quarter - and increasingly bit into free cash flow - the market's question shifted from "how much are you spending" to "is any of this actually coming back as revenue." The same dynamic shows up elsewhere this earnings season: Broadcom's $63 billion financing arrangement for Anthropic's AI chip purchases (see related article) and Amazon's AWS growth of 39% alongside its own newly negative free cash flow (see related article) are both variations on the same underlying tension - the growing gap between what's being poured into AI infrastructure and the cash that spending is currently generating.

## What to Take Away From This

- **After-hours reactions aren't the final verdict.** Alphabet shares fell 9% in after-hours trading right after the earnings release, then fell further to roughly 12% once the regular session opened the next day. Thin after-hours liquidity captures only a first-pass reaction; the real direction and magnitude often aren't settled until the full trading session has priced in the earnings call and follow-up analysis.
- **"Worst day ever" headlines can repeat.** The same stock set a new all-time one-day loss record twice within three months, both tied to the same theme - AI capital spending. A single sharp drop shouldn't automatically be read as a buying opportunity; it's worth asking whether the same risk could resurface again next quarter.
- **Separate non-cash charges from operating cash reality.** Much of this quarter's EPS miss came from a non-cash markdown on the SpaceX equity stake - an accounting item, not a cash outflow. The negative free cash flow, by contrast, reflects actual cash leaving the business. Both look like "bad news" headlines, but they carry very different weight for an investment decision.
- **Look at business segments separately, not just the consolidated numbers.** Google Cloud grew 103% and delivered a record quarter, while Search and YouTube advertising both cooled at the same time. Headline revenue growth alone can mask this kind of internal divergence between a company's fastest- and slowest-growing businesses.

## FAQ

### How much did Alphabet's stock actually fall?

Shares fell about 9% in after-hours trading on the night of October 28, right after the earnings release. The following day, October 29, the stock fell further during the regular session - about 12% from the prior close (reports range from 11.9% to 12.6%) - closing around $283. That was reported as exceeding the 11.63% single-day drop set during the March 2020 pandemic panic.

### Why did the stock fall more in regular trading than in after-hours trading?

After-hours trading has thin volume, so prices there tend to be rough estimates rather than settled market consensus. Over the following day, as the earnings call transcript and analyst notes circulated, investor focus shifted from the EPS miss itself toward the heavier prospect that 2027 capex would increase significantly and could top $300 billion - a shift that appears to have intensified selling once the regular session opened.

### Didn't something similar happen just three months ago?

Yes. On July 23, Alphabet raised its 2026 capex guidance to a range of $195-205 billion, and that announcement alone sent the stock down 7.1% in a single session, erasing roughly $300 billion in market value - the largest one-day loss in the company's history at that time. The October 29 decline surpassed that record for a second time within the same year.

### Google Cloud grew 103%. Why did the stock still fall?

Google Cloud's growth genuinely beat expectations. But Search and YouTube advertising revenue both missed consensus, and more importantly, the capital spending needed to sustain Cloud's growth has pushed quarterly capex above $50 billion and turned free cash flow negative. Investors weighed the company's overall cash position and future spending burden more heavily than the strength in any single segment.
