## That "Professional Investors Only" Line You Keep Running Into

Open a [CFD](/en/basics/cfd-contract-for-difference-explained/) account application and you'll be told you need to register as a professional investor first. Read the fine print on an ELS or a private fund's offering document and somewhere in it is a warning that the product "may not be suitable for retail investors." Hedge funds and private credit vehicles like BDCs are often closed to ordinary individuals from the start. Look closely enough at investing and the phrase "professional investor" keeps showing up. What exactly draws that line, why the law splits investors into two categories in the first place, and how much that split actually protects anyone — those are questions fewer people can answer than you'd expect. This lesson covers how that boundary works and why it's back under debate in Korea right now. It isn't about which products to buy; it's about how the classification system itself functions.

## The Line Korea's Capital Markets Act Draws

Korea's Financial Investment Services and Capital Markets Act splits investors into two groups based on their expertise and the scale of assets they hold — specifically, their ability to bear investment risk on their own. Those who can are professional investors; those who can't are retail investors. The reasoning is straightforward: some investment products are complex, carry no cap on losses, or run on heavy leverage, and a buyer who doesn't fully grasp that risk can end up with losses they can't undo. Rather than applying the same protections to everyone, the law exempts investors deemed capable of assessing risk and absorbing losses on their own, while applying three protective layers more strictly to everyone else. Banks, securities firms, insurers, and sufficiently large corporations are classified as professional investors by default — but the more interesting detail is that individuals can also register as professional investors if they clear a specific set of requirements. In other words, "professional investor" isn't a fixed identity assigned at birth; it's a status anyone can obtain by meeting asset, income, or expertise thresholds and going through a registration process.

## Three Protections That Apply Only to Retail Investors

Korea's Financial Consumer Protection Act (금소법) sets out three principles meant to protect retail investors, and in principle none of them apply to professional investors. First, the **suitability principle** (Article 17) requires a seller to collect information about a customer's investment goals, financial situation, and experience before recommending a product, and bars recommending anything that doesn't fit that profile. Second, the **appropriateness principle** is a lighter version that kicks in when a customer approaches a seller unprompted and asks to trade a high-risk product like a derivative on their own initiative — the seller still has to check the customer's information and warn them if the product is clearly a poor fit, but if the customer simply declines to provide that information, even this check and the explanation duty that follows it can be skipped. Third, the **duty to explain** requires the seller to walk the customer through the product's risk, structure, and costs in terms they can understand.

These three carry very different weight once a dispute reaches court. Violating the duty to explain shifts the burden onto the seller to prove it wasn't negligent, and the resulting damages get a degree of legal presumption — but a suitability-principle violation carries no such burden-shifting, leaving the investor to prove the violation, the fault, the damage, and the causal link all on their own. Being classified as a retail investor, in short, means being guaranteed the right not to be recommended an unsuitable product, the right to be warned about a risky one, and the right to have it explained in plain terms. Being classified as a professional investor means starting the trade with all three of those protections stripped away.

## How an Individual Actually Qualifies

Individuals, not just institutions, can apply to their brokerage to be designated an "individual professional investor." The requirements split into a mandatory set and an elective set. The mandatory requirement is that the investor's average month-end balance of financial investment products designated by the Financial Services Commission must have reached at least 50 million won for at least one year within the past five years, and the account used for that must be at least one year old. That balance threshold used to be 500 million won before it was cut to 50 million won in 2019 — a reminder that the bar for individual professional-investor status has loosened considerably over time. On top of the mandatory requirement, applicants need to clear one of three elective tracks: an income test (personal income of at least 100 million won, or 150 million won combined for a married couple, in the prior year), an asset test (net assets excluding the value tied to one's primary residence of at least 500 million won, combined for married couples), or a professional-credential test (holding a license such as CPA, attorney, certified tax accountant, certified appraiser, patent attorney, investment asset manager, chartered financial analyst, or financial risk manager, with at least a year working in that field). The application goes through the investor's brokerage, which reviews balance certificates, account-opening confirmation, and income documentation, typically within two to seven business days. Once granted, the designation is valid for two years and must be renewed afterward; an investor already certified by one brokerage can usually transfer that certification to another without a fresh review.

## How Other Markets Draw the Same Line — The U.S. "Accredited Investor"

Splitting investors by wealth or income isn't uniquely Korean. The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission's Regulation D uses the concept of an "accredited investor": an individual qualifies with a net worth over $1 million excluding their primary residence, or income of at least $200,000 ($300,000 combined for a married couple) in each of the prior two years with a reasonable expectation of the same this year. In 2020, the SEC added a knowledge-based path — holding a securities license such as Series 7, 65, or 82 — reflecting the same concern now surfacing in Korea: that expertise shouldn't be measured by money alone. The mechanics differ, though. The U.S. framework determines whether an investor can access products structured as private placements without a public offering process, while Korea's framework grants a distinct legal status that determines whether suitability and explanation duties apply in the first place. The EU and UK's MiFID regime, which further splits investors into "professional clients" and "eligible counterparties" with graduated protection levels, reflects a similar underlying concern. All three systems share the premise that protections can be relaxed once wealth or income clears a threshold — and in none of them has the debate over whether that premise actually holds up been fully settled.

## Did the Line Actually Protect Anyone? Lessons from Lime, the DLF Scandal, and CFDs

In practice, this boundary has been at the center of several real disputes. The [DLF (derivative-linked fund) scandal](/en/basics/dlf-derivative-linked-fund-explained/) and the [Lime Asset Management redemption freeze](/en/basics/fund-redemption-suspension-liquidity-mismatch/) both involved products structured as private placements rather than public offerings, which narrowed the scope of the suitability principle and explanation duty from the start — and both revealed bank branches selling high-risk products to ordinary individuals without adequate explanation, directly triggering a strengthening of the Financial Consumer Protection Act itself. CFDs point the other way: they were designed as a professional-investor-only product where suitability and explanation duties never applied in the first place, yet the SG Securities scandal showed that holding professional-investor status didn't guarantee the investor actually understood the risk they were taking. Both cases point to the same conclusion from opposite directions — there's always a gap between legal classification (retail or professional) and actual risk comprehension, and no system has fully closed it.

## The Current Debate: "Does Having Money Make You a Professional?"

That gap is driving a fresh round of reform discussion. Reporting indicates the number of individual professional investors has grown roughly 7.6-fold over the past five years, with average investment per person reaching a substantial figure — a direct result of the 2019 cut in the balance threshold from 500 million to 50 million won. By 2026, calls to revisit the system had grown louder. The criticism is simple: under the current rules, meeting either the income or asset test is enough, but having money and being able to actually analyze a complex product's risk are two entirely different capabilities. Repeated large losses on leveraged products like CFDs, along with disputes over private placements of overseas unlisted shares sold to Korean investors, keep getting cited as evidence for this criticism. As a result, there's ongoing pressure to add some mechanism — investor education, a risk-literacy check — that verifies knowledge and experience rather than relying on wealth and income alone, echoing the credential-based path the U.S. added in 2020. No specific reform has been finalized yet, and doing so means balancing the financial industry's interest in keeping access to higher-risk, higher-return products open against regulators' push for stronger protection. Whichever direction the system moves, reconciling "size of assets" with "actual ability to understand risk" looks set to remain a live question for a while longer.

## Key Takeaways

- Korea's Capital Markets Act splits investors into professional and retail categories based on expertise and asset scale, and the suitability principle, appropriateness principle, and duty to explain apply, in principle, only to retail investors.
- These three protections carry different legal weight: a duty-to-explain violation shifts the burden of proof to the seller, while a suitability violation leaves the investor to prove everything themselves.
- Individuals can register as professional investors by meeting a mandatory financial-balance requirement (at least 50 million won, held for a year) plus one of three elective tests — income, assets, or professional credentials.
- The DLF and Lime scandals showed what happens when suitability and explanation duties don't function properly; the CFD market showed that professional-investor status doesn't guarantee actual risk comprehension.
- With individual professional investors up roughly 7.6-fold in five years, growing criticism that "wealth alone doesn't prove expertise" is fueling an active debate over reforming the system.

## FAQ

### Does becoming a professional investor let me trade any financial product without restriction?
No. Professional-investor status means the suitability principle, appropriateness principle, and duty to explain are exempted or reduced — it doesn't automatically grant access to every product. Products like CFDs often carry their own separate eligibility or margin requirements on top of professional-investor status.

### Can a professional investor switch back to retail status?
Yes. Professional investors designated under the categories specified in the Financial Consumer Protection Act's implementing decree can request conversion to retail status, and financial firms are required to keep records of such conversions. The designation itself also expires after two years if not renewed, which ends professional-investor status automatically.

### Does qualifying as a professional investor mean you're actually a skilled investor?
Not necessarily. The current individual professional-investor requirements combine a financial-balance and account-tenure requirement with one of three elective tests — income, assets, or professional credentials — none of which directly verifies the ability to analyze a complex product's structure or risk. That gap is exactly what's driving the current debate over reforming the system.

### Do corporations also have to meet these requirements to be professional investors?
No, not in the same way. Banks, securities firms, insurers, asset managers, publicly listed companies, and sufficiently large public institutions or corporations are classified as professional investors by default under the Capital Markets Act, without the per-case asset or income verification individuals have to go through — a fundamentally different mechanism than the individual professional-investor designation.
