## What Happened

Nasdaq 100 futures opened weak and stayed that way through Monday morning, November 16, sliding roughly 0.9% in early trading before the decline deepened toward 1% as the session wore on. The timing mattered: this was the day before Nvidia (NASDAQ: NVDA) was due to report fiscal third-quarter results after Tuesday's close. Normally, pre-earnings jitters build gradually over a trading day. This time, the pressure was already visible in futures before the opening bell had even rung.

The proximate trigger wasn't anything that happened in the United States. It was a sharp overnight selloff in Asian chip stocks that had already finished trading hours before New York opened. Taiwan's benchmark TAIEX, home to the foundry and packaging companies that sit at the center of Nvidia's supply chain, sank more than 5% on the day. South Korea's market, dominated by the memory chipmakers that produce the high-bandwidth memory Nvidia's GPUs depend on, fell nearly 4% intraday. With chip-sector leaders in both markets dropping in tandem, the shock traveled straight across the Pacific into US futures pricing - a reminder that the AI chip supply chain increasingly trades as a single global risk unit rather than as separate, unrelated national markets.

The reaction was unusually sharp because of where Nvidia already stood heading into this week. The stock closed at $162.85 on Friday, November 13, after HSBC slashed its price target from $375 to $170 and downgraded the shares to Hold - a close that pushed Nvidia's market capitalization below $4 trillion and extended its losing streak to six straight weeks. Layer onto that the fact that Tuesday's call will be the first full investor call since CEO Jensen Huang's abrupt resignation on November 12, and it's little wonder options markets were already pricing in a roughly 9.6% post-earnings swing - the largest implied move since 2024. Against that backdrop, a steep overnight drop across Nvidia's own supplier base read to traders less like noise and more like the market pre-positioning defensively before the number everyone is waiting on.

US-listed semiconductor funds carried that weakness into premarket trading. Assets tracking the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index, along with the SOXX and SMH chip ETFs, fell by more than the broader Nasdaq 100 futures move - consistent with the sector's usual pattern of amplifying whatever the index as a whole is doing. Nvidia shares themselves traded lower in premarket action, and AMD and Broadcom moved in the same direction. Investors broadly treated Monday as a tense holding pattern ahead of Tuesday's print, one made harder to ignore by a very concrete overnight signal from the companies that actually build the chips.

## Why Asian Chip Stocks Move US Futures Before the Opening Bell

The mechanics start with time zones. By the time US markets close for the day, Asian exchanges are still hours away from their own open, and they finish their full session long before New York traders arrive at their desks. In that overnight window, US index futures keep trading, and the single most concrete piece of "real-time news" available to those futures traders is whatever just happened in Taipei and Seoul. Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company and Korea's memory producers aren't just suppliers in the abstract - they make the advanced logic chips and high-bandwidth memory that go directly into Nvidia's products, which makes their share prices a genuine leading read on how real AI chip demand actually looks, as opposed to how it sounds in a press release. When those stocks drop sharply before Wall Street even opens, futures traders treat it as an early preview of what might be coming for Nvidia and the broader US chip complex, and they price that preview in immediately.

That link runs especially hot the day before an Nvidia earnings report. A steep drop across the supplier base doesn't just reflect volatility for its own sake - it actively resets how investors frame what Nvidia is about to say. As covered elsewhere on this site, rising memory prices and tightening supply have been rattling shares of SanDisk, Micron, and Western Digital for months, and Nvidia itself has already warned that higher memory costs could push next quarter's gross margin down to the 71%-72% range. An overnight rout in Asia's chip names lands on top of that same narrative, reinforcing - rather than introducing - concerns about costs and demand across the entire supply chain.

It's worth being clear, though, that this leading signal doesn't always point the same direction twice. Back on August 25, a selloff in US chip stocks pushed South Korea's market down as much as 3.7% intraday ahead of that quarter's Nvidia report, stirring similar anxiety. When Nvidia then beat expectations on the actual earnings day, Asian markets reversed higher almost immediately. In other words, an overnight drop in Asian chip stocks is a signal that uncertainty is elevated, not a forecast that the coming earnings report will disappoint. It's also worth remembering that overnight futures trade on thinner volume than the regular session, which tends to exaggerate price swings relative to what actually happens once the cash market opens - meaning a roughly 1% futures decline is not a guarantee that the regular session will open down by the same amount.

The rest of this week's calendar amplified the reaction further. Beyond Nvidia's Tuesday report, the Federal Reserve is set to release minutes from its October 27-28 FOMC meeting on Wednesday, November 18, and Anthropic's Nasdaq IPO is working through final pricing in the same stretch of days. With several high-stakes events compressed into a single week, a single overnight data point out of Asia carried more weight in futures pricing than it likely would have on a quieter calendar.

## What to Take Away From This

- **Checking overnight Asian chip-stock action before the US open can be a genuinely useful habit, but treat it as a measure of uncertainty, not a forecast of direction.** Asian chip markets have swung sharply ahead of past Nvidia reports only to reverse completely once the actual numbers landed.
- **Don't equate a futures move with what the regular session will do.** Overnight futures trade on thinner volume, which tends to exaggerate price swings relative to the eventual cash-market reaction. A roughly 1% futures decline is a data point, not a prediction.
- **Think of the AI chip supply chain as a single risk bloc rather than a set of unrelated tickers.** Nvidia, Taiwan's foundry and packaging companies, and Korea's memory makers trade on different exchanges but sit inside the same demand-and-supply cycle. Watching the whole chain, not just one name, gives a fuller picture before making a trade.
- **When several major catalysts land in the same week, be wary of over-weighting any single headline.** With Nvidia's earnings, Fed minutes, and Anthropic's IPO all landing within three days of each other, it can be more disciplined to keep position sizing conservative until each event actually resolves, rather than reacting hard to the first piece of news.

## FAQ

### Does a drop in Asian chip stocks mean Nvidia's earnings will disappoint?
Not necessarily. A selloff in Asian chip stocks reflects broader market anxiety about the supply chain, not a preview of Nvidia's actual revenue or profit. In fact, Asian markets have sold off ahead of past Nvidia reports only to rally immediately once Nvidia beat expectations on the actual print.

### Will this futures decline carry through to the regular trading session?
Not necessarily. Overnight futures trade on much thinner volume than the regular session, which tends to exaggerate price moves relative to what eventually happens once trading volume picks back up during market hours. The regular session can open calmer than futures implied, or shift direction entirely on new headlines.

### With so much happening this week, what should investors watch first?
Three distinct catalysts land within three days of each other: Nvidia's earnings on November 17 (watch the gross-margin trend and any commentary tied to the leadership transition), the FOMC minutes on November 18, and Anthropic's IPO pricing in the same window. Because each carries a different kind of uncertainty - corporate earnings, monetary policy, and new-issue demand - it's worth tracking them as separate events rather than assuming one outcome determines the others.
