## My Index Fund Beat a 'Flat' Benchmark — What Happened?

You've probably seen headlines like "the S&P 500 barely moved over the past decade" or "this market has gone nowhere since 2015." Yet plenty of investors who held a plain index fund tracking that same market over the same stretch ended up ahead of what the headline number implied. Both are supposedly following "the index," so why the gap? The answer isn't that the fund did anything clever. It's that the number quoted in the news and the number the fund actually compares itself to are two different kinds of index entirely. One counts price moves only — the **price return index**. The other adds back dividends on top of price moves — the **total return index (TR index)**. This lesson covers what separates them, how large that gap actually gets, and why index funds and ETFs quietly benchmark against the TR version rather than the one in the headlines.

## The Price Index: It Only Counts What the Price Does

The S&P 500, the Dow, the Nasdaq Composite, Korea's KOSPI — the numbers reported daily in financial media are, by default, **price return indices**. As covered in [What Is Market Capitalization?](/en/basics/market-capitalization/), an index like this is built from the combined market value of its constituents relative to a base period, scaled to a reference value. That calculation captures one thing: how much the stock prices themselves moved. It says nothing about cash dividends a company pays out along the way. The moment a dividend lands in your account, your actual wealth goes up — but the price index keeps calculating as if that payment never happened. So a price index that looks flat for a decade can still have sat under a market that, once dividends are counted, generated a real, positive return for anyone actually holding the shares. A price index measures "how far did prices move," not "how much did an investor actually make."

## The Total Return Index: Assume Every Dividend Gets Reinvested

To fill that gap, index providers publish a parallel version using the exact same constituents, calculated differently: the **total return index (TR)**. A TR index assumes that every cash dividend paid by a constituent is reinvested back into that same stock on the payment date, and recalculates the index as if each holding's weight grew by that amount. S&P publishes an "S&P 500 Total Return Index" alongside the standard (price) S&P 500. Korea's exchange operator has done the same: since 2018 it expanded TR-index publication to KOSPI, KOSDAQ, KRX300, KOSDAQ150 and dividend indices, and in 2020 it added 11 sector-level KOSPI 200 total return indices. The widely used KOSPI 200 TR index specifically assumes pre-tax cash dividends from KOSPI 200 constituents are reinvested, then adds that dividend yield on top of the price change. The two index versions aren't tracking different baskets of stocks — they're the same basket, calculated two different ways depending on whether dividends are counted or thrown away.

## The Gap Compounds — Small Per Year, Large Over Decades

The scale of the difference becomes clear once you put numbers on it. Over a single year, the gap is modest: in 2021, the S&P 500's price return was 26.9%, versus 28.7% on a total return basis — about 2 percentage points. Stretch the window to 1999 through 2023, and the price index's cumulative return comes to roughly 282.6%, while the total return index's cumulative return comes to roughly 508.3% — nearly double. Go back even further, to 1925, and the annualized price return sits around 6.4% versus 10.3% annualized on a total return basis. The mechanism is simple: even a dividend yield of just a couple of percent per year, reinvested and compounded across decades, snowballs into a very different final number than the same yield paid out and never counted. This is the same compounding logic covered in [Why Invest? Compound Interest Explained](/en/basics/why-invest-compound-interest/), just operating inside the index calculation itself rather than in a savings account. The direction holds in lower-yielding markets too — Korea's KOSPI and KOSPI 200 have historically shown a persistent, widening gap between the price and TR versions once the comparison window stretches past a few years.

## Why Funds and ETFs Benchmark Against the TR Version

This is where confusion sets in. The number quoted daily in the news is the price index, but most index funds and ETFs tracking, say, the KOSPI 200 actually measure their own performance against the KOSPI 200 **TR** index, not the plain price version. The reason is straightforward: a fund doesn't ignore the dividends its holdings pay — it either reinvests that cash or distributes it to shareholders. Either way, that cash flow is real and shows up in the fund's return. Comparing that return against a price index that never counted dividends in the first place would make the fund look like it's beating its benchmark through skill, when it's really just collecting dividends the price index was never designed to capture. As covered in [ETF Tracking Error and Premium/Discount](/en/basics/etf-tracking-error-premium-discount/), a passive fund's job is to match its benchmark closely, not outperform it — so fund managers set the TR index as that benchmark precisely so tracking error gets measured fairly, dividends and all.

This also connects to how a fund distributes income. Some ETFs pay dividends out to holders as cash distributions; others reinvest internally without ever distributing, letting the compounding happen automatically inside the fund's net asset value. The second type mirrors a TR index cleanly — the investor doesn't have to do anything, and the compounding effect accrues on its own. With a distributing ETF, though, if you simply spend the cash distributions rather than reinvesting them, your own personal return won't compound the way the TR benchmark implies, even though the fund itself is tracking a TR index. The TR index, in other words, shows the maximum compounding available if every dividend gets put straight back to work — capturing that in practice still requires you to actually reinvest what you receive.

## The Trap: Don't Judge Long-Term Stock Returns on Price Alone

Missing this distinction leads to two mirror-image mistakes. The first: assuming your fund's manager is unusually skilled because it "beat" a price index, when in most cases a plain index fund tracking a dividend-paying market will structurally outperform the price index regardless of any stock-picking. The second, more consequential mistake: concluding from a flat price chart that "stocks haven't really gone anywhere, so a savings account would have done just as well" — a judgment that throws out every dividend paid along the way, the same cash flows covered in [Building a Long-Term Dividend Portfolio](/en/basics/dividend-portfolio-criteria/). The correct comparison always matches index type to index type: judge a fund against its TR benchmark, not the price index, and before citing "the market has returned X% over Y years" from a headline, check whether that figure is price-only or total return. This mirrors, in the opposite direction, the trap covered in [Nominal vs. Real Returns](/en/basics/nominal-vs-real-returns/): ignoring inflation makes nominal returns look better than they really are, while ignoring dividends makes a price index look worse than the real investor experience actually was. Both mistakes come from the same root cause — not checking exactly what a quoted number does and doesn't include.

## Takeaway

- Price indices (the KOSPI, KOSPI 200, S&P 500 numbers quoted daily) reflect price moves only; dividends aren't counted.
- Total return (TR) indices use the same constituents but assume pre-tax cash dividends are reinvested, adding dividend yield on top of price change.
- The gap is small year to year but compounds heavily over time — the S&P 500's 1999–2023 cumulative return was roughly 282.6% (price) versus 508.3% (total return), nearly double.
- Most index funds and ETFs actually collect dividends, so fair performance comparison requires benchmarking against the TR index, not the price index — which is exactly why fund managers set TR as the official benchmark.
- Reading "the market hasn't moved" from a price chart alone throws out decades of dividend income; always check whether a quoted return is price-only or total return before drawing conclusions.

## FAQ

### How do I check whether my index fund tracks a price index or a TR index?
Check the fund's prospectus or the ETF product page under "underlying index" or "benchmark index." If it just says "KOSPI 200" or "S&P 500," that's the price version; if it says "KOSPI 200 TR" or "S&P 500 Total Return," that's the TR version. Most newer index funds and ETFs, in Korea and globally, now benchmark against the TR version.

### Can a distributing ETF (one that pays cash dividends to holders) still benchmark against a TR index?
Yes. A TR index only assumes dividends are reinvested for calculation purposes — it says nothing about whether the fund itself distributes cash to investors or reinvests internally. Both distributing and accumulating (non-distributing) ETFs commonly use a TR index as their official benchmark for tracking-error purposes.

### Does this gap matter for low-dividend, growth-heavy indices?
Less so. The lower a market's dividend yield, the smaller the gap between its price and TR versions. The relationship flips for high-yield segments — dividend indices, REIT indices — where the price-versus-TR gap widens substantially, which is exactly why checking which version is being quoted matters even more for those asset classes.
