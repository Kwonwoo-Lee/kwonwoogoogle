## What Happened

Netflix (Nasdaq: NFLX) reports third-quarter 2026 earnings after the closing bell on Tuesday, October 20. What makes the timing notable isn't just the date - it's where the stock sits going in. Shares have slid from a 52-week high near $125 earlier this year down to the $65-$70 range, putting Netflix near its 52-week low and down more than 25% year-to-date heading into the print. That stands out sharply against a Nasdaq Composite that has repeatedly hit fresh records this year on the back of AI and chip stocks - Netflix's slump has been its own story, not the market's.

Start with the number that matters most: Netflix's own guidance. Back when it reported second-quarter results, the company guided to Q3 revenue of $12.86 billion and earnings per share of $0.82 - both below the roughly $13 billion and $0.84 Wall Street had been modeling. Analysts have since marked their models down to match, and the current consensus sits at about $12.88 billion in revenue (up 11.9% year-over-year), essentially in line with management's own number. In other words, the bar for a "beat" has already been lowered twice this year before a single Q3 number has been reported.

Netflix isn't reporting alone this week. Before Monday's open on October 19, jet-engine maker GE Aerospace (NYSE: GE) reports with consensus EPS around $2.00. The very next morning, October 20, telecom giant Verizon (NYSE: VZ, consensus EPS near $1.28, revenue near $34.8 billion) and beverage maker Coca-Cola (NYSE: KO, consensus EPS around $0.87-$0.90) report before the bell - hours ahead of Netflix's after-the-close release that same day. The week wraps up Friday, October 23, with S&P Global's flash October manufacturing and services PMI readings, making this a week where single-company earnings and macro data land almost back-to-back.

All of this follows directly on the heels of a brutal stretch for tech stocks: the Nasdaq just posted its worst week since June (-4.5%) between October 12 and 16, while the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index logged an eight-session losing streak - its longest since 2006 - amid fears over AI data-center debt tied to names like CoreWeave and Oracle. Whether this week's earnings, starting with Netflix, calm that anxiety or add to it is the next real test for the market. The Fed's next FOMC meeting, scheduled for October 28, adds another layer: investors will be watching this week's corporate results as an early read on how resilient US consumers and businesses actually are heading into that decision.

## Why the Gap Between the Stock and Wall Street's Targets Got So Wide

To understand that gap, you have to go back through two separate earnings disappointments Netflix has already delivered this year. The first came with Q1 results. Netflix had been pursuing an acquisition of Warner Bros. Discovery (WBD) through last December, but walked away from the bidding war in February after Paramount Skydance came in with a rival offer of $31 a share - roughly $111 billion for the whole company. In exchange, Netflix collected a $2.8 billion termination fee, booked as non-operating income, that inflated Q1 net income 82.7% year-over-year to $5.28 billion and pushed EPS to $1.23 - comfortably ahead of the company's own $0.76 guidance. Strip that one-time fee out, though, and underlying profitability actually fell short of the $1.34 analysts had been expecting before the news broke. Revenue of $12.25 billion, up 16.2%, was solid in its own right - but the stock still dropped by a double-digit percentage immediately after the report. It was a textbook case of the market refusing to reward a headline beat once it understood the quality of the earnings behind it.

The second, more direct hit came with Q2 results in July. This time it wasn't the quarter just reported that spooked investors - it was the Q3 guidance management issued alongside it. Shares fell as much as 9%-12% the next session on that guidance alone. Compounding the concern, Netflix also announced it would scale back its twice-a-year viewership engagement reports to just once a year starting in 2027, a move that read to many analysts as closing one of the few real-time windows investors had into how Netflix's content slate was actually performing against competitors like YouTube. Full-year revenue guidance was also narrowed from a $50.7-$51.7 billion range down to $51.0-$51.4 billion, trimming the high end of expectations.

Advertising sits at the center of the same debate. Netflix has reaffirmed its roughly $3 billion full-year 2026 ad-revenue target and says its ad-tier advertiser base has grown 70% year-over-year to more than 4,000 advertisers. On volume alone, the ad business keeps expanding. But the market is watching a different metric: pricing power - whether advertisers are willing to pay rising premiums for Netflix's audience, not just whether more of them are showing up. With content costs projected to rise roughly 10% this year, weighted toward the first half, any gap between advertising price growth and cost growth becomes a direct hit to margin.

Against that backdrop of two straight guidance-driven stumbles, Wall Street's price targets have already come down sharply. Morgan Stanley, still at Overweight, cut its target from $90 to $83 and then to $80 (as of October 8). Wedbush, still at Outperform, cut from $105 to $90 (October 16). KeyBanc cut from $92 to $85 (October 13), and Bank of America cut from $125 to $105. HSBC went further, downgrading the stock from Buy to Hold and cutting its target from $96 to $76 (September 22). Running the other way, Evercore's Mark Mahaney actually raised his Outperform target from $100 to $110 (September 14) - a sign Wall Street itself is split. After all those moves, the average target sits around $90-94 depending on which tally you use - still above the mid-October stock price in the $65-$70 range, but the most bearish calls (Wells Fargo at $57, Rosenblatt at $75) are now close to or below where the stock is actually trading. In other words, the gap isn't because targets stayed still - it's that targets have already been cut hard to reflect the two guidance misses, and the stock has simply fallen even faster and further than the cuts.

## What to Take Away From This

- **Stock prices react to forward guidance, not the quarter that just closed.** Both of Netflix's 2026 earnings drops happened even as reported revenue kept growing at double-digit rates - what moved the stock was management's outlook for the quarter ahead falling short of expectations. When reading an earnings report, the forward guidance deserves at least as much attention as the headline numbers.
- **Always strip out one-time items before judging a "beat."** The $2.8 billion termination fee inflated Q1 net income by over 80%, but that is not a repeatable operating profit. Reacting only to headline EPS or net-income growth, without checking what's driving it, can lead to a badly mistaken read on a company's real trajectory.
- **A gap between the stock price and analyst targets is a question, not a signal to buy.** The fact that the average target still sits above the current price doesn't mean the stock is cheap - it means those targets need to be checked against whether they've actually absorbed the company's two guidance misses this year.
- **Reduced disclosure has a cost, even if it isn't on the income statement.** Cutting the frequency of engagement reporting may save Netflix money and effort, but it also removes one of the few tools outside investors had to verify the company's competitive position in real time - and that kind of opacity often shows up later as a higher risk premium in the stock.
