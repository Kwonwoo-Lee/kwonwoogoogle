## Why an Airline's or Retailer's Debt Ratio Jumped Around 2019

Pull up ten years of financial statements for an airline or a large retail chain and one stretch looks strange: revenue and operating income barely move, but total assets and total liabilities both jump sharply around 2019. The company didn't suddenly borrow money to buy something, and its business model didn't change. It's still leasing the same aircraft, still renting the same stores. What changed is the accounting rule for how those lease contracts get recorded. **IFRS 16**, fully effective from 2019, pulled operating leases that used to sit entirely off the balance sheet onto it as assets and liabilities. This lesson walks through how leases used to be classified, why the new rule moves debt ratios, EBITDA and the cash flow statement all at once, and how investors should filter that shift when reading financial statements.

## The Old Split: Operating Leases vs. Finance Leases

Under the previous standard (IAS 17), companies sorted the leases they used into two buckets that were treated completely differently. A **finance lease** was one where, even though legal title stayed with the leasing company, the economics looked like ownership — the lease term covered most of the asset's useful life, or the lessee could buy the asset cheaply at the end. Finance leases were capitalized from day one: an asset and a liability both went on the balance sheet, the asset got depreciated, and the liability accrued interest.

An **operating lease**, by contrast, was treated as simply renting an asset with no substantial transfer of ownership — a typical store lease or a short-term aircraft lease. Under the old rule, the lessee just expensed the rent payment each period and recorded nothing on the balance sheet at all. That's exactly where the problem was. Two retailers running the same 100 stores — one that buys its buildings with a bank loan, one that leases the identical buildings — had nearly identical economics but wildly different balance sheets: the first showed real debt, the second looked almost debt-free. Operating leases were a textbook channel for **off-balance-sheet financing**, and critics had flagged this distortion for decades before the rule finally changed.

## IFRS 16: Erasing the Operating/Finance Distinction

IFRS 16 removed that split for lessees entirely. A company leasing an asset no longer needs to ask whether a given contract is a finance lease or an operating lease. With a couple of narrow exceptions — short-term leases of 12 months or less, and low-value asset leases (small office equipment and the like) — nearly every lease now has to go on the balance sheet as two items at once: a **right-of-use asset**, representing the value of the right to use that asset over the lease term, and a **lease liability**, the present value of the remaining lease payments owed. Crucially, this change applies only to the lessee side. Lessors — the companies leasing assets out — still classify their contracts as finance or operating leases under rules barely changed from before. That asymmetry means the exact same lease contract can look completely different depending on whether you're reading the lessee's financials or the lessor's.

## How the Right-of-Use Asset and Lease Liability Actually Move: A Worked Example

Walk through a simplified numerical case. A retailer signs a 5-year store lease, paying ₩200 million at the end of each year, with a 5% discount rate. At the start of the contract, the company calculates the present value of those five ₩200 million payments — about ₩870 million — and books that figure as both a **lease liability** and a matching **right-of-use asset**. From there, the two items evolve separately each year. The right-of-use asset is depreciated like any fixed asset, evenly over the 5-year lease term, showing up on the income statement as depreciation expense. The lease liability is reduced by splitting each ₩200 million cash payment into an **interest** portion (the opening liability balance times the 5% discount rate) and a **principal repayment** portion that pays down the liability itself.

(The figures below are simplified and hypothetical, built purely to illustrate the mechanism — they are not real company data.)

| | IAS 17 (old operating-lease treatment) | IFRS 16 (new treatment) |
|---|---|---|
| Balance sheet | No asset or liability recognized | Right-of-use asset and lease liability each recognized at roughly ₩870 million |
| Income statement (each year) | ₩200 million rent, a single operating expense line | Depreciation expense of ~₩174 million + interest expense (~₩44 million in year one) |
| Operating income (pre-depreciation view) | Reduced directly by the rent payment | Rent disappears from operating expense, so operating income (before depreciation) rises |
| EBITDA | Rent is deducted above the EBITDA line | Rent converts into depreciation and interest, both excluded from EBITDA → EBITDA rises |
| Cash flow statement | Full rent payment deducted from operating cash flow | Interest portion stays in operating (or financing) activities; principal repayment moves to financing activities |

The key point: the actual cash the company spends — ₩200 million a year, unchanged — hasn't moved at all. What changed is purely which line of the financial statements that ₩200 million gets split across and labeled as. Because the economics are identical and only the accounting presentation shifted, this sits in the same category as [deferred tax](/en/basics/deferred-tax-asset-liability-explained/) or goodwill impairment — items where the presentation, not the underlying cash flow, is what investors need to parse carefully.

## What Happens to the Ratios: Debt Ratios and EBITDA Rise Together

This presentation shift moves several ratios investors actually rely on, all at once. The most direct effect is on the **debt-to-equity ratio**: a lease liability that used to be entirely invisible now shows up in full, so companies that lease heavily — retailers, airlines — see their reported debt ratios rise noticeably. At the same time, **EBITDA** (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization) also rises. Rent used to be a straight operating expense that reduced EBITDA dollar for dollar; now that same cash outflow splits into depreciation and interest, both of which get added back by definition when calculating EBITDA. Revenue and actual cash spending haven't changed one bit, but the EBITDA figure looks larger. The **interest coverage ratio** (operating income ÷ interest expense), by contrast, often moves less than you'd expect, because the numerator and denominator both grow together — the newly recognized lease interest expense offsets much of the EBITDA increase. The cash flow statement shifts too: lease payments that used to flow entirely through operating cash flow now have their principal-repayment portion reclassified into financing cash flow, which tends to make reported operating cash flow look stronger than it did under the old rule.

## Why the Impact Varies So Much by Industry

How big this shift looks depends heavily on how lease-dependent a given industry already was. Retail and restaurant chains that lease their stores, airlines that lease aircraft, and telecom or shipping companies that lease towers or vessels all saw right-of-use assets and lease liabilities become a large share of their balance sheets overnight. Manufacturers that mostly own the assets they use, or companies with few lease contracts to begin with, barely felt the change. That means even within the same sector, companies that lease more of their footprint will show a bigger jump in reported debt ratios than peers that own more of theirs — and comparing two companies across different industries on debt ratio alone risks mistaking a difference in leasing intensity for an actual difference in financial health.

## What Investors Should Actually Watch: EBITDAR and Comparability

Analysts and credit rating agencies commonly correct for this distortion with **EBITDAR** (EBITDA before Rent — adding lease-related rent back to EBITDA). For heavily leased sectors like airlines or hotels, looking at EBITDAR, or a lease-adjusted leverage ratio such as (net debt + lease liabilities) ÷ EBITDAR, gives a fairer basis for comparing companies with different leasing intensity than raw EBITDA does. **Time-series comparisons** deserve the same caution: stitching together pre- and post-IFRS 16 financial statements and reading the resulting jump in debt ratio or EBITDA as a sign of business deterioration or improvement is a common mistake — the jump is a reporting-standard effect, not an operating one. Financial statement notes on leases typically break out the right-of-use asset and lease liability in detail and often discuss the transition impact directly, so checking those notes first is worthwhile for any company with meaningful lease exposure. It's also worth noting that lease liabilities are increasingly treated as debt-like items when calculating [enterprise value](/en/basics/enterprise-value-vs-equity-value/), so confirming exactly how a reported valuation multiple handled lease liabilities matters before comparing it against a sector average.

## Key Takeaways

- Before IFRS 16, only finance leases showed up as balance sheet assets and liabilities; operating leases were expensed as rent and served as a common off-balance-sheet financing channel.
- IFRS 16 erases that distinction for lessees: with narrow exceptions for short-term and low-value leases, nearly every lease now creates a **right-of-use asset** and a **lease liability**. Lessor accounting is essentially unchanged.
- Lease payments split into depreciation (on the right-of-use asset) and interest (on the lease liability). Actual cash spending doesn't change, but reported debt ratios and EBITDA both rise.
- The size of the impact depends heavily on industry leasing intensity, so comparing debt ratios or EBITDA across different industries without adjusting for leases can be misleading.
- EBITDAR and lease-adjusted leverage ratios, plus the lease footnotes in the filings, help separate the accounting-driven jump from a genuine change in the underlying business.

## FAQ

### Did the company actually borrow more money?
No. The actual cash the company spends — its annual lease payment — hasn't changed at all. What changed is purely how that payment gets presented on the financial statements. That said, credit rating agencies and loan covenants often do treat the newly recognized lease liability as real debt, so the shift isn't purely cosmetic in its practical consequences.

### Do all lease contracts end up on the balance sheet?
No. Short-term leases of 12 months or less, and low-value asset leases (small office equipment and similar items), can still be expensed the old way as a policy election. How much a company relies on these exceptions varies, so checking the footnote detail matters when comparing lease exposure across companies.

### Is a higher EBITDA after adopting IFRS 16 a good sign?
Not on its own. The EBITDA increase from adopting IFRS 16 doesn't reflect operating improvement — it reflects rent shifting into depreciation and interest, which EBITDA excludes by definition. It's only meaningful when compared against a prior year or peer reported under the same standard, never against pre-adoption figures.

### Did US GAAP change the same way?
The US standard, ASC 842, also moved to put nearly all leases on the balance sheet, much like IFRS 16. But the income statement treatment still differs in one important way: ASC 842 keeps a distinction that lets certain leases be presented as a single, straight-line "operating lease expense" rather than split into separate depreciation and interest lines the way IFRS 16 requires for every lease. That means a direct comparison of lease-related income statement metrics between a US filer and an IFRS filer isn't quite apples-to-apples.
