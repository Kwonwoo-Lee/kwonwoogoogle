## What Happened

On the evening of Monday, November 2, two of the most closely watched reports of this earnings season landed within hours of each other. Apple (Nasdaq: AAPL) and Palantir Technologies (Nasdaq: PLTR) both posted quarterly results that beat Wall Street's revenue and earnings-per-share estimates. And both stocks fell anyway. For a season that has repeatedly punished misses, this was a reminder that beating the number is no longer the same thing as satisfying the market.

Apple's report covered its fiscal fourth quarter, the three months ended September 26, and it carried extra weight as the first earnings call under CEO John Ternus, who took over on September 1. Tim Cook, who had run Apple since taking the reins from Steve Jobs in 2011, moved into the role of executive chairman, making this the company's first CEO transition in fifteen years. Revenue came in at $114.5 billion, up 12% year over year and above the roughly $113 billion analysts expected. Diluted EPS was $2.10, up 14%, beating the consensus of around $1.98 to $1.99 - though about $0.05 of that came from a one-time tariff-refund benefit, putting underlying EPS closer to $2.05. iPhone revenue rose 17% to $57.6 billion, and Services grew 10% to $31.6 billion. The one soft spot was Greater China, where sales landed roughly $1 billion below estimates.

None of that stopped shares from sliding after hours. Reports on the magnitude varied: 9to5Mac tracked an intraday after-hours drop of as much as 4.43% before the stock settled down 2.81% at $328.81, while other outlets put the decline anywhere from about 1% to 4.5%. The consistent culprit across coverage was Apple's guidance for the December quarter, where it projected revenue growth of just 7% to 10% year over year - below what investors had been positioned for - alongside warnings that DRAM and NAND memory costs would keep climbing through year-end, squeezing margins into the next report.

Palantir's report told a parallel story. Third-quarter revenue hit $2.313 billion, up 96% year over year and above $2 billion for the first time ever, comfortably clearing the roughly $2.17 to $2.18 billion consensus. U.S. commercial revenue surpassed U.S. government revenue for the first time in the company's history. GAAP net income reached $1.128 billion, a 49% margin, and both GAAP and adjusted EPS came in at $0.44, also ahead of estimates. Palantir raised its full-year 2026 revenue outlook to roughly $8.5 billion.

Yet PLTR shares still dropped - down about 3.08% in the regular session to $178.44, then falling another roughly 3.8% after hours into the $171 range. The explanation investors zeroed in on was total contract value, a forward-looking measure of new bookings: TCV came in at $2.918 billion, up just 6% year over year, a sharp deceleration next to the 96% revenue growth rate. At a stock trading near 122 times earnings, any sign that new-deal momentum is cooling gets magnified, a dynamic sharpened by Michael Burry's public short position, built on an estimate that fair value sits closer to $46 a share.

Both reports landed on a market that was already on edge. The S&P 500 closed down 0.51% at 7,541.40 that day, its third straight decline, and the Nasdaq Composite fell 1.07% to 25,164.36, with investors cautious ahead of the U.S. midterm elections the next day. The Dow, by contrast, rose 0.41% to 51,983.98, lifted by a 7.6% plunge in Brent crude to $104.77 a barrel after Iranian and Qatari negotiators emerged from talks with a draft peace proposal. Cheaper oil buoyed old-economy and travel stocks even as two of the market's highest-multiple names got marked down on their own earnings.

## Why "Beating the Number" Wasn't Enough

The mechanism behind both selloffs is the same: stock prices reflect expectations about the future, not a scorecard of the quarter that just ended. Apple's September-quarter results are already in the books; what the market priced on November 2 was whether the current pace holds into December, and a soft revenue-growth guide paired with a concrete rising-cost warning on memory chips answered that question in the wrong direction. Palantir's case is structurally identical even though the numbers look different: 96% revenue growth is a historical fact, but 6% TCV growth is a forecast of what next year's revenue line will look like, and that forecast decelerated sharply. At a valuation built on the assumption that growth keeps accelerating, even a modest cooling signal gets amplified into an outsized stock move.

The other shared thread is that the bar kept rising. Apple's results were judged not purely on their own merits but as a referendum on a brand-new CEO's first quarter, which invites harsher scrutiny by design. Palantir had already strung together several beat-and-raise quarters, which meant the market had priced in continued acceleration rather than just continued growth - so deceleration, even from a historically fast pace, reads as disappointing relative to what was already baked into the share price.

## What to Take Away From This

- **Separate the headline numbers from the forward indicators.** A strong quarter that just ended matters less than a soft guide or decelerating bookings pointing at the next one - the market trades on the latter.
- **Valuation multiplies sensitivity to growth-rate signals.** At Palantir's roughly 122x earnings multiple, the direction of the growth rate matters more than the growth rate itself.
- **A new CEO's first earnings call is graded on a curve, and not a lenient one.** Apple's debut guidance under Ternus became as much a confidence test as a numbers test.
- **The same selloff can have two different root causes.** Apple's was a margin/cost story tied to memory prices; Palantir's was a bookings/growth story tied to valuation. Conflating them misses what to actually watch next quarter.
- **After-hours percentage moves are noisy and provisional.** Both stocks saw wildly different figures reported across outlets; the move that matters is the one that holds once regular trading resumes.

## FAQ

### Why did Apple's stock fall when it beat earnings estimates?
Revenue and EPS both topped Wall Street's forecasts, but Apple's guidance for December-quarter revenue growth (7% to 10%) came in below what investors wanted, and management flagged that rising memory chip costs would keep pressuring margins. The fact that this was new CEO John Ternus's first guidance only raised the scrutiny.

### How did Palantir grow revenue 96% and still see its stock drop?
The revenue growth rate was historically strong, but the growth rate of new bookings (total contract value) slowed sharply to just 6% year over year. At Palantir's high valuation, that deceleration in forward-looking demand outweighed the backward-looking revenue beat in investors' eyes.

### Does this mean beating earnings estimates no longer matters for stocks?
Not exactly - it means a beat on past-quarter numbers is necessary but not sufficient. Markets are forward-looking, so guidance, bookings, and margin trajectory for the next quarter now carry as much or more weight than whether the quarter that just ended beat consensus.
