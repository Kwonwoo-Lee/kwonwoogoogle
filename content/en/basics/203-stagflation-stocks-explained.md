## Why Stagflation Is Called the Worst Combination

A recession is never welcome news, but investors at least have a familiar playbook for it: when growth slows, the central bank cuts rates, the lower discount rate lifts valuations, and that cushions some of the damage. Rising prices aren't automatically disastrous either, because inflation is often a byproduct of a hot economy — and in a hot economy, corporate revenue and earnings tend to be rising too. The real trouble starts when both of these "bad" signals show up together, pointing the same way. **Stagflation** — a blend of stagnation and inflation — describes an economy where growth is weakening while prices keep climbing, and it's dangerous precisely because neither side offers rescue. If the central bank cuts rates to support growth, it pours fuel on already-high inflation. If it raises rates to fight inflation, it pushes an already-cooling economy into a deeper slump. This lesson walks through why these two problems don't offset each other the way they normally would, and why the combination presses on stocks and bonds at the same time.

## The Mechanism: Not All Inflation Comes From the Same Place

Inflation driven by different causes has very different effects on the real economy. **Demand-pull inflation** happens when households and businesses are spending more, pushing prices up. In that case, output, employment, and corporate revenue tend to rise right alongside prices — the economy is strong enough to afford higher prices, and earnings growth absorbs much of the burden for investors. **Supply-side (or cost-push) inflation** is a different animal entirely. It happens when an input businesses need — oil, raw materials, labor — suddenly becomes much more expensive because of an external shock. Companies raise prices to cover the higher cost, but at the same time cut back on production and hiring because costs are up and the outlook has turned uncertain. The result is prices rising while output, consumption, and employment are shrinking — the opposite combination from demand-pull inflation. This is exactly why the oil shocks of the 1970s and early 1980s are the textbook example of stagflation: when Middle East oil supply was suddenly cut, the price of crude spiked, and because nearly every industry uses oil as either fuel or feedstock, production costs jumped across the entire economy at once. That cost spike passed straight through into consumer prices, even as output, investment, and hiring slowed.

## The Central Bank's Dilemma: No Clean Tool to Reach For

In an ordinary business cycle, a central bank's job is relatively simple because growth and inflation usually point the same direction. When the economy is overheating and inflation is rising, raising rates cools both at once. When the economy is cooling and inflation is falling, cutting rates supports both at once. Stagflation breaks that setup entirely. Fighting inflation calls for higher rates, but an economy that's already sliding toward recession can't easily absorb higher borrowing costs. Supporting growth calls for lower rates, but adding liquidity to an economy already dealing with a supply-driven price spike risks pushing inflation up another notch. Either choice makes the other problem worse — and that trade-off is the core reason central banks are so often criticized for hesitating, moving late, or responding half-heartedly once a stagflationary episode is underway. It's also part of why an inverted yield curve, covered in [what a yield curve inversion signals](/en/basics/yield-curve-inversion/), is often read as a market bet that the central bank will eventually have to choose growth over inflation once that trade-off becomes unavoidable.

## The Double Blow to Stocks: Valuations and Earnings Squeezed Together

As covered in [discounted cash flow (DCF) explained](/en/basics/discounted-cash-flow-dcf/), a stock's fair value comes from discounting its expected future cash flows back to the present. When the discount rate rises, the present value of a given stream of future cash flows falls — and if the expected future cash flows themselves shrink at the same time, value gets cut twice over. In an ordinary recession, these two forces partly cancel out: the growth outlook worsens, which is bad, but the central bank is cutting rates and lowering the discount rate, which cushions the hit to valuation multiples. In ordinary inflation, a rising discount rate is bad, but a hot economy usually brings stronger revenue and earnings growth that offsets much of it.

Stagflation removes the cushion on both sides. The central bank holds rates high (or keeps raising them) to fight inflation, pushing the discount rate up, while the slowing economy simultaneously drags down the earnings outlook. The pressure on valuation multiples and the pressure on earnings forecasts no longer offset each other — they stack in the same direction, hitting share prices twice. On top of that, there's a direct hit to the income statement itself. Input costs — commodities, energy, labor — are spiking, but companies can't simply pass the full cost on to customers, because raising prices too aggressively in a weakening economy accelerates the drop in demand. The result is revenue growth slowing down while cost of goods sold climbs, squeezing operating margins from a third direction. The discount-rate mechanism covered in [how interest rates affect stock valuations](/en/basics/interest-rates-and-stock-valuations/) is working against stocks especially hard here, precisely because all three pressures land at once.

## Bonds Don't Offer Shelter Either — When the Usual Hedge Breaks Down

Investors normally lean on bonds as insurance against a stock sell-off. When the economy weakens, the central bank cuts rates, and falling rates push bond prices up — so bonds rally exactly when stocks are falling, cushioning the portfolio. As covered in [risk parity and the All Weather portfolio](/en/basics/risk-parity-all-weather-portfolio/)'s breakdown of the "four seasons" of the business cycle, stagflation (falling growth, rising inflation) is specifically called out as the one regime where neither stocks nor long-term bonds tend to do well. When a central bank fighting inflation can't easily cut rates — or has to keep raising them — bond prices get pressured too, so stocks and bonds end up falling together instead of offsetting each other. That breaks the core assumption behind a traditional allocation like a 60/40 stock-bond split, which only diversifies risk if the two assets move in opposite directions. In the US during the 1970s, real (inflation-adjusted) total stock returns stayed negative for an extended stretch, and bonds were squeezed at the same time by surging interest rates — a widely cited period in financial history where blending stocks and bonds failed to keep pace with inflation for years at a stretch. As covered in [nominal vs. real returns](/en/basics/nominal-vs-real-returns/), a positive nominal return can still mean a real loss of purchasing power, and that gap is often most visible during exactly this kind of regime.

## What the 1970s Taught: Policy Alone Didn't Fix It

The 1970s stagflation episode is cited so often not just because inflation was high, but because the condition proved resistant to ordinary central-bank tools and persisted for years. Through the first oil shock in the early 1970s and the second in 1979, US consumer price inflation climbed into double digits while unemployment also stayed elevated — the opposite of the usual inverse relationship between the two. What's generally credited with ending that era wasn't a gradual recovery, but a deliberate shock: Fed Chair Paul Volcker, who took office in 1979, pushed the policy rate up to extraordinary levels specifically to force a deep, intentional recession. The lesson most commonly drawn from that episode is that stagflation doesn't tend to resolve itself the way an ordinary downturn eventually does — breaking it often requires sacrificing one side (usually growth) with real, sustained pain. Concerns about a repeat resurfaced around 2021–2022, when supply-chain disruptions and a war-driven spike in energy and commodity prices overlapped; many analysts argue that the unusually aggressive, rapid pace of rate hikes central banks pursued at the time was shaped directly by a determination to avoid the kind of prolonged, entrenched inflation the 1970s left behind.

## Which Assets Tend to Hold Up Better — Understanding the Structure, Not a Signal to Buy

Not every asset gets hit equally hard in a stagflationary regime. This isn't a buy signal for any specific asset, but it's worth understanding structurally why some asset classes are less exposed. **Commodities and energy** are often the very source of the price shock driving the inflation, so they tend to rise alongside it. **Gold** has historically seen demand pick up when confidence in currency value is shaken. **Consumer staples** companies sell products people keep buying regardless of the economic cycle, which gives them relatively more pricing power to pass rising costs through than most other sectors. **Growth stocks**, by contrast — especially tech names whose value depends heavily on cash flows far in the future — tend to be among the most directly and severely exposed to rising discount rates. Understanding this structural tendency is a very different exercise from deciding exactly what to buy or sell and when; that second question belongs to the strategies course, and this lesson focuses only on why stagflation affects different asset classes so unevenly.

## Key Takeaways

- Stagflation combines slowing growth with rising prices, and it's harder to manage than an ordinary recession or ordinary inflation precisely because neither side offers the central bank's usual rescue tool.
- Demand-driven inflation comes with strong growth; supply-shock inflation — like the 1970s oil shocks — comes with slowing output and hiring instead.
- Central banks get trapped in a trade-off: raising rates to fight inflation deepens the slowdown, while cutting rates to support growth risks pushing inflation higher.
- Stocks face a triple squeeze: a rising discount rate compresses valuations, a weakening outlook compresses earnings forecasts, and rising input costs compress operating margins directly.
- Bonds lose their usual hedging power when rates can't easily fall, so stocks and bonds can decline together, undermining the logic behind traditional diversification.

## FAQ

### How is stagflation different from an ordinary recession with falling prices?
Both involve a weak economy, but the direction of prices is the opposite. In a typical downturn, falling demand usually pulls prices down too, giving the central bank room to cut rates and support both growth and price stability at once. In stagflation, prices keep rising even as the economy weakens, so cutting rates risks making inflation worse rather than helping.

### How do you know if the economy is actually in stagflation right now?
A modest uptick in prices or a slight slowdown in growth isn't enough to call it stagflation. The term is generally reserved for a state where elevated inflation persists for a sustained period while growth or employment indicators are clearly and simultaneously deteriorating — not a one-off oil price spike or a brief supply disruption.

### Could a 1970s-style stagflation happen again?
Opinions in financial markets are divided. Some analysts argue a repeat of that exact pattern is less likely given changes in energy independence, labor-market structure, and central banks' credibility in fighting inflation compared to the 1970s. Others note that similar fears keep resurfacing whenever supply shocks or geopolitical risk flare up. Rather than trying to predict whether stagflation will actually happen at any given moment, it's more useful to understand the structural mechanism behind why it's so hard to manage when it does.

### Should I change my portfolio every time stagflation fears come up in the news?
Reacting to every headline about stagflation risk isn't generally advisable — as noted above, plenty of temporary oil-price spikes or supply-chain scares have raised the same fears without turning into actual stagflation. The mechanism covered in this lesson explains why this particular regime is unusually hard to navigate; the specific timing and method of any portfolio adjustment depends on an individual's own goals, time horizon, and risk tolerance.
