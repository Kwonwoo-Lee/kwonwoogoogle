## What Exactly Is "Private Equity" Doing When It Shows Up in M&A Headlines?

"A private equity firm won control through a tender offer." "A buyout fund is pushing to take the company private." "Private equity has quietly built up a large stake." Private equity fund (PEF) headlines show up constantly in M&A news, and the term sits right next to "hedge fund" so often that the two get blurred together. As covered in [hedge fund strategies](/en/basics/hedge-fund-strategies-explained/), hedge funds trade listed securities and typically hold positions for relatively short stretches. Private equity does something different: it buys a company's shares outright, or enough of them to control the business, holds on for years, actively reshapes the company, and then sells. This lesson walks through where a PEF's money comes from, who it answers to, and how the LP-GP structure and fee mechanics that sit underneath every private equity deal actually work. It's not about trading signals — it's about understanding how this kind of capital intervenes in a listed company's ownership and control.

## How Private Equity Differs From a Public Fund: Capital Open Only to a Few

As covered in [what is an ETF](/en/basics/etf-basics/), an index fund or an ordinary mutual fund is open to anyone with a small amount of cash, and its operations are tightly regulated and disclosed. Private equity is built the opposite way. It's structured as a privately placed investment vehicle, legally limited to a small number of investors — typically capped well under a few hundred — and restricted to qualified investors: pension funds, mutual aid associations, banks, insurers, and high-net-worth individuals. Minimum commitments usually run into the millions of dollars, which is why ordinary retail investors essentially can't buy in directly. In exchange for these restrictions, private equity faces far looser operating rules than public funds, which is what allows it to buy large, controlling stakes in operating companies and use heavy borrowing — strategies a mutual fund simply isn't permitted to run. The specific category aimed at taking real control of a company, rather than a passive minority stake, is usually what financial news means when it reports a "private equity takeover" of a listed firm.

## The LP-GP Structure: Separating Who Funds the Deal From Who Runs It

The defining feature of a private equity fund is a clean legal split between the people who supply the capital and the people who decide what to do with it. Most PEFs are structured as limited partnerships, with two classes of partner:

- **LP (Limited Partner):** The capital providers — pension funds, insurers, banks, endowments, and large corporations are the typical participants. As the name suggests, their liability is capped at the amount they committed, and by law they generally cannot be involved in day-to-day investment decisions like voting the fund's shares.
- **GP (General Partner):** The management firm that actually runs the fund — deciding which companies to buy, when to buy them, and when to sell. The GP can bear liability beyond its own capital contribution, and names like KKR, Blackstone, MBK Partners, or Han & Company are all operating in this GP role.

This split matters for how you read the news. A headline saying "a private equity firm invested $2 billion" almost never means that firm's own balance sheet supplied the cash — it means the fund the firm manages, capitalized mostly by its LPs, made the investment. The GP earns its keep by raising and managing that capital on everyone else's behalf.

## The Fee Structure: Management Fees and Carried Interest

A GP's income comes from two distinct sources. The first is the **management fee**, paid annually regardless of performance, typically somewhere around 1.5–2% of committed capital in most markets (rates vary by fund size and region). The second is **carried interest** — often just called "carry" — the GP's cut of the profit once an investment is actually sold for a gain, typically around 20% of the gains realized. This is structurally similar to the "two and twenty" fee model covered in [hedge fund strategies](/en/basics/hedge-fund-strategies-explained/), but private equity carry usually comes with an added condition: a **hurdle rate**. LPs have to receive a minimum return on their capital first, and only once that threshold is cleared does the GP start collecting its share of the remaining profit. That design matters because it means the GP can't get rich simply by raising a bigger fund — it only earns the big payoff by actually selling the companies it bought for more than it paid, which gives the GP a direct incentive to genuinely improve the businesses it controls rather than just sit on them.

## Blind Funds vs. Project Funds: Raising Money First, or Picking the Target First

Private equity firms raise capital in two different ways.

| | Blind Fund | Project Fund |
|---|---|---|
| Target company | Unknown when capital is raised | Identified in advance |
| LP's decision basis | Trust in the GP's track record | Evaluation of the specific deal |
| Advantage | GP can move fast when a good deal appears | LPs can assess the exact risk before committing |
| Typical use | Standard fundraising for large PE firms | Special-purpose vehicles for a specific takeover |

A blind fund asks LPs to commit capital on faith in the GP's skill and history before any specific deal exists — the LP is investing "blind" to the eventual target. A project fund works the other way around: a specific deal is already lined up, and capital is raised deal-by-deal to fund it. When M&A coverage mentions a "consortium of financial and strategic investors" forming to pursue a particular listed company, that consortium is very often the investor group behind exactly this kind of project fund.

## The Fund Lifecycle: Raise, Hold, Exit — Usually 8 to 10 Years

A private equity fund typically runs for 8 to 10 years once it's raised, moving through three broad phases. During the **investment period**, the GP deploys committed capital into actual acquisitions. This phase frequently relies on the structure covered in [leveraged buyouts](/en/basics/leveraged-buyout-lbo-explained/), where the target company's own assets and future cash flow back the debt used to fund its own purchase, letting the GP control a large company with a comparatively small equity check. During the **holding period**, the GP works to actually raise the value of the business — restructuring underperforming divisions, replacing management, or selling off non-core assets to clean up the balance sheet. Finally, in the **exit phase**, the GP sells the now-improved company to a strategic buyer, to another private equity firm, or takes it public through an IPO. The phrase "financial investor (FI) exit" that shows up in market news refers to exactly this final stage, where the PEF unwinds its stake — and it's one of the reasons overhang concerns, covered in [IPO lock-ups](/en/basics/ipo-lockup-period-overhang/), can build around a company that was previously owned by a buyout fund.

## Private Equity vs. Hedge Funds vs. Venture Capital

All three raise capital privately, but the target, time horizon, and the nature of the stake they hold are different.

| | Private Equity | Hedge Fund | Venture Capital |
|---|---|---|---|
| Typical target | Mature companies (private or public) | Listed securities broadly | Early/growth-stage private companies |
| Nature of the stake | Control or a large, influential stake | Small, often short-term positions | Minority stake |
| Holding period | Usually 3–7 years | Days to years, depending on strategy | Typically 5+ years |
| Use of leverage | Heavy, especially via LBOs | Strategy-dependent | Rarely used |
| Source of return | Value creation, then sale | Market direction or price dislocations | A small number of companies growing explosively |

Private equity typically buys control of already-established, mature businesses and improves them before selling at a higher valuation. Venture capital takes the opposite bet — small stakes in unproven early-stage companies, hoping a handful turn into outsized winners. Hedge funds differ from both in that they generally aren't trying to change the underlying business at all; they're exploiting information already reflected (or mispriced) in market prices.

## Why This Matters for Investors

Few individual investors ever commit capital to a PEF directly, but it's fairly common for a stock you hold to become a private equity target. News that a buyout fund has built a large stake or launched a tender offer usually signals an attempt to take the company private or gain enough control to push through a restructuring. That can create an opportunity for existing shareholders to sell at a premium to the market price through the tender, but as covered in [mandatory tender offers in Korea](/en/basics/mandatory-tender-offer-korea/), whether every shareholder gets genuinely equal treatment in that process remains an actively debated regulatory question. On the flip side, if a private equity-owned company later goes public through an IPO, anyone subscribing to that offering should factor in the possibility of a large overhang once the fund's lock-up expires — the PEF still needs its own exit, and a chunk of the float can hit the market at once. It's also worth watching for layoffs or asset sales in the period right after a buyout, since PEFs often move quickly to improve near-term performance, which can say as much about a company's near-term stability as its headline financials do.

## Key Takeaways

- A private equity fund (PEF) pools capital from LPs (pension funds, insurers, and similar institutions) and is run by a GP, typically investing in large, often controlling, stakes in operating companies.
- The GP earns an annual management fee plus carried interest — a share of profits, usually around 20%, once an investment is sold for a gain, often only after LPs clear a minimum hurdle return.
- Funds are raised either as blind funds (capital committed before any target is identified) or project funds (raised for one specific deal).
- A fund's lifecycle usually spans 8–10 years across an investment period, a holding period focused on raising the target's value, and an exit, frequently financed through a leveraged buyout.
- Private equity buys and improves mature businesses before selling them, which distinguishes it from both hedge funds and venture capital in target, time horizon, and the role leverage plays.
- When a stock you hold becomes a private equity takeover or tender-offer target, the offer price and potential post-IPO overhang are both worth weighing.

## FAQ

### How is private equity different from a hedge fund?
Private equity typically buys control, or a very large stake, in an established company and spends years improving it before selling. A [hedge fund](/en/basics/hedge-fund-strategies-explained/) trades listed securities, usually over a much shorter horizon, aiming to capture price moves or mispricings rather than change the underlying business. The biggest differences are holding period and how directly the fund intervenes in how the company is run.

### Can an individual retail investor put money into a private equity fund?
Private equity funds are legally restricted to qualified investors and typically require minimum commitments in the millions of dollars, which puts direct participation out of reach for most retail investors. One indirect route is buying shares when a company previously owned by a PE fund goes public — through the IPO itself or afterward on the open market.

### Is it automatically good news if my stock becomes a private equity tender-offer target?
Tender offers are often priced above the prevailing market price, which can be a near-term positive for the stock. But whether the deal actually closes depends on financing, regulatory review, and negotiations with existing major shareholders, so an announcement alone isn't a guarantee the transaction goes through.
