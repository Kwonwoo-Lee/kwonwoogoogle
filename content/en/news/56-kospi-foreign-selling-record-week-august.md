## What Happened

Between August 3 and 7, foreign investors net sold approximately 7.26 trillion won (roughly $5.1 billion) worth of Kospi-listed shares over five trading sessions - the largest weekly net-selling total on record. The previous high, 7.05 trillion won, was set in the second week of August 2021, when concern over falling DRAM prices triggered a chip-stock selloff. Five years later, the same two stocks - Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix - once again sat at the center of the selling.

The week itself was a volatile one. Kospi opened August 3 down 5.12% (338.00 points) at 6,257.45, driven by heavy foreign selling in overnight futures markets. The index whipsawed over the following sessions before staging an intraday rally of as much as 1.83% on August 7 - only for foreign investors to flip back to net selling in the afternoon, pulling the index down to close at 6,258.77, off 0.60% (37.61 points) for the day. Foreign investors alone sold roughly 860 billion won that single session. Averaged across the full week, foreigners sold more than 1.4 trillion won per trading day.

Over the same stretch, the won held near 1,416 per dollar - close to its weakest level of the year. A weaker won raises the exchange-rate risk foreign holders face on won-denominated assets, giving them an added incentive to sell even when share prices themselves aren't necessarily falling. Analysts point to this currency dynamic as a meaningful contributor to the scale of this week's outflow.

## Why Foreign Investors Are Selling Korea Right Now

Two broader trends help explain the record outflow. The first is growing unease about the pace of AI capital spending among US Big Tech. Market estimates suggest capex growth tied to AI infrastructure, which ran as high as 78.5% this year across major hyperscalers, could slow to roughly 30.2% next year. A deceleration in capex growth doesn't mean spending stops - but it does suggest the revenue growth rate for the chip and memory supply chain feeding that spending, with Nvidia at the top, could slow too. Combined with signs that operating profit growth at Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix may have already peaked in the second quarter, that's prompted a broader re-rating of the "AI supercycle" narrative that has powered Kospi's valuation for much of the past year.

The second is Kospi's own valuation overhang. The index rallied sharply in the first half of the year, pushing to record levels and lifting Korea's weighting in global benchmark indices high enough to trigger mechanical rebalancing sales from index-tracking funds. Layered on top of that, the sell-side sidecar triggers and sharp swings seen between August 5 and 7 encouraged short-term traders to lock in gains, feeding a cycle where selling begets more selling.

Monetary policy adds another layer. The Federal Reserve held rates steady at its July 29 meeting - its second under new Chair Kevin Warsh - and Warsh's tone came across more hawkish than markets had expected, with some now pricing in the possibility of a rate hike rather than a cut before year-end. There's no FOMC meeting scheduled in August; the next major catalyst is the Jackson Hole symposium, running August 27-29. That gap leaves room for policy uncertainty to weigh on risk appetite for emerging and Asian equities in the meantime, and the fact that won weakness accompanied this week's Kospi outflow suggests something more macro than a single stock story is at work.

It's worth noting this isn't a simple, one-directional "Sell Korea" story. Just a week earlier, on July 31, foreign investors net bought a record 8.16 trillion won (including NextTrade volume) - the largest single-day net purchase on record. Seeing record buying and record selling within roughly ten days of each other suggests the foreign capital currently active in Korean equities leans more toward short-term trading and hedge-fund-style positioning than long-term, buy-and-hold allocation. That kind of capital tends to swing hard on individual catalysts - earnings, policy announcements, currency moves - rather than commit to a single direction, which is itself a source of elevated volatility.

## What to Take Away From This

- **Weekly and monthly flow data reveals patterns single days hide.** Individual sessions can look noisy and directionless; aggregating five days of foreign flow makes the underlying positioning much clearer.
- **Currency moves matter as much as price moves for foreign holders.** A weakening won cuts into dollar-denominated returns even when share prices hold steady, so tracking the exchange rate alongside the index is worthwhile.
- **Record buying and record selling can occur within the same month.** That whiplash is itself informative - it suggests short-term, tactical capital rather than patient, long-term allocation is currently driving a large share of flows.
- **Valuation re-ratings don't require an actual earnings miss.** Samsung and SK Hynix's profits haven't declined - only the expected growth rate has come into question - and that alone was enough to trigger this week's selling, a reminder that markets price future growth expectations, not just current results.
- **Keep the macro calendar in view.** With no FOMC meeting in August, the Jackson Hole symposium in late August becomes the next scheduled catalyst for monetary-policy-driven volatility.
