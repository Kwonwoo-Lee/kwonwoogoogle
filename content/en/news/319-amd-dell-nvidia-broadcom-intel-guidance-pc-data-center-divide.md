## What Happened

Intel (Nasdaq: INTC) handed Wall Street a weak fourth-quarter guide after Thursday's close on October 29, and the fallout rippled across the entire chip sector the next trading day, Friday, October 30. What stood out wasn't that semiconductor stocks fell together - it's that they fell by wildly different amounts. Intel itself plunged somewhere between roughly 20% and 30% intraday, with outlets reporting slightly different figures depending on when they marked the move. PC maker Dell Technologies dropped about 13%. AMD (Nasdaq: AMD) sank roughly 10.8%, landing solidly in double-digit territory. Nvidia (Nasdaq: NVDA), by contrast, slipped only about 3%, and Broadcom (Nasdaq: AVGO) fell roughly 3.8% in pre-market trading. Four chip-adjacent companies, the same piece of bad news, and a spread in reaction that ran three to four times wider between the hardest-hit and the least-affected names.

The trigger was the substance of Intel's own numbers. The company guided fourth-quarter revenue to a range of $15 billion to $16 billion, well below the roughly $17 billion analysts had modeled. Adjusted earnings per share guidance came in around $0.25, barely half of the roughly $0.43 consensus. Adjusted gross margin guidance also stepped down sharply, from 44.1% in the third quarter to an expected 38% in the fourth. On the call, management pointed directly at a weakening PC market as the core driver, forecasting the PC market would contract by around 12% in 2026 and another 10% in 2027, with the slump persisting at least through the first half of 2027. A production incident at an Intel fabrication facility in Israel added further pressure, expected to shave roughly $300 million off fourth-quarter revenue on its own.

## Why the Reactions Diverged by Three to Four Times: It's About Revenue Mix

Intel's guidance shock clearly rattled the whole sector, but the size of the "penalty" investors assigned to each stock tracked almost exactly with how exposed that company is to the PC market. Dell sells PCs and laptops directly, so it absorbs nearly the full brunt of any PC-demand warning Intel issues - which is why it was the second-hardest-hit name after Intel itself. AMD carries meaningful exposure too: alongside its data-center server chips, it runs a sizable client-computing business selling CPUs for PCs and laptops, so Intel's downgraded PC outlook raised fears that AMD's own upcoming results would face similar headwinds. Bank of America, for instance, estimated that weak PC demand could drag as much as $750 million off AMD's upcoming quarterly revenue - but also noted that stronger server and data-center sales could offset more than $500 million of that hit. In other words, AMD is a stock with both a PC-exposure "weakness" and a data-center-growth "strength" baked in simultaneously, and on October 30 the market priced in the weakness first and asked questions about the strength later.

Nvidia and Broadcom sit on the opposite end of the spectrum. Neither company sells meaningful volumes of PC chips; the overwhelming majority of their revenue comes from data-center AI accelerators and networking silicon, which gives them little direct linkage to a PC-demand warning. Some analysts even read part of Intel's report as a positive signal for this group: Intel's own data-center and AI (DCAI) segment revenue jumped roughly 78% to 80% year-over-year, evidence that AI infrastructure spending remains robust across the industry - a dynamic that, if anything, favors data-center-heavy names like Nvidia, Broadcom, and Marvell Technology rather than hurting them. So while both stocks took some pressure simply from being lumped into "chip stocks" on a bad semiconductor headline day, investors appear to have differentiated fairly quickly based on actual revenue composition, leaving their declines far smaller than AMD's or Dell's.

## What This Really Tells Us: AMD's Own Earnings Are the Real Test

What makes this episode particularly interesting is that AMD wasn't even the company that caused it. AMD didn't report its own disappointing results - its stock fell more than 10% purely because of a competitor's guidance. That's a textbook case of "peer read-through" in the semiconductor sector, where one company's earnings report doesn't stay contained to that company but instead moves the stocks of others with similar revenue structures. The catch is that this kind of read-through isn't always precisely calibrated. Yes, AMD sells PC CPUs just like Intel does, but its server-side AI chip business (the Instinct line) is on a distinctly different growth trajectory than Intel's data-center unit. Even so, the market initially punished AMD under the blanket label of "another PC-exposed chipmaker" and only later - if at all - priced in the more nuanced differences in revenue mix.

That's exactly what makes AMD's next earnings report so consequential. AMD is scheduled to report third-quarter results on Tuesday, November 3, and that release will determine whether the market's working hypothesis from October 30 - that AMD, too, is vulnerable to the PC slowdown - actually holds up, or whether it gets overwhelmed by strength in the data-center segment instead. If AMD's server and AI-chip revenue comes in above Bank of America's offsetting estimate, Friday's selloff will likely be remembered as an overreaction that gets partially unwound. If AMD's client-computing revenue turns out to have softened in step with Intel's, the decline will instead look like an accurate, pre-emptive pricing of what was coming. Either way, the chip-sector decoupling on October 30 wasn't really a finished "event" - it was a bet placed ahead of the numbers that will actually settle the question.

## What to Take Away From This

- **The same bad news doesn't do the same damage to every stock in a sector.** Intel's PC-demand warning hit PC-exposed names like Dell and AMD directly, while data-center-focused Nvidia and Broadcom felt only indirect pressure. Don't treat "chip stocks" as one undifferentiated basket - check how much of each company's revenue actually comes from PCs versus data centers.
- **"Peer read-through" selling can overshoot.** AMD's stock moved on a competitor's guidance, not its own results. That kind of decline can partially reverse once the market has time to price in the specific differences in each company's business mix.
- **Look for the offsetting factor analysts cite, not just the headline risk.** Bank of America flagged both the downside (up to $750 million from the PC segment) and the offset (over $500 million from servers) for AMD in the same note. Weighing a single piece of bad news against the strength of a company's other business lines produces a more balanced read than reacting to the headline alone.
- **One company's guidance often previews what's coming from the next one in line.** Intel's PC warning has already shaped the market's expectations heading into AMD's November 3 report. A sector leader's results and guidance can function as a preview of what laggard reporters are likely to show.

## FAQ

### Why did AMD stock fall more than 10% without reporting its own earnings?
Because AMD sells chips for both PCs (client computing) and servers. When Intel, AMD's direct competitor, slashed its fourth-quarter guidance and blamed weakening PC demand, investors priced in the risk that AMD's own PC business would face similar pressure in its upcoming report.

### Why did Nvidia and Broadcom fall by so much less?
Both companies generate the vast majority of their revenue from data-center AI accelerators and networking chips, with minimal exposure to the PC market. Since Intel's warning was specifically about PC demand, it had a much smaller direct read-through to these two names.

### Does AMD's November 3 earnings report need to confirm this selloff?
Yes. Because the October 30 decline was priced in ahead of AMD's own numbers rather than caused by them, how AMD's client-computing revenue and server/AI revenue actually come in on November 3 will determine whether the market's pre-emptive selloff was justified or overdone.
