## Same Shipyard, Same Contract, Same Cash — Different Quarterly Story

A shipyard signs the same vessel order and collects the same cash either way, yet depending on exactly *when* that contract's revenue gets booked on the income statement, this quarter's operating profit can look completely different. Trace the roots of the large construction and shipbuilding "earnings shock" episodes that hit Korean companies in 2013 and again in 2015, and the common thread isn't that revenue vanished — it's that revenue and profit booked early in prior quarters came back all at once, later, as a correction. The accrual problem covered in [earnings quality and accruals](/en/basics/earnings-quality-accruals/) shows up in a particularly sharp form in exactly this area — the rule that decides *when* revenue gets recognized in the first place. That rule is **IFRS 15** (in Korea, K-IFRS No. 1115, mandatory from 2018), and this lesson walks through the logic it uses to time revenue, and why that logic should change how investors read a reported top line.

## The 5-Step Model: Revenue Gets Built in Five Stages

IFRS 15 decides how much revenue to recognize and when through five steps. Step 1 is **identifying the contract** — confirming a legally enforceable agreement with a customer actually exists. Step 2 is **identifying performance obligations** — breaking a single contract into the distinct promises it contains (a product sale and a separate installation service, for instance, may be two obligations rather than one). Step 3 is **determining the transaction price**, factoring in discounts, rebates and variable consideration to arrive at the total consideration the company expects to receive. Step 4 is **allocating the transaction price** across multiple performance obligations, generally in proportion to each obligation's standalone selling price. And the step that matters most for investors is Step 5: **recognizing revenue as each performance obligation is satisfied**. That last step is what decides whether revenue shows up all at once or gets spread across years — and it's also the step investors most often misread when they look only at a headline revenue-growth number.

## Point-in-Time vs. Over-Time: The Test Is Where Control Sits

IFRS 15 splits performance obligations into two buckets that get recognized at completely different speeds: satisfied **over time**, or satisfied at a single **point in time**. An obligation qualifies as over-time if any one of three conditions holds. First, the customer simultaneously receives and consumes the benefit as the company performs — a recurring monthly service is the clearest example. Second, the company's performance creates or enhances an asset the customer already controls, such as building on land the customer owns. Third, the company's output has no alternative use to the company, and it holds an enforceable right to payment for work completed to date — this is the bucket that captures custom-built ships and large-scale plant construction. A half-finished, customer-specific LNG carrier can't be sold to a different buyer, and these contracts typically guarantee progress payments as work proceeds. If none of the three conditions apply, control transfers at a single moment, judged by indicators like legal title transfer, physical possession, transfer of risks and rewards, and customer acceptance — the familiar pattern behind a retail sale, where revenue is recognized the moment goods change hands.

## Percentage of Completion: A Worked Example

Shipbuilding and construction contracts — the long-duration projects typical of order-backlog industries — are usually recognized over time using the **cost-to-cost input method**, with a simple formula:

**Cumulative revenue recognized = Contract price × (Cumulative actual cost incurred ÷ Total estimated cost to complete)**

(The figures below are simplified and hypothetical, built purely to illustrate the mechanism — they are not real company data.) Picture a plant construction contract worth ₩100 billion. In year one, actual costs incurred come to ₩30 billion, against an original total-cost estimate of ₩80 billion — a 37.5% completion rate, translating into ₩37.5 billion of recognized revenue. Now suppose that in year two, site conditions worsen and the total estimated cost has to be revised upward from ₩80 billion to ₩95 billion. That revision gets reflected all in that one period, and part of the revenue and profit recognized in year one effectively gets reversed in substance.

| | Year 1 (original estimate) | Year 2 (revised cost estimate) |
|---|---|---|
| Total estimated cost | ₩80 billion | ₩95 billion |
| Cumulative actual cost incurred | ₩30 billion | ₩60 billion |
| Completion percentage | 37.5% | 63.2% |
| Cumulative revenue recognized | ₩37.5 billion | ₩63.2 billion |
| Revenue recognized that period | ₩37.5 billion | ₩25.7 billion |

The problem sits in the denominator: **total estimated cost** is a forward-looking management estimate, not an observed fact. Understate it, and the calculated completion percentage comes out too high — pulling revenue and profit that haven't actually been earned yet into the current period. That distortion doesn't disappear; it accumulates until the estimate finally gets corrected to something realistic, at which point the correction lands as a lump-sum loss all in one quarter. The large operating losses major Korean construction firms booked in 2013, and major shipbuilders booked in 2015, both tied back to exactly this kind of cost-estimate revision on overseas plant and offshore projects. This is a structural feature of input-method percentage-of-completion accounting generally, not something unique to Korea — but it shows up most sharply wherever long-duration, order-backlog contracts make up a large share of revenue.

## Contract Assets and Contract Liabilities: What Unbilled Revenue Signals

IFRS 15 requires companies to track the gap between when revenue is recognized and when it's actually billed or collected, using two balance-sheet items. When recognized revenue exceeds the amount actually billed to the customer, the difference sits as a **contract asset** (often labeled "unbilled revenue" or "unbilled receivables" in construction and shipbuilding disclosures). When cash already collected from the customer exceeds recognized revenue, the difference sits as a **contract liability** (deferred revenue). A contract asset growing materially faster than revenue is worth watching — it can mean revenue is being recognized ahead of the pace at which it's actually being billed and collected. That alone isn't proof of a problem: contract assets naturally grow alongside revenue in a healthy, growing order-backlog business. On the opposite side, a SaaS company that collects a year's subscription fee upfront recognizes that cash as revenue gradually, month by month, as the service is actually delivered, carrying the unearned portion as a contract liability in the meantime. The "deferred revenue" discussed for subscription businesses in [earnings quality and accruals](/en/basics/earnings-quality-accruals/) is just another name for this same contract liability — the same IFRS 15 framework produces cases where revenue lags cash and cases where it leads it.

## Two Classic Channels for Manipulation: Cost Understatement and Bill-and-Hold

Two well-documented patterns let a company book revenue before the underlying performance obligation is genuinely satisfied. The first, covered above, is deliberately understating total estimated cost on a long-duration contract to inflate the completion percentage and pull revenue forward — this has repeatedly been the central issue in accounting-fraud controversies surrounding Korea's order-backlog industries. The second is **bill-and-hold** revenue recognition: issuing an invoice and booking revenue on goods that haven't actually been shipped or delivered to the customer yet. Both IFRS 15 and US GAAP allow this only under a strict set of conditions — an explicit customer request, the goods separately identified as belonging to that customer, the goods ready for immediate physical transfer, and the company unable to redirect the product to a different buyer. Recognizing revenue on invoices issued outside those conditions has long been one of the most commonly cited fact patterns in revenue-recognition fraud cases.

## What Investors Can Actually Check

Investors can't re-run a company's judgment on when control transfers, but a few things in the financial statement notes are checkable without any special access. Start by comparing revenue growth against operating cash flow growth over several quarters, watching for a gap that widens persistently rather than fluctuating around zero. IFRS 15 requires separate disclosure of contract asset and contract liability balances, so checking whether unbilled revenue (contract assets) is growing much faster than revenue is a second useful screen. For companies with substantial long-duration contracts, the footnotes discussing how completion percentages and total cost estimates have changed period to period, and any large write-downs or reversals of previously recognized contract assets, are worth reading directly. None of this amounts to re-deriving the accounting yourself — it's closer to a habit of not taking a single headline "revenue grew" number at face value without asking what assumptions and what timing produced it. Metrics like the [Beneish M-score](/en/basics/beneish-m-score-earnings-manipulation/), which quantifies receivables growth and revenue-recognition shifts into a single score, are really asking the same underlying question from a different angle: how closely is this quarter's revenue tracking actual performance and actual cash collection.

## Key Takeaways

- IFRS 15 determines both how much revenue to recognize and when, through five steps: identify the contract, identify performance obligations, determine the transaction price, allocate it across obligations, and recognize revenue as each obligation is satisfied.
- Whether an obligation is satisfied over time or at a single point in time changes the speed of recognition entirely — long-duration contracts use cost-based percentage of completion, while a typical retail sale recognizes revenue at delivery.
- The total-estimated-cost figure behind percentage of completion is a management estimate; understating it inflates revenue and profit now, and the distortion returns as a lump-sum loss once the estimate is corrected.
- Contract assets (unbilled revenue) and contract liabilities (deferred revenue) show the gap between recognized revenue and actual billing or cash collection — a contract asset growing faster than revenue is a signal worth investigating, not proof of a problem on its own.

## FAQ

### If only the timing of recognition changes, does the actual cash the company receives change too?
No. The total cash that changes hands under a contract is the same regardless of how many periods its revenue gets split across. What changes is which quarter's income statement that revenue and profit show up on, and in what amounts. That timing still matters directly, though, because it's exactly the number investors use to judge quarterly growth and profitability.

### Is a large contract asset (unbilled revenue) always a red flag?
Not by itself. A construction or shipbuilding company with a growing order backlog will naturally carry growing contract assets as more projects are in progress simultaneously. The pattern worth investigating is a contract asset growing persistently and noticeably faster than revenue over several periods — that's when it's worth checking the footnotes for whether revenue is being recognized well ahead of actual billing and collection.

### Some companies' reported revenue jumped or dropped right when IFRS 15 took effect — did their underlying business actually change?
Not necessarily. When IFRS 15 took full effect in 2018, certain industries — telecoms and construction among them, where contracts often had to be split into multiple separate performance obligations for the first time — saw reported revenue shift mechanically because of the new rule itself, not because the underlying business changed. It's safer to compare periods reported under the same standard, or compare against industry peers, rather than drawing conclusions from a jump that straddles the transition.

### Is growing deferred revenue (a contract liability) at a SaaS company a good sign?
Generally, yes — rising contract liabilities alongside new bookings usually means more future revenue is already locked in and waiting to be recognized. That said, if new-contract growth is actually slowing while deferred revenue keeps growing at its old pace, it's worth checking whether renewal terms changed or a one-time factor is involved, rather than assuming the trend will simply continue.
