## What Happened

Black Friday, November 27, brought U.S. markets a shortened trading day, with the New York Stock Exchange and Nasdaq both closing at 1 p.m. Eastern. But a shorter session didn't mean a shorter argument about where stocks go from here. All three major indexes staged a sharp rebound this week. Heading into Thursday's close (markets were closed for Thanksgiving on November 26, so Wednesday, November 25 was the last full session), the S&P 500 was up 3.9% for the week, on pace for its best weekly performance since April. The catch: just one week earlier, the index had plunged 8.6%, pushing its relative strength index below 30 - deep oversold territory. In other words, this week's bounce arrived right after the kind of capitulation-style selloff that usually raises a specific question: is this a genuine turn, or just a dead-cat bounce inside a bigger decline?

That question matters because the S&P 500 had just crossed a meaningful line. On November 20, the index officially entered correction territory, down 11.6% from the record closing high of 7,818.93 it set on October 6. For context, as of November 18 the index closed at 7,323.63 - already 6.33% below that record and in the midst of a five-session losing streak - before the decline deepened further over the following two sessions. Charlie Bilello, chief market strategist at Compound, classified this slide as the 34th correction of 5% or more in the S&P 500 since March 2009. As of November 18, its magnitude (7.1% at that point) was actually milder than the historical median drop of 7.6%, but its duration (42 days) had already run longer than the typical 26-day correction. This wasn't an unusually deep selloff - it was an unusually drawn-out one.

When a deeply oversold index snaps back this hard in a single week, two of Wall Street's most closely watched research shops arrived at opposite conclusions - and that disagreement, not the bounce itself, is the real story this week.

## Deutsche Bank vs. Capital Economics: Same Data, Opposite Conclusions

Deutsche Bank strategist Henry Allen's analysis starts from history: every S&P 500 bear market since the 1962 "Kennedy Slide" has coincided with at least one of three conditions - an actual recession, data deteriorating badly enough to stoke recession fears, or a Federal Reserve rate-hike cycle. Allen's point is that none of those three boxes is currently checked. The interest-rate angle is especially notable: markets are now pricing in fewer additional Fed rate hikes through September 2027 than they were back on October 6, the very day the S&P 500 hit its record high. If anything, the rate-hike outlook has eased since the top, not worsened. A separate CNBC tally backs up the broader argument: of the 13 U.S. bear markets since 1946, eight overlapped with an NBER-recognized recession - but five did not, with 1987's "Black Monday" crash and the 2022 inflation-driven selloff standing as the best-known examples of a bear market without a recession. Read that way, the rule "correction means recession is coming" has historically been wrong roughly four times out of ten.

Capital Economics takes the opposite side, publishing a note bluntly titled "We don't think the rally in the S&P 500 will last." Its argument leans on pattern-matching rather than macro conditions: there have been 11 prior instances since World War II when the S&P 500 fell more than 10% from an all-time high within a two-month span. In most of those cases, the sharp rebounds that followed turned out to be rallies inside an ongoing bear market, not the start of a new bull run. The note specifically points to April 2025 and March 2020 - two of the single strongest one-day rallies in S&P 500 history - noting that neither one actually marked the bottom. Both came while the broader decline was still playing out. The implication: a powerful bounce, on its own, tells you very little about whether the selling is actually over.

What makes this split genuinely interesting is that both firms are looking at essentially the same market - the same correction, the same bounce - and reaching for the same general method (comparing this episode to history). They just weight different variables. Deutsche Bank leans on the absence of recession-adjacent conditions; Capital Economics leans on how often sharp rebounds like this one have historically preceded further declines. It's a clean illustration of how market forecasting often comes down less to the raw numbers than to which lens you choose to view them through.

## Why It Matters

The forces most commonly blamed for this pullback - cooling AI capital-spending growth, stretched valuations among megacap tech names, and Fed policy - are also the forces that would need to worsen for this correction to deepen into a bear market, or ease for the rebound to prove durable. Black Friday's premarket action captured the uncertainty well: futures for the Dow, S&P 500, and Nasdaq all pointed higher, putting the week on track for roughly 4% gains across the board, yet the light volume typical of a holiday half-day session made it harder to read real conviction into the move. Thin-volume sessions tend to exaggerate price swings in both directions, a detail worth remembering before reading too much into any single day's close.

## What to Take Away From This

A "correction" and a "recession" are not the same thing, and conflating them is a common mistake. Five of the last 13 U.S. bear markets occurred without any accompanying recession, so a double-digit drawdown alone is weak evidence that the broader economy is in trouble.

A sharp rebound doesn't automatically confirm the bottom is in. Both March 2020 and April 2025 produced historic single-day rallies that arrived before the actual low, not after it. The context behind a bounce - what's happening with rates, data, and other recession-adjacent indicators - matters more than the size of the bounce itself.

The same data set can support opposite conclusions depending on which variables an analyst chooses to emphasize. Rather than anchoring to a single research house's call, it's worth comparing competing arguments and building your own framework for what would change your mind.

Price action on thin-volume, holiday-shortened sessions deserves a discount. A shortened trading day with lighter participation can produce moves that look more decisive than they really are.

## FAQ

### What's the difference between a market "correction" and a "bear market"?
A correction is generally defined as a decline of 10% or more from a recent high, while a bear market refers to a decline of 20% or more. The S&P 500 entered correction territory on November 20, down 11.6% from its October 6 record close of 7,818.93, but it remains well short of the roughly 20% decline that would define a bear market.

### How can investors tell whether this bounce is the real bottom?
There's no way to know for certain in real time. One approach is to keep tracking the three conditions Deutsche Bank's Henry Allen flagged - an actual recession, recession-fear-inducing data deterioration, or a Fed hiking cycle - against incoming economic data and Fed commentary. At the same time, it's worth weighing Capital Economics' historical counterpoint: many past rebounds of this size turned out to be rallies within an ongoing bear market rather than a genuine turning point.

### Should a light-volume Black Friday session be trusted as a market signal?
Not fully. With fewer participants and lower trading volume than a normal session, the same order flow can move prices more than it would on a typical day, exaggerating the apparent strength or weakness of a move. It's generally safer to wait and see how price action holds up once regular full-volume sessions resume the following week.
