## What Happened

Black Friday, November 27, was supposed to be a quiet, half-day session - U.S. markets closed at 1 p.m. Eastern, as they always do the day after Thanksgiving. Instead, it delivered one of the more interesting divergences of the month. The Dow Jones Industrial Average closed at 50,774.94, up roughly 592 points, or about 1.2%. The S&P 500 finished at 7,297.49, up about 1.6%. The Nasdaq Composite led the pack, closing at 24,129.50, up roughly 2.3%, with chip and AI-linked names doing most of the lifting. For the week, the gains compounded into something genuinely rare: the S&P 500 rose about 5.6%, the Dow about 5.5%, and the Nasdaq about 6.7% - the S&P 500's best weekly performance since April 2025, a stretch of roughly 19 months. Even after that bounce, November itself remained a losing month, with the S&P 500 still down around 3.7% and the Nasdaq down about 5.1% for the month to date, a reminder that one strong week doesn't erase a rough autumn.

Underneath the index-level numbers, one stock told a sharper story than any of the headline figures. SanDisk (NASDAQ: SNDK) spiked as much as roughly 16% intraday, trading near $85, making it one of the S&P 500's biggest single-day gainers. Western Digital (NASDAQ: WDC) and Seagate Technology (NASDAQ: STX) rallied alongside it. Micron Technology (NASDAQ: MU), the sector's most closely watched bellwether, also joined the move - but only rose about 7.6%, roughly half of SanDisk's gain. Two memory and storage stocks reacting to essentially the same sector-wide catalyst, on the same day, with one moving twice as hard as the other - that gap is the real story worth unpacking, and it says something specific about what actually moved SanDisk versus what moved the broader group.

## Why SanDisk Outran Micron: A Specific Catalyst Versus a General Rebound

The broader memory-and-storage complex had been in brutal shape heading into Friday. According to market commentary around the rally, SanDisk and Western Digital had lost close to 60% of their value over the trailing month, while Micron and Seagate were down somewhere in the 30%-to-40% range over a similar stretch. The proximate cause of that slide was a familiar one for the memory sector: fears that NAND and DRAM supply additions from the likes of Samsung and SK Hynix, combined with questions about whether AI-related capital spending might be peaking, were about to soften a pricing environment that had been unusually strong for most of 2026. When a sector has been hit that hard, almost any piece of good news tends to produce an outsized bounce - and Friday brought exactly that kind of news, but it landed asymmetrically.

The specific trigger was a joint announcement from Kioxia and Nvidia. The two companies disclosed a demonstration in which 144 of Kioxia's XD8 solid-state drives were installed inside an Nvidia GB200 NVL72 system - Nvidia's flagship rack-scale AI computing platform - to show that NAND flash storage can be used to meaningfully extend the effective memory capacity available to GPUs. That matters because one of the tightest bottlenecks in large-scale AI training and inference right now is memory bandwidth and capacity around the GPU itself; DRAM and high-bandwidth memory are expensive and supply-constrained, so any credible technical path that lets flash storage shoulder part of that load is, at least in theory, a new source of demand for NAND products. SanDisk is the direct beneficiary of this specific story because it manufactures NAND flash through a joint venture with Kioxia - the two companies' NAND operations are tightly linked, so a demo that puts Kioxia's product directly inside Nvidia's reference architecture reads, for traders, as a SanDisk-adjacent story almost as much as a Kioxia one.

Micron's Friday move, by contrast, wasn't really about this demo at all - it was a sympathy rally. Micron doesn't have a joint-venture relationship with Kioxia, and it wasn't named in the Nvidia announcement. What lifted Micron was the general sector-wide relief that comes when the single most beaten-down name in a group suddenly spikes: if SanDisk's NAND business just got a vote of confidence from the world's dominant AI chip company, the thinking goes, maybe the sector-wide oversupply narrative was overdone across the board, and Micron - which makes both DRAM/HBM and NAND - gets some of that benefit of the doubt. That is a real, logical read-through, but it's a diffuse, sentiment-driven one rather than a company-specific catalyst, which is exactly why it produced roughly half the move. It's also worth flagging what several analysts pointed out the same day: the Kioxia-Nvidia demonstration is a proof-of-concept, not a disclosed purchase order. Nvidia showing that 144 SSDs can extend GPU memory in a lab-style setup is meaningfully different from SanDisk booking a specific new revenue stream, and the gap between "this technology works" and "this technology is now generating material sales" is one the stock price arguably jumped ahead of.

Zooming out, Friday's rally in memory names landed inside a wider rebound across AI-linked stocks that drove the Nasdaq's outperformance for the week. Nvidia itself closed up about 0.8% at $150.49 on Friday, a modest daily move that undersells a turbulent several weeks: the stock had cratered when CEO Jensen Huang abruptly resigned on November 12 amid a board investigation, then rocketed roughly 30% higher after the board unanimously reinstated him as CEO on November 23 following input from senior U.S. government officials that his return posed no national-security concern (see our earlier coverage for the full timeline). Taken together with this week's broader bounce, Nvidia finished the week up roughly 20%. All of this sits against the backdrop of a market that, as of November 20, was still technically in a correction - down more than 11% from its October 6 record high - so Friday's memory-stock pop and the week's broader rally were, in part, beaten-down, high-beta names snapping back hard once the most acute fear in the market eased.

Micron's own fiscal first-quarter earnings report, scheduled for December 17, now takes on added importance as the next real test of whether Friday's optimism is backed by anything concrete. As the sector's largest pure-play U.S. memory maker with the most comprehensive DRAM and NAND disclosures, Micron's commentary on pricing trends and AI-related demand will likely do more to confirm or undercut Friday's rally than any single technology demo could.

## What to Take Away From This

A sector-wide rally and a company-specific catalyst are not the same thing, even when they show up on the same chart on the same day. SanDisk's gain was tied to a concrete, if preliminary, technical announcement that directly involves its own NAND business; Micron's gain was a sympathy move riding on SanDisk's news. When several related stocks jump together, it's worth asking which ones actually have a direct line to the news and which ones are simply catching a lift from sector-wide relief - the size of the gap between them (16% versus 7.6% here) is itself a useful signal about how much of the move is substance versus sentiment.

Be skeptical of the gap between "the technology works" and "the technology is generating revenue." A demonstration showing that NAND SSDs can extend GPU memory capacity in Nvidia's reference architecture is a real technical milestone, but it is not the same as a confirmed purchase order or a guidance update. Markets frequently price in the best-case commercial outcome of a proof-of-concept well before that outcome is confirmed, which is part of why such moves can be volatile in both directions.

Severely oversold stocks tend to overreact to good news, in both directions. SanDisk and Western Digital had reportedly lost close to 60% of their value in the trailing month before this rally; a stock beaten down that hard has very little marginal selling pressure left and can move disproportionately on comparatively modest news, which is part of why the percentage gain looked so large relative to the substance of the catalyst itself.

Treat the next hard data point as the real test. For memory stocks specifically, Micron's December 17 earnings report - with actual pricing, bookings, and guidance data - will say far more about whether this week's optimism is justified than Friday's single technology demonstration did.

## FAQ

### Why did SanDisk rise so much more than Micron on the same day?
SanDisk makes NAND flash through a joint venture with Kioxia, which was directly named in Friday's announcement with Nvidia about using SSDs to extend GPU memory capacity. That gave SanDisk a direct, company-specific catalyst. Micron wasn't part of that announcement; its gain was a broader sympathy rally as investors speculated that the sector-wide oversupply fears driving the month-long selloff might be overdone across memory makers generally, which is a more diffuse, sentiment-based move.

### Does the Kioxia-Nvidia SSD demonstration mean SanDisk has a new source of revenue?
Not yet, based on what was disclosed. The announcement described a technical demonstration - 144 Kioxia SSDs installed in an Nvidia GB200 NVL72 system to show that flash storage can extend GPU memory - rather than a confirmed commercial order or updated sales guidance from either company. It's a proof-of-concept that could support future demand for NAND products if the approach is adopted at scale, but the stock move priced in a chunk of that future possibility well ahead of any confirmed contract.

### Is the memory sector's selloff actually over?
That's unresolved. SanDisk, Western Digital, Micron, and Seagate had all fallen sharply over the trailing month on fears of NAND and DRAM oversupply and questions about peak AI capital spending. Friday's rally reflects optimism that some of that fear was overdone, but the underlying supply-and-demand picture hasn't fundamentally changed with one announcement. Micron's December 17 earnings report, which will include hard pricing and demand data, is widely seen as the next meaningful checkpoint for the sector.

### How does this fit into the broader stock market's week?
The memory-stock rally was one piece of a much larger AI-and-chip-led rebound that drove the S&P 500 to its best week since April 2025 (about 5.6%) and the Nasdaq up about 6.7%, even though the S&P 500 had entered a correction on November 20 and remains down for November overall. Nvidia's own volatile month - CEO Jensen Huang's November 12 resignation and November 23 reinstatement - was a major driver of that broader swing, and Friday's memory-stock pop rode on the same wave of risk appetite returning to beaten-down AI-adjacent names.
