## What Happened

On Tuesday, August 11, the Korea Customs Service released its preliminary trade figures for August 1-10, 2026. Total exports for the period came in at $21.3 billion, up 45.3% from the same window a year earlier — a record for any August 1-10 stretch on record, comfortably surpassing the previous high of $15.6 billion set in August 2022.

Semiconductors did nearly all of the heavy lifting. Chip exports for the ten-day period hit $9.952 billion, an eye-catching 155.4% jump year-over-year and itself a record for the same early-August window. Semiconductors accounted for 46.8% of total exports, up 20.1 percentage points from a year ago — meaning close to one in every two dollars of goods Korea shipped abroad during those ten days was a chip. Imports rose 23.1% to $19.488 billion, leaving a trade surplus of $1.798 billion for the period. Korea's cumulative trade surplus for the year now stands at $169.558 billion.

The market reaction to the data was notable in its own right. The KOSPI opened roughly 1% lower on the day, weighed down by combined selling from retail and institutional investors, but reversed course through the morning session as the export figures landed and reshaped sentiment. The index closed up 45.87 points, or 0.73%, at 6,345.53 — its second straight day of gains. Samsung Electronics did most of the driving, jumping about 4.13% on the day. The rally inside the chip sector was uneven, though: SK Hynix added just 0.35%, while SK Square actually fell 0.42%. The KOSDAQ, meanwhile, closed up a more modest 0.39% at 857.84. On the flow side, foreign investors were net buyers concentrated in large-cap semiconductor names, while some institutional and retail money appeared to rotate out of KOSDAQ and into KOSPI names.

## Why One Data Release Turned the Whole Session Around

To understand why this mattered, it helps to know what the Korea Customs Service's "first 10 days" export report actually is. Published well ahead of the full monthly trade figures, it is a snapshot built from just ten days of customs clearance data — too short a sample to fully represent a whole month's trend, but useful enough that traders treat it as an early read on where that month's exports are headed. Given how heavily Korea's economy and stock market lean on semiconductors, the chip line item inside this report functions almost as a proxy for near-term earnings direction across the KOSPI.

Two structural forces are driving this particular surge. The first is the explosion in demand for high-bandwidth memory (HBM), the specialized, higher-margin memory chips that go into AI accelerators and servers — demand that has scaled directly with the broader AI infrastructure buildout happening globally. The second is a broader pricing cycle: prices for commodity memory products like DRAM and NAND flash have been climbing as AI-driven demand meets supply discipline from producers who cut output during the 2022-2023 downturn. In other words, the 155.4% growth figure reflects both a volume effect (more high-value chips shipped) and a price effect (each chip fetching more), reinforcing each other rather than one masking the other.

Timing adds another layer to why this landed the way it did. Just the prior week, foreign investors had recorded their largest-ever weekly net sell-off of KOSPI shares, stoking concern about capital flight from Korean equities. Against that backdrop, a semiconductor export beat this strong read as evidence that the foreign selling was more likely a short-term positioning shift than a signal of deteriorating fundamentals — and foreign investors' return as net buyers of large-cap chip names on the very day of the release lent that interpretation some support. Still, the split reaction between Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix is worth sitting with. Samsung's surge looks like a catch-up trade, as the market had been slower to price in its expanding foundry, memory, and HBM supply story; SK Hynix, having already run hard this year as the market's preferred HBM pure-play, appears to have much of that optimism already reflected in its valuation, leaving less room for an immediate pop on the same headline.

Zooming out, the longer arc of this cycle is instructive. Korean chip exports spent much of 2022 and 2023 in decline as global IT demand cooled and memory inventories piled up, forcing both Samsung and SK Hynix into steep production cuts. The generative-AI wave that took hold from 2024 onward revived demand for high-performance server and data-center memory, and that recovery, combined with the earlier production discipline, is what has been pushing memory prices — and now export figures — sharply higher. This week's numbers look like a cycle approaching a peak phase rather than a new baseline. Semiconductors remain one of the most cyclical industries in the world, and a 155% year-over-year jump is also partly a function of comparing against a relatively weak base period from last year — a detail worth keeping in mind before extrapolating this pace of growth indefinitely.

## What to Take Away From This

- **Early-month trade data is a useful leading indicator, but it's a short sample.** A ten-day customs snapshot can diverge meaningfully from the eventual full-month figure, so treat it as a directional signal to be cross-checked against the complete monthly release rather than a final verdict.
- **Sector-wide good news doesn't guarantee uniform stock reactions.** Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix moved in opposite directions on the same headline. How much optimism is already priced into a stock's valuation, and which part of the product mix it's most exposed to, can determine whether the same catalyst produces a rally or a shrug.
- **Don't extrapolate the day's opening move.** KOSPI opened down roughly 1% before a single data release flipped the session positive. On days with major scheduled economic releases, intraday volatility can be sharp enough that early price action tells you little about where the close will land.
- **Separate the volume effect from the price effect to judge how durable a cycle really is.** This surge reflects both rising HBM shipment volumes and rising memory prices. Which of the two is doing more of the work matters for assessing how long the current upswing can last.
