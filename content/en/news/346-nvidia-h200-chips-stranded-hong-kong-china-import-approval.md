## What Happened

On Wednesday, November 5, Reuters reported a striking detail, citing two people familiar with the matter: roughly 600,000 of Nvidia's (NASDAQ: NVDA) latest H200 AI accelerators are sitting in Hong Kong warehouses, going nowhere. On the US side, this shouldn't be happening anymore. The Trump administration reversed the Biden-era export ban back in December, and by January had formally cleared H200 exports to China. The barrier that was supposed to be the hard part - Washington letting Nvidia sell - cleared months ago. Instead, the chips are stuck because of the opposite side of the transaction: China's own import approval process.

According to the report, ByteDance, Alibaba (NYSE: BABA), and Tencent have already been cleared to purchase more than 400,000 H200 chips combined, with US export licenses capping each company's allotment at up to 100,000 units. On paper, both governments have signed off. But Chinese regulators reportedly told these companies to leave most of what they've already bought sitting in Hong Kong rather than bringing it onto the mainland. Some sources said only limited quantities have actually cleared customs - a handful of major Chinese internet firms reportedly received roughly 10,000 chips each. By simple math, well under a fifth of the approved volume is actually in service.

Why would Beijing let its own companies buy chips and then order them parked in a warehouse? Reporting points to two overlapping reasons. The first is industrial policy: Chinese regulators have reportedly been steering ByteDance, Alibaba, and Tencent toward prioritizing domestic chips - Huawei's among them - for AI inference workloads, part of a broader push to reduce dependence on US technology. That's consistent with separate estimates suggesting total import approvals could stay below 200,000 units. The second reason is more mundane: even if Beijing granted blanket approval tomorrow, Hong Kong itself reportedly lacks the data center capacity and power infrastructure to run this volume of high-density compute at scale.

The market reaction landed somewhere unexpected: it showed up first in Hong Kong-listed Chinese foundries, not in Nvidia's own stock. Semiconductor Manufacturing International Corp (SMIC, 0981.HK) fell as much as 7% intraday, and Hua Hong Semiconductor (1347.HK) dropped nearly 6%. That might look backwards at first glance, but the logic holds together: if Nvidia's top-tier chips eventually do flood into China at scale, the "domestic substitution" story that has propped up Chinese foundries and homegrown AI chipmakers gets harder to tell. When news reaffirms that the chips remain stuck for now, it can take some of that pressure back off those stocks in the short term, even without resolving the underlying overhang.

## Why It Matters: Nvidia's Own Guidance Already Assumes Zero China Revenue

The real significance of this story isn't just another "China risk" headline - it's a reminder of exactly what Nvidia has and hasn't promised investors. In its recent earnings guidance, Nvidia's management has baked in zero Data Center revenue from China. In other words, the company itself doesn't currently assume this revenue will materialize, and has stripped it out of its own forecast entirely. That's conservative accounting, but it's also an important signal about how the market should be weighing this news.

The trouble is that Wall Street's own math hasn't always matched that conservatism. When Washington's export approval first became public, analysts at firms including UBS floated estimates that resumed China sales could add $5 billion to $10 billion in quarterly revenue. Those numbers make for a compelling headline, but they tend to skip past how many distinct gates sit behind the word "approval." The first gate is the US export license. The second is Chinese import and customs clearance. The third is whether the data center infrastructure to actually run the chips even exists. This Hong Kong episode makes clear that clearing the first gate does absolutely nothing to guarantee the second or third will open.

This has, in fact, been a recurring pattern throughout the year. Back in January, reports surfaced that Chinese customs officials had been instructed to "prevent" H200 chips from entering the country, which briefly rattled Nvidia's stock alongside news that some component production had been paused. Since then, the story has swung repeatedly between reports of partial import approvals and reports - like this one - that most of the approved volume remains stuck in Hong Kong. Rather than a single resolved headline, this has become something closer to a running serial that has stretched across most of 2026. Analyst sentiment has stayed overwhelmingly bullish throughout - one tally found 44 of 48 covering analysts rate Nvidia a "Strong Buy" - which suggests the market broadly treats China revenue as upside optionality rather than something the stock needs to keep climbing. But that framing is exactly why each new reminder of how hard that option is to actually exercise keeps reigniting the valuation debate.

## What to Take Away From This

The central lesson here is that for any stock entangled in geopolitics, the word "approval" needs to be unpacked into its separate stages rather than treated as a single event. Many investors read an "export approved" headline and assume the issue is resolved. In reality, cross-border transactions in advanced technology typically pass through at least three independent gates: the exporting country's license, the importing country's clearance, and the physical infrastructure needed to actually deploy the product. If any single gate stays closed, the other two being open is worth nothing to revenue. This H200 episode is close to a textbook illustration of that principle.

The second lesson is to weight a company's own guidance more heavily than analysts' optimistic add-on estimates. The fact that Nvidia guided to zero China revenue is itself a signal that management understands this uncertainty better than anyone outside the company. UBS's $5-10 billion estimate isn't necessarily wrong, but it rests on an "if every gate opens" conditional that investors need to check for themselves. Until that condition is met, that hypothetical revenue has no real claim on today's share price.

The third lesson is recognizing that the same piece of news can cut in opposite directions for different parts of a value chain. Nvidia investors might read this as a frustrating overhang, while investors in SMIC or Hua Hong might read the exact same fact as reassurance that Nvidia's chips aren't flooding the market as fast as feared. Understanding that one fact can move different links in a supply chain in opposite directions sharpens how you decide which stock to position in, and in which direction. Nvidia's fiscal third-quarter earnings, due November 17, are likely to be the next real test of how management is framing this China inventory-and-approval question to the Street.

## FAQ

### If the US already approved the exports, why are the chips still stuck?
Because an export license only grants permission for the US side of the deal - it has nothing to do with whether China permits those chips onto the mainland. In this case, Chinese regulators approved the purchases themselves but reportedly instructed companies to leave most of the hardware in Hong Kong rather than clear it through customs, a move widely read as protecting China's domestic chip industry and reducing reliance on US technology.

### Does this seriously threaten Nvidia's near-term earnings?
Not in the way a guidance cut would, since Nvidia's management has already modeled China Data Center revenue at zero. What it does threaten is the timeline for the more optimistic scenarios - like UBS's estimated $5-10 billion in quarterly upside - actually becoming real, which keeps a lid on bullish long-term valuation arguments tied to a China reopening.

### Why would ByteDance, Alibaba, and Tencent buy chips they can't even use yet?
Advanced AI accelerators run on supply chains where ordering and delivery can be separated by many months, so securing purchase rights and licenses early is standard practice in case policy eases later. Chinese companies are reported to have orders outstanding for more than 2 million H200 units in total - far beyond what Nvidia can currently produce or ship, regardless of the import bottleneck.
