## Putting a Price Tag on a Specific Interest Rate

[How QE and QT move stock prices](/en/basics/quantitative-easing-tightening-stock-market/) covered a policy where the central bank fixes how much it will buy and lets the resulting yield fall wherever the market takes it. Yield curve control (YCC) runs that sequence in reverse. The central bank announces a target for a specific maturity — "we will keep the 10-year yield near zero" — and then buys however much debt is needed to hold the yield there. QE decides the quantity first and lets the price (yield) follow; YCC decides the price first and lets the quantity follow. Both get filed under "unconventional monetary policy," but the mechanics run in opposite directions. This lesson covers how YCC actually pins a yield in place, and why — almost without exception — it has a history of ending in a sudden, disorderly break rather than a quiet exit.

## The Mechanism: Unlimited Buying to Defend a Target

The logic is straightforward. Once a central bank declares a ceiling (or target) for a specific bond's yield, it buys that bond whenever the market tries to push its yield above that level. Buying a bond raises its price, which lowers its yield — so sustained buying keeps the yield pinned below target. Because a central bank issues the currency its own bonds are denominated in, there's no hard limit on how much it can spend defending a target set in that currency. That's the theoretical appeal of YCC over plain QE: if the market fully believes the bank will defend the level no matter what, nobody wants to sell below it, which means the bank may end up buying very little to keep the promise. Credibility becomes self-reinforcing — belief that the peg holds reduces the actual buying needed to hold it.

## Why It Tends to Break: The Same Setup as a Currency Peg

This is exactly where YCC runs into the same structural weakness covered in [what causes a currency crisis](/en/basics/currency-crisis-speculative-attack-explained/). A fixed exchange rate is a promise to defend a price (the exchange rate); YCC is a promise to defend a price (a bond yield). Both rest on the government or central bank using force to hold a market price away from where fundamentals would otherwise put it. Just as reserves and the current account determine how credible a currency peg looks, a YCC target's credibility rests on how far that target has drifted from where growth and inflation actually sit. The moment investors conclude the target is out of step with fundamentals, buying the bond below the target yield and waiting for the central bank to eventually abandon the defense — at which point yields jump and bond prices fall — becomes an attractive bet. Defending against that bet means buying ever more bonds, which pumps more money into the system and adds to inflation pressure — directly undermining the price stability the policy was often meant to protect in the first place. Eventually the central bank is left with two choices: abandon the target to protect price stability, or keep defending it and absorb whatever side effects pile up.

## Case One: Wartime America, Capping Rates to Finance a War Cheaply

The first large-scale YCC program ran at the US Federal Reserve in the 1940s. In April 1942, at the Treasury's request, the Fed agreed to pin short-term bill rates at 0.375% and cap long-term Treasury yields at 2.5%, to keep wartime borrowing cheap. Defending that ceiling meant the Fed gave up control over its own balance sheet size — in effect, handing monetary independence over to the Treasury's funding needs. The cap persisted after the war, but as inflation surged alongside the 1950 outbreak of the Korean War (annualized CPI inflation reportedly reached around 21% by February 1951), continuing to buy bonds to defend the 2.5% ceiling was directly fueling the inflation it was supposed to help contain. The March 1951 Treasury-Fed Accord formally ended the arrangement and restored the Fed's independence — an episode still cited today as the starting point of the modern, independent Federal Reserve.

## Case Two: Japan, an Eight-Year Run That Ended on Its Own Terms

The longest-running modern YCC program belonged to the Bank of Japan. It introduced a target of roughly 0% for the 10-year Japanese government bond (JGB) yield in September 2016. The tolerance band around that target widened from ±0.25% to ±0.5% in December 2022 — read by markets as an effective tightening — and the BOJ loosened its enforcement further in July 2023, allowing yields to drift beyond the band more freely. On March 19, 2024, under Governor Kazuo Ueda, the BOJ formally ended both YCC and negative interest rates, judging that unconventional easing had run its course after nearly a decade of near-zero inflation. Unlike the case below, Japan's exit was a choice the central bank made itself rather than one forced on it by markets — though the cost was a balance sheet that swelled dramatically: JGB holdings reportedly reached roughly 91% of Japan's GDP by mid-2024.

## Case Three: Australia, Where the Market Broke the Target First

The Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA) launched its own YCC on March 19, 2020, targeting a yield of about 0.25% on the 3-year government bond, later lowering that target to 0.1% in November 2020, tied to a bond maturing in April 2024. In October 2021, as the economy recovered and inflation expectations rose faster than expected, the yield on that bond surged well above target. Rather than step up purchases to defend it, the RBA let the market-driven yield stand, and on November 2, 2021, formally discontinued the target. A later internal review acknowledged the episode caused "considerable" reputational damage, with many in the market reading it as a broken promise. It's a clean illustration of how the very feature that makes YCC appealing — credibility doing the work instead of large-scale buying — turns into a liability the instant that credibility cracks.

## How This Reaches the Stock Market

While a YCC target holds, its effect on stocks runs through the same portfolio-rebalancing channel covered in [how QE and QT move stock prices](/en/basics/quantitative-easing-tightening-stock-market/): an artificially suppressed long-term yield keeps the discount rate low, as described in [discounted cash flow intuition](/en/basics/discounted-cash-flow-dcf/), with growth and tech stocks — whose value sits furthest in the future — benefiting the most. The added risk with YCC is how the exit tends to work. QE can taper gradually, slowing the pace of purchases over time. YCC is a price target, not a pace, so there's rarely a gradual off-ramp: the moment the target is dropped, the yield that had been held down tends to jump all at once, pushing the discount rate up sharply and hitting the longest-duration assets — growth stocks and long-dated bonds — hardest and fastest. The market unease around Japan's exit and the sharp yield repricing right after Australia abandoned its target are both examples of this non-linear exit risk. YCC supports valuations while it holds, but its ending can be far more abrupt than a typical QE taper.

## Key Takeaways

- YCC means a central bank fixes a target yield for a specific maturity first, then buys whatever quantity of bonds is needed to defend it — the reverse of QE, which fixes the quantity and lets the yield follow.
- Like a currency peg, a YCC target becomes vulnerable once markets conclude it's out of step with fundamentals, forcing the central bank to choose between abandoning the target or defending it at the cost of fueling inflation.
- The US capped long-term rates at 2.5% from 1942 until the 1951 Treasury-Fed Accord; the BOJ ran YCC for almost eight years before ending it on its own terms in March 2024; Australia's RBA had its target broken by the market first, in October-November 2021.
- YCC supports growth-stock valuations the same way QE does while it holds, but its exit tends to be sharper and less gradual, exposing long-duration assets to a larger shock.

## FAQ

### Isn't YCC basically the same thing as QE?
They point the same direction but work in reverse order. QE fixes the purchase amount and lets the resulting yield float; YCC fixes the yield and lets the purchase amount float with market pressure. YCC can need far less buying if credibility holds, but the buying can spiral out of control if that credibility breaks.

### Has any Asian central bank besides Japan tried YCC?
The Reserve Bank of Australia ran a YCC-style program targeting a 3-year bond yield from 2020 to 2021, discussed above. Most other major central banks have avoided adopting it as formal policy.

### Is any major central bank running YCC today?
With the Bank of Japan's exit in March 2024, it's hard to find a major central bank formally running YCC at the moment. It remains a tool that could be revisited if rates fall back toward zero in a future downturn.

### Would YCC be riskier for an emerging-market central bank?
Likely yes. YCC assumes a central bank can credibly buy unlimited amounts of its own currency's bonds. In a country with heavy foreign-currency debt or weaker confidence in its own currency, expanded bond buying can quickly spill into currency instability and capital flight — compounding the kind of speculative attack covered in [what causes a currency crisis](/en/basics/currency-crisis-speculative-attack-explained/), making the failure mode harder to manage than in a developed economy.
