## What Happened

Late Sunday, November 22, endpoint-security leader CrowdStrike (Nasdaq: CRWD) and cloud/zero-trust security specialist Zscaler (Nasdaq: ZS) announced they had signed a definitive merger agreement. The deal is structured as an all-stock transaction: Zscaler shareholders will receive 1.135 shares of CrowdStrike common stock for every Zscaler share they hold. Based on CrowdStrike's closing price on Friday, November 20, that exchange ratio implies a value of about $260.41 per Zscaler share - a roughly 34% premium to Friday's close. On an enterprise-value basis, the overall deal is valued at approximately $43.9 billion.

Both boards approved the agreement unanimously. Zscaler founder and CEO Jay Chaudhry and his affiliated entities, who together hold roughly 34% of Zscaler's shares, have already signed voting agreements committing to support the deal. With the founder's own stake locked in on the "yes" side, the odds of shareholders voting the deal down look slim. Closing is targeted for the second half of CrowdStrike's fiscal year 2028 (the year running from February 2027 to January 2028), and until then the two companies will continue operating independently. The agreement also includes a $1.4 billion termination fee payable to CrowdStrike if Zscaler walks away to accept a superior competing offer.

The market's reaction split sharply down the middle. CrowdStrike, the acquirer, saw its stock drop almost 10% on the day of the announcement. Zscaler, the target, surged the opposite direction - shares jumped 16.8% to $227.45. Notably, even that jump leaves Zscaler trading below the deal's implied value of $260.41, a gap that signals the market isn't pricing the transaction as a 100% certainty. Two stocks, one piece of M&A news, and two completely opposite one-day moves.

## Why the Buyer Fell and the Target Soared

A target's stock rising and an acquirer's stock falling on deal announcement day is a common enough pattern in M&A, but the gap here was unusually wide, and the reasons are worth unpacking on both sides.

Zscaler's pop is the easier half to explain: shareholders were suddenly offered a path to realize value 34% above Friday's closing price. What's more instructive is why Zscaler still trades below that $260.41 implied value rather than right up against it. This is the signature of merger arbitrage: traders who buy into announced deals typically price in both the probability the deal actually closes and the time it will take to get there, which is why Zscaler settled at $227.45 - about 13% below the implied deal price - rather than converging on it immediately. Because this is an all-stock deal rather than cash, that discount also reflects the fact that the ultimate value Zscaler holders receive keeps moving along with CrowdStrike's own share price all the way through closing, adding another layer of uncertainty that arbitrageurs have to price in.

CrowdStrike's nearly 10% drop reflects a more layered set of concerns. The first is dilution. An all-stock deal means CrowdStrike has to issue new shares to Zscaler's shareholders, which mechanically reduces existing CrowdStrike holders' percentage ownership of the combined company - and at a $43.9 billion transaction size, that dilution isn't trivial. The second is integration risk. CrowdStrike's core strength is endpoint security, while Zscaler's is network and cloud security - adjacent but technically distinct stacks, built on different architectures with different engineering teams, sales organizations, and customer relationships. Folding two large, differently-built companies into one rarely goes exactly according to plan, and investors routinely discount acquirer stocks for exactly that uncertainty. Third, some of the selling likely reflects straightforward sticker shock: a 34% premium on a company already carrying a rich valuation raised real questions in parts of the market about whether CrowdStrike is overpaying.

Even so, CrowdStrike's strategic logic for pursuing the deal is coherent. The company says the acquisition pushes it into network security, which it describes as one of the largest segments of the entire cybersecurity market, expanding its total addressable market by more than $185 billion by calendar year 2029. The combined company is expected to start life with more than $10 billion in combined annual recurring revenue. Management has outlined roughly $500 million in annual cost synergies within 18 to 24 months of closing and about $1 billion in additional annual revenue synergies within 30 months - projections, it's worth stressing, rather than guarantees.

The deal also sits inside a broader "platformization" wave sweeping the cybersecurity sector. Rival Palo Alto Networks recently agreed to acquire identity and access management specialist CyberArk for roughly $25 billion, part of the same industry-wide shift away from selling point solutions and toward bundling endpoint, network, cloud, and identity security into single integrated platforms. Enterprise customers increasingly want to manage security from one vendor rather than stitching together tools from several, and the leading cybersecurity companies are racing to acquire their way into being that single vendor before a competitor gets there first.

## What to Take Away From This

- **Acquirer and target stocks almost always move in opposite directions on deal day.** Target shareholders get an immediate, locked-in premium; acquirer shareholders take on new dilution and integration uncertainty. Opposite reactions to the same announcement don't mean one side is "right" and the other "wrong" - they're pricing different risks.
- **The gap between a target's trading price and the implied deal value is the market's running bet on deal certainty.** Zscaler trading at $227.45 against an implied $260.41 value tells you the market is leaving room for the deal to fall apart or get renegotiated. Watching whether that gap narrows or widens over the coming weeks is a live read on how confident the market is that this closes as announced.
- **In an all-stock deal, the "price" isn't fixed.** What Zscaler shareholders ultimately receive depends on CrowdStrike's share price at closing, not at announcement. If CrowdStrike shares keep falling between now and the close, the real value delivered to Zscaler holders falls right along with it.
- **Zoom out to the sector trend, not just the single transaction.** Palo Alto's CyberArk purchase and this deal are separate corporate decisions, but both reflect the same platform-consolidation push across cybersecurity. One company's big move often forces competitors into their own deals, so a wave of M&A in one subsector is worth tracking as a group, not headline by headline.

## FAQ

### CrowdStrike is the one buying Zscaler - why did its stock fall more than Zscaler's rose on a percentage basis matters less than why it fell at all?
Because it's an all-stock deal, CrowdStrike has to issue new shares, diluting existing shareholders. On top of that, investors are weighing integration risk from merging two differently-built security platforms and questioning whether a 34% premium is too rich. Zscaler shareholders, by contrast, get an immediate, guaranteed premium, which is why their stock moved the other way.

### Why is Zscaler trading below the $260.41 implied deal price?
This is normal and is called merger arbitrage. The deal still needs to clear shareholder approval and regulatory review before it can close, and there's a real chance - however small - that terms change or the deal falls through along the way. The market prices in that uncertainty and the time remaining until closing by trading the stock at a discount to the announced value. A narrower gap signals growing market confidence the deal closes as announced.

### When is the deal expected to close?
CrowdStrike expects to complete the acquisition during the second half of its fiscal year 2028, which runs from February 2027 to January 2028. That timeline assumes the usual closing conditions are met, including Zscaler shareholder approval and regulatory clearance.

### What happens if Zscaler gets a better offer from another company?
The merger agreement includes a $1.4 billion termination fee that Zscaler would owe CrowdStrike if it walks away from the deal to accept a superior competing offer. Break fees like this are standard in large M&A agreements and are designed to discourage rival bidders and protect the original deal's chances of closing.
