## What Happened

After the close on Thursday, October 15, aluminum producer Alcoa (NYSE: AA) and online brokerage Interactive Brokers (NASDAQ: IBKR) both reported third-quarter 2026 results, alongside J.B. Hunt, BNY, Ares, and Fastenal in a busy mid-cycle earnings day. Of that group, the two stocks that stood out moved in opposite directions on remarkably similar-looking numbers.

Alcoa posted earnings per share of $1.82, beating the analyst consensus of roughly $1.54 by a wide $0.28 margin. Revenue, however, came in at $3.7 billion, slightly below the $3.78 billion Wall Street expected. On paper, that's an ambiguous combination - a clear EPS beat paired with a modest revenue miss. Yet shares opened Friday's premarket session up 1.9% at $41.27, and gains kept building through the day, with the stock touching roughly 7% above Thursday's close - above $43 - at points during the session. Alongside the earnings, Alcoa trimmed its full-year alumina production guidance to 9.5-9.6 million metric tons because of disruptions at its Pinjarra refinery, but held its aluminum production guidance steady at 2.4-2.6 million metric tons. The company also guided Section 232 tariff costs on aluminum imported from Canada to fall by roughly $10 million sequentially, based on recent pricing and shipment expectations. Alcoa went into the print already a beaten-down stock: shares had surged more than 50% earlier this year as Strait of Hormuz tensions pushed aluminum prices to a four-year high, only to give that gain back and more, arriving at Thursday's report down roughly 22% year-to-date.

Interactive Brokers told the opposite story. Adjusted EPS of $0.67 missed the $0.68 consensus by a single penny, but revenue beat expectations, coming in at roughly $1.87 billion on an adjusted basis ($1.91 billion as reported). The underlying growth metrics were, by most measures, spectacular. Customer accounts rose 35% year-over-year to 5.58 million. Daily average revenue trades (DARTs) climbed 18% to 4.27 million. Customer equity reached $964.7 billion, up 27%, while customer margin loans jumped 36% to $105.2 billion. The board also raised the quarterly dividend from $0.0875 to $0.15 per share - a roughly 71% increase. Despite all of that, shares fell about 1% on the news. Interactive Brokers had entered the quarter up somewhere between 40% and 50% for the year, pushing its price-to-earnings ratio to around 41 - roughly double its 2022-2024 average of about 20.

## Why the Same Earnings Season Produced Opposite Results

The real divide between these two stocks isn't which specific line item beat or missed - it's how much optimism, or pessimism, was already baked into each share price heading into the report. Alcoa walked into earnings down 22% for the year, a stock the market had largely written off. When expectations are pushed that low, a result doesn't need to be flawless to spark a big percentage move - a clear EPS beat and steady production guidance, even alongside a soft revenue number, is enough to trigger a sharp relief rally. The guidance for falling tariff costs mattered too: tariffs have been one of the persistent headwinds weighing on Alcoa's story all year, so any sign that pressure is easing reads as genuinely new good news layered on top of an already-low bar.

Interactive Brokers sat at the opposite end of the expectations spectrum. The stock had already rallied 40-50% this year, and its P/E ratio near double its historical norm signals that the market had priced in something close to flawless execution. The quarter's growth numbers were, by almost any objective standard, excellent - a fintech or brokerage posting 35% account growth, 18% higher trading volume, and 27% growth in customer assets is rare. But in a stock already priced for near-perfect growth, a one-cent EPS miss is enough to read as the first crack in that growth story. This isn't a one-off pattern either: Interactive Brokers stock rose earlier this year even when Q1 EPS missed consensus, then fell after a Q2 beat - a history of reactions driven less by beat-or-miss outcomes than by how each result compares with an already-elevated bar of expectations.

The structural differences between the two businesses reinforce the pattern. Alcoa is a capital-intensive, commodity-price-driven manufacturer whose earnings swing with leverage in both directions - profits expand quickly when prices rise and contract just as sharply when they fall. That volatility means the market often cares less about a single quarter's beat than about the production and cost guidance that signals whether the trend can continue, which is exactly why steady output targets paired with falling tariff costs moved the stock more than the headline EPS number alone might suggest. Interactive Brokers, by contrast, sells a structural growth story built on compounding account openings, trading volume, and margin lending. When that kind of growth is treated as the baseline expectation rather than a pleasant surprise, any hint of deceleration - even a single penny of EPS - can trigger a broader re-rating of the entire valuation.

The dividend hike adds an interesting wrinkle. A 71% increase to the payout is normally an unambiguous positive signal, yet it did nothing to prevent the stock from falling. That underscores a broader point about how growth stocks get valued: investors hold Interactive Brokers for its compounding account and trading-volume growth, not for its dividend yield, so a stronger capital-return policy wasn't enough to offset what the market read as a small crack in the growth narrative.

## What to Take Away From This

- **A beat-or-miss headline doesn't predict the stock reaction on its own.** Alcoa beat EPS and missed revenue, yet rallied; Interactive Brokers beat revenue and missed EPS by a penny, yet fell. Which line item matters more depends entirely on the narrative the market has already attached to that stock.
- **Check how far a stock has already moved before the earnings print.** Alcoa, down 22% year-to-date, had a low bar that even a mixed result could clear. Interactive Brokers, up 40-50% for the year, had a high bar that even strong growth metrics struggled to clear.
- **A valuation running at double its historical average leaves little room for disappointment.** When a stock's P/E sits far above its own historical norm, it generally needs consistently flawless execution to justify that premium - making even small misses more punishing.
- **Dividend increases don't automatically support a stock price in every situation.** For a name trading on its growth story, sustaining the growth rate matters far more to the share price than how much cash is returned to shareholders.
- **For commodity-exposed companies, forward guidance can matter more than the quarter itself.** Alcoa's steady production targets and improving tariff-cost outlook arguably carry more weight for the stock's longer-term path than a single quarter's EPS surprise.

## FAQ

### Why did Alcoa stock jump more than 7% despite missing on revenue?

The revenue miss was relatively small - about 2% below the $3.78 billion consensus - while the EPS beat was a meaningful $0.28 above expectations. Combined with the fact that the stock was already down 22% for the year and guidance pointed to falling tariff costs, the market weighed the EPS surprise and improving cost outlook far more heavily than the modest revenue shortfall.

### Interactive Brokers' growth numbers look great - so why did the stock fall?

Double-digit growth in accounts, trading volume, and customer assets is genuinely strong, but a stock that had already risen 40-50% this year had largely priced in growth of that magnitude as the expected outcome rather than a surprise. Against that backdrop, a one-cent miss on adjusted EPS was read as an early sign of a cooling growth story, prompting some profit-taking.

### Isn't it strange that the stock fell even after a 71% dividend increase?

A dividend hike is usually read as a clear positive, but its impact depends heavily on why investors hold the stock in the first place. Interactive Brokers has traded as a high-growth platform story rather than an income stock, so a stronger payout wasn't enough to offset what the market interpreted as an early crack in that growth narrative.
