## Preordering a Product Is Not the Same as Investing

You've probably seen a campaign like "Preorder this new tumbler for $10" on a platform like Wadiz or Kickstarter. That's **reward-based crowdfunding** — legally a preorder or a donation, and what the backer gets back is a product or service, not money. On the very same platform, though, you'll sometimes see a different kind of campaign: "Become a shareholder in this startup." That's an entirely different legal animal called **equity crowdfunding** (in Korea, formally "online small-amount investment brokerage"). Here, the money you hand over buys an actual security the company issues — typically common stock or a corporate bond. The two look similar on a platform's screen, but one lives in consumer-protection law while the other carries all the shareholder rights and risks covered in [What Is a Stock](/en/basics/what-is-a-stock/). This lesson covers the legal framework that lets retail investors put small amounts of money into unlisted startups, and the structural constraints every investor needs to understand before doing so.

## The Legal Framework: a Lighter-Touch Broker-Dealer Category

Normally, if an unlisted company wants to sell securities to the general public, it must file a full registration statement with regulators and go through the same prospectus review that applies to a public offering — a process too expensive and slow for most early-stage startups to bear. Korea's **online small-amount investment brokerage** category, created through a 2016 capital markets law amendment, exists specifically to lower that barrier. It works on two fronts. On the platform side, entry requirements are relaxed dramatically: instead of needing a full brokerage license, a platform can operate simply by **registering**. On the issuer side, companies are exempted from filing a full registration statement, but only if they qualify as a small or venture business within seven years of founding (social enterprises are exempt from that age limit), and only outside a negative list of industries — finance, insurance, real estate, and gambling-adjacent businesses — where it's especially hard for retail investors to judge where the money is actually going. In place of the heavy prospectus, the issuing company posts a standardized business plan, financial summary, and risk disclosure online. The design philosophy isn't "skip the review" — it's "shrink the review to a cost a small company can actually absorb."

## Three Safeguards That Replace the Missing Prospectus

Dropping full registration-statement review opens a gap in investor protection, and the framework fills it with three specific mechanisms.

**First, tiered investment caps.** Investors are sorted into professional investors, income-qualified "accredited" investors, and everyone else (retail investors), and each tier faces different limits. As generally understood, a retail investor faces both a per-company annual cap (in the low thousands of dollars) and a separate aggregate annual cap across all companies combined (roughly double that), income-qualified investors get a cap several times higher, and professional investors face no cap at all. These thresholds have been revised upward more than once to help the market grow, so always check the exact current figures a platform discloses before investing rather than relying on any single number as permanent.

**Second, a central records-keeping authority.** If an investor puts money into one company through one platform and more money into another company through a different platform, no single platform can see whether that investor is approaching their overall cap. Korea Securities Depository is designated as the **central records management institution**, pulling investment records from every registered platform to check for cap violations across the entire market. The depository also runs **Crowdnet**, a consolidated disclosure site where you can look up the business plans and risk factors of every company currently running a campaign.

**Third, segregated escrow and an all-or-nothing refund structure.** Money an investor commits doesn't sit in the platform's own account — it's held by a separate escrow institution, so a platform's own financial trouble can't touch investor funds. If a campaign fails to reach its funding target, the raise is treated as if it never happened and every investor gets a full refund; funds are released to the company only once the target is met. How much oversubscription a company will accept beyond its target is decided in advance when the campaign is structured.

## What You Actually Receive: From Common Stock to Convertible Notes

Not every equity crowdfunding campaign issues the same type of security. The most common form is plain **common stock**: the investor becomes a literal shareholder with dividend rights (which almost never materialize at this stage) and a claim on any leftover assets in liquidation, though the stake is typically far too small to carry any real voting influence. The second most common form is a [convertible bond](/en/basics/convertible-bonds-cb-bw/): the money goes in as debt with a right to convert into equity under agreed terms. This is especially common at the earliest stage, where putting a firm number on a company's valuation is hard to defend — instead, the conversion price gets pegged to whatever price the company's next funding round sets. Less commonly, a structure resembling [redeemable convertible preferred stock](/en/basics/rcps-redeemable-convertible-preferred-stock/) shows up at crowdfunding scale, carrying the same dual redemption-and-conversion rights described in that lesson, just much smaller. Whatever the form, the first thing to check in any offering document is exactly what security you're receiving and under what terms it converts or gets repaid — because that single detail determines your place in line if the company fails, and how your stake gets diluted in the next funding round.

## How This Differs From Venture Capital and Angel Investing

Both venture capital and equity crowdfunding fund the same kind of unlisted startup, but they differ enormously in how much information and control the investor gets. A VC firm typically spends months on due diligence — reviewing financial statements, interviewing founders, consulting industry experts — and then negotiates contractual rights such as a board seat or veto power over major decisions. The risk a VC takes comes bundled with monitoring and intervention tools. A crowdfunding investor gets none of that. The only information available is whatever business plan and financial summary the company itself chose to post, with no independent due-diligence team verifying it and no channel to influence how the company is run afterward. Equity crowdfunding effectively trades "access with a small check" for "information asymmetry the investor has to absorb alone" — which is exactly why cross-checking a company's claims against other crowdfunded deals in the same industry, or a founder's publicly verifiable track record, matters more here than almost anywhere else in investing.

## The Market in Numbers: 1,093 Companies, About $170 Million, Over a Decade

It helps to see the actual scale of this market. According to Korea Securities Depository, the central records authority, a cumulative 1,093 companies had used equity crowdfunding since the system launched in January 2016 through the end of 2025, raising roughly 234.6 billion won (on the order of $170 million) in total. Spread across a full decade, that's often smaller than a single rights offering from one mid-sized listed company — a reminder of how small this market still is relative to the broader capital markets. At the same time, it has quietly become a recurring funding path for early-stage startups that don't clear the bar venture capital firms typically set. Issuers also face their own annual fundraising cap through this channel, and like the investor caps, that ceiling has been raised more than once since launch — another figure worth confirming at the time you're actually investing rather than assuming it hasn't changed.

## The Biggest Catch: You Can't Sell for a Year

The sharpest difference between buying listed stock and participating in equity crowdfunding isn't expected return — it's **liquidity**. A listed stock trade executes the moment you place a sell order while the market is open. A security bought through equity crowdfunding, by contrast, carries a **mandatory one-year resale restriction** designed as an investor-protection measure. During that year, barring an exceptional event like the company going public or being acquired, there is essentially no way to convert the holding back into cash even if you need it. Things don't improve dramatically once the year passes, either. Over-the-counter markets for unlisted shares exist — Korea's [K-OTC](/en/basics/k-otc-market-explained/) is one example — but most crowdfunded early-stage startups either trade there with almost no volume or aren't registered on such a market at all. In practice, the realistic exit path for most crowdfunding investors narrows down to the company eventually going public through an [IPO](/en/basics/ipo-lockup-period-overhang/) or getting acquired — both of which can take years, and neither of which is guaranteed to happen at all. Equity crowdfunding is perhaps the starkest illustration of a principle worth internalizing everywhere: easy access on the way in says nothing about how easy the exit will be.

## A Checklist Before You Invest

If you're genuinely considering a deal, verify at least four things directly in the offering materials. **First**, exactly which security you'll receive — common stock, convertible note, preferred-style structure — and its conversion or redemption terms. **Second**, the company's plans for future funding rounds, how much dilution that implies, and the existing ownership split between founders and prior investors. **Third**, how the current funding progress compares to the target and how close the deadline is — under the all-or-nothing structure, a campaign that's well short of its goal as the deadline approaches may simply fail and refund everyone. **Fourth**, if the company has raised money through crowdfunding or another channel before, how closely its actual results tracked the plan it promised back then. Most early-stage companies, as a general feature of venture investing and not something unique to crowdfunding, either shut down or pivot away from their original plan; a small check size is no reason to skip the analysis.

## Key Takeaways

- Equity crowdfunding is legally nothing like reward-based preorder campaigns — investors receive an actual security (stock, debt, or a hybrid), carrying real shareholder or creditor rights and risks.
- Korea's online small-amount investment brokerage framework, created in 2016, exempts qualifying small or venture businesses (under seven years old) from full registration-statement filing in exchange for a simplified disclosure regime.
- Three mechanisms fill the resulting protection gap: tiered investor caps, central cross-platform record-keeping by Korea Securities Depository, and segregated escrow paired with an all-or-nothing refund structure.
- What you receive varies — common stock, convertible notes, or preferred-style instruments — and the type determines both your priority if the company fails and how future rounds dilute your stake.
- The biggest structural risk isn't the expected return but liquidity: a mandatory one-year resale lock, followed by a market with almost no secondary trading until an IPO or acquisition — if either ever happens.

## FAQ

### Is there a cap on how much a retail investor can put into equity crowdfunding?
Yes. Both retail and income-qualified investors face a per-company cap and a separate aggregate annual cap across all companies; only professional investors are exempt. The exact dollar figures have been revised upward more than once since the system launched, so check the current limits a platform discloses before investing rather than assuming an older figure still applies.

### When can I sell shares I bought through equity crowdfunding?
Not for one year after purchase — resale is restricted by law during that period. Even after the year is up, the shares aren't listed on an exchange, so there's no guaranteed market to sell into; unless the company registers on an over-the-counter market like K-OTC, a real exit usually has to wait for an IPO or acquisition.

### Do investor caps apply to reward-based crowdfunding too?
No. Reward-based crowdfunding is a preorder or donation where backers receive a product or service rather than a security, so it falls outside capital markets law entirely and isn't subject to investor caps or securities-related investor protections. Even when both types appear side by side on the same platform, their legal nature is completely different.
