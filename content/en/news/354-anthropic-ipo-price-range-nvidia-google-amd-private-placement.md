## What Happened

On Tuesday, November 10, Anthropic filed an amended S-1 with the SEC that put a real number on its long-rumored Nasdaq listing for the first time: a price range of $70 to $78 per share, covering 825 million shares of Class A common stock. At the top of that range, the offering alone could raise roughly $64.35 billion, implying a fully diluted valuation of somewhere between $1.3 trillion and $1.4 trillion. The company has applied to list under the ticker "ANT" on the Nasdaq Global Select Market. According to Reuters and other outlets, pricing is expected to be finalized on Wednesday, November 18, with trading set to begin the next day, Thursday, November 19.

Getting to this number wasn't entirely clean. An earlier draft that briefly circulated to banks had shown a different range - $70 to $80 a share on 866.7 million shares - before a company representative said that document contained inaccurate pricing details. That figure was withdrawn and replaced by the formal amended filing on November 10. It's also worth being precise about what "$70-$78" actually means right now: the registration statement has not yet gone effective, so shares cannot legally be sold or subscribed to yet, and the final price could still land above or below the stated range once the roadshow wraps up.

The detail that's drawing the most attention, though, is a separate $26 billion private placement happening alongside the public offering. Seven companies have agreed to buy Anthropic's non-voting Class N shares at the IPO price: Nvidia and Google at $10 billion each, AMD at $2.5 billion, Broadcom, Micron and Microsoft at $1 billion each, and a TSMC-affiliated entity at roughly $500 million. Add that to the public offering and the combined raise tops out around $90.4 billion. The Class N shares carry no voting rights and convert to Class A only when sold to an unaffiliated third party; they aren't part of the registered offering, carry no underwriting discount, and the whole placement is contingent on the IPO actually closing. Reuters has reported that demand for the public shares at the proposed range is already fully covered.

## The Mechanism: Why Suppliers Are Bankrolling Their Own Customer

The thing that makes this structure worth pausing on is that none of these seven buyers are ordinary financial investors. Nvidia supplies the GPUs that train and run Anthropic's models. Google provides cloud infrastructure, including TPU capacity, and already owns roughly a 14% stake as an existing shareholder. AMD, Broadcom and Micron supply AI accelerators, networking silicon and memory, respectively. In plain terms: the companies writing the checks are, in large part, the same companies collecting Anthropic's spending on the other side of the ledger.

That's exactly the setup Wall Street has started calling "circular financing," and the concern is straightforward. When Nvidia puts $10 billion into Anthropic's equity, a meaningful share of that capital can realistically flow right back to Nvidia as Anthropic spends on GPUs. Google's cloud contracts work the same way in reverse. When a supplier holds equity in its own customer, the same dollar can effectively get counted twice - once as "investment return" on the supplier's balance sheet, once as "revenue growth" on the customer's income statement - which makes it genuinely harder for outside investors to tell whether reported growth reflects independent end demand or capital simply recycling within a closed loop of AI infrastructure companies. None of that makes the arrangement illegal or even unusual for the industry, but it is precisely the kind of structure that analysts have flagged repeatedly this year when questioning how much of the AI capex boom is self-reinforcing.

There are also straightforward strategic reasons for the suppliers to do this. Locking in equity in a top customer during a period of tight GPU supply strengthens long-term purchasing relationships. Getting in before the stock trades publicly matters too: a $1.3-$1.4 trillion valuation looks comparatively cheap next to the roughly $2 trillion figure that circulated in private-market chatter just months ago. And with Google and Nvidia also holding investment and supply relationships with OpenAI, hedging exposure across both leading AI labs protects each supplier's revenue regardless of which lab ends up ahead.

The pricing itself says something about market sentiment, too. Anthropic's original S-1, filed October 26, disclosed that third-quarter revenue had jumped sharply year-over-year even as the company's operating result flipped from a profit to a loss and net losses hit $23.29 billion for the quarter (see our earlier coverage comparing Anthropic's and OpenAI's Q3 numbers). That seven major suppliers are still committing $26 billion on top of an apparently well-covered public book, despite those losses, suggests the market is pricing this as a multi-year bet on the AI infrastructure buildout rather than a near-term profitability story. The flip side is that the same concentration of capital flowing in a loop makes the downside sharper if end-user demand for AI services ever falls short of what's been built to serve it.

## What to Take Away From This

- **Who is investing matters as much as how much.** When a supplier buys equity in its own customer at scale, invested capital and reported revenue can be two labels on the same underlying dollar. Look past the headline growth rate and ask where the demand is ultimately coming from.
- **A price range is not a final price.** Until the registration statement goes effective, the $70-$78 figure can move in either direction. Treat November 18 as the date to watch for the actual number, with trading starting November 19.
- **Non-voting private-placement structures carry real information.** Class N shares with no voting rights, specific conversion triggers, and no underwriting fees tell you about control and incentives in ways the headline dollar figure alone doesn't.
- **Large AI infrastructure financing deals ripple outward.** The relative size of each supplier's commitment - Nvidia and Google at $10 billion versus Microsoft and Broadcom at $1 billion - is itself a signal of how each company privately weighs its own exposure to the AI buildout.

## FAQ

### When can ordinary investors actually buy Anthropic (ANT) stock?
Pricing is expected to be finalized on Wednesday, November 18, with trading reported to begin the next day, Thursday, November 19, on Nasdaq. That timeline isn't locked in until the registration statement goes effective, so it could shift. Retail allocation in the IPO itself is typically limited; once shares begin trading, anyone with a standard brokerage account will be able to buy ANT on the open market.

### Is the Nvidia/Google circular-financing structure automatically a red flag?
Not automatically. Suppliers investing in major customers is a long-standing practice in chips and cloud computing, and it isn't illegal or inherently fraudulent. The concern analysts raise is narrower: it becomes harder for outside investors to separate growth driven by genuine end demand from capital simply moving in a loop among a small group of AI infrastructure companies, which is a real factor to weigh in due diligence rather than a disqualifying one.

### Why is the valuation range only $1.3-$1.4 trillion when $2 trillion was floated earlier this year?
Private funding rounds earlier in 2026 had valued Anthropic close to $2 trillion. Since then, the company's Q3 swing from an operating profit to an operating loss, its disclosure of massive multi-year compute purchase and lease commitments, and broader investor caution around AI valuations have all pulled expectations down. The final IPO price could still land above or below the current $70-$78 range depending on roadshow demand.

### Does the private placement's success depend on the public IPO going through?
Yes. The $26 billion private placement is a separate transaction from the public offering, but it is explicitly contingent on the IPO actually closing, and settles immediately afterward. In other words, Nvidia, Google and the other five suppliers are committing capital on the assumption that the public offering succeeds - and their participation is itself functioning as a vote of confidence that reinforces demand for the IPO.
