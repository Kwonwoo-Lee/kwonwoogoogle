## Jobs Data Comes In Weak. Stocks Rally. What Just Happened?

One month, the U.S. nonfarm payrolls report comes in at roughly half of what economists expected. By the textbook logic — weaker job growth means a cooling economy — stocks should fall. Instead, the market closes higher. A few weeks later, the opposite plays out: CPI comes in at a level most people would call "still fine," and stocks sell off hard. Both moments look backwards to anyone reading the numbers at face value, but they're explained by the same single mechanism: what moves prices isn't whether a data point is good or bad in isolation, it's **how that data point shifts the market's expectation for where the central bank's policy rate is headed next**. [How Interest Rates Affect Stock Valuations](/en/basics/interest-rates-and-stock-valuations/) covered how the rate itself acts as a discount rate on future cash flows. This lesson covers the transmission step before that: how a single economic release can instantly reprice what the market expects that discount rate to become.

## It's the Surprise, Not the Number, That Moves Prices

Stock prices — and more directly, bond yields — are forward-looking: they already price in expectations about the future today. Before CPI or a jobs report is even released, the market has formed a consensus estimate, and that estimate is already baked into current prices and yields. So if the actual print matches that consensus exactly, markets barely move, regardless of whether the absolute number looks "good" or "bad" by any outside standard. What actually moves prices is the gap between the release and the consensus — the **surprise**. This is the same mechanism covered in [Analyst Consensus and Target Prices](/en/basics/analyst-consensus-target-price/) for corporate earnings. The difference in scope matters: an earnings surprise moves one stock, while a CPI or jobs surprise shifts the expected policy path and discount rate for every asset at once, which is why the whole market reacts in sync.

## The Fed's Reaction Function: One Data Point, A Whole Rate Path Repriced

The next layer is the **central bank reaction function**. The Fed doesn't set rates arbitrarily on meeting day — it responds in a reasonably systematic way to how inflation and employment data evolve. Simplified models like the Taylor Rule formalize this idea: when inflation runs above target or the labor market looks overheated, the probability of tightening rises; when inflation cools or employment weakens, the probability of easing rises. Market participants carry some version of this reaction function in their heads, and the instant a data release lands, they recompute: given this number, how does the probability of a cut or hike at the next FOMC meeting change? Tools like the CME's FedWatch, which derive rate-cut and rate-hike odds from fed funds futures prices, visibly jump within minutes of a release. In other words, what a CPI or jobs report actually moves isn't "today's inflation or employment level" — it's the market's collective bet on the Fed's rate path over the next several meetings.

## Walking Through the Transmission With Numbers

This is easier to see with a hypothetical example. Say the market consensus heading into a payrolls report is 180,000 new jobs, and based on that assumption, fed funds futures imply roughly a 30% probability of a rate cut at the next FOMC meeting. If the actual print comes in close to consensus — say 170,000 — there's little new information to digest, so the implied cut probability stays near 30% and stocks barely react. But if the actual number comes in at just 80,000, the market recalculates: labor demand is cooling faster than priced in, so the implied cut probability might jump from 30% to 60-70% within minutes. That shift flows straight into lower Treasury yields, and the lower discount rate becomes a tailwind for stock valuations. Flip it around: if payrolls print at 320,000, well above consensus, the market reads that as "the labor market is still running hot," the implied cut probability can collapse toward 10%, yields rise, and stocks face a headwind. In all three cases, the actual level of job creation never changes retroactively — only the gap versus consensus, and the probability recalculation that gap triggers, moves prices.

It's worth adding that this relationship isn't fixed in direction. When the market's dominant fear is a growth slowdown or recession, a weak jobs report can spook investors about the real economy more than it comforts them about rate cuts, and stocks can fall on weak data rather than rally. When the market's dominant fear is an overheating economy and sticky inflation, that same weak print gets read as relief, and stocks rally. The same data, the same direction of surprise, can produce opposite stock reactions depending on which fear — growth or inflation — is dominating investor attention at that moment. That's usually the real answer when a data release "doesn't react the way it used to."

## Headline vs. Core: Not Every Number in the Release Carries Equal Weight

A related trap is treating every figure inside a release as equally important to the Fed's reaction function. CPI is reported as a headline figure covering all goods and services, and separately as a **core** figure that strips out volatile food and energy prices. If a spike in oil prices pushes headline CPI above consensus while core CPI comes in in line or even cooler, markets often read that as a transient shock rather than a shift in the underlying inflation trend, and the rate-path reaction is muted. Fed officials have repeatedly signaled in public remarks that they weight core measures — and especially sticky components like services inflation — more heavily than the headline number when assessing underlying price pressure, and markets adjust their reaction accordingly in real time. The same logic applies to jobs reports: the headline job-creation number can surprise one way while average hourly earnings (wage growth) surprises the other way, and a hotter-than-expected wage number is frequently read as a sign of wage-driven inflation pressure that can override an otherwise soft headline print. Knowing which sub-component the market treats as more diagnostic of the Fed's next move matters more than reacting to the headline alone.

## FOMC Day and the Dot Plot: The Path Matters More Than the Decision

This same reaction function governs FOMC meeting days. Many investors assume the rate decision itself is what drives the market reaction, but the actual decision is frequently already priced in almost exactly, since it's visible in fed funds futures well ahead of the meeting. What actually moves markets on FOMC day is the language of the policy statement, remarks from the Fed Chair in the post-meeting press conference, and — on quarterly meetings (March, June, September, December) — the **dot plot**. The dot plot shows each FOMC participant's individual projection for the appropriate policy rate over the next one to three years, plotted as a scatter of dots. Even when the rate decision matches expectations exactly, if the dots shift lower than the prior quarter's projection (signaling a faster path toward cuts) or higher (signaling a slower path), the market reprices that expected future discount rate instantly, and stocks move sharply. The lesson is that the real inflection point on FOMC day is usually a change in what the Fed is expected to do next, not what it just did.

## Why Korean Markets React to U.S. Data Too

A Korean investor might reasonably ask why the KOSPI moves on a U.S. CPI or jobs report that has nothing directly to do with Korean corporate earnings. The answer is that the reaction function doesn't stop at the U.S. border. A shift in expectations for the U.S. rate path immediately changes global dollar liquidity and risk appetite, and as covered in [How Exchange Rates and Foreign Investor Flows Interact](/en/basics/exchange-rate-foreign-investor-flows/), the won-dollar rate and foreign investor positioning in Korean equities are tightly linked to exactly that. When U.S. rate-cut expectations rise, dollar weakness tends to accompany renewed foreign inflows into emerging and Korean markets; when those expectations fade, dollar strength tends to coincide with outflow pressure. The Bank of Korea runs its own independent reaction function, weighing domestic inflation, growth, and the risk of capital outflows tied to the Korea-U.S. rate gap — but because the BOK's room to diverge from the Fed's path is structurally limited, a surprise in U.S. CPI or payrolls also shifts expectations for the BOK's next move, and that expectation shift transmits straight into Korean equities.

## Takeaway

- What moves stock prices around economic data releases isn't the absolute level of the number, but how that release shifts the market's expectation for the Fed's rate path.
- Because consensus is already priced in, it's the surprise — the gap between the release and consensus — that triggers the reaction, exactly as with corporate earnings surprises, but applied to the entire market's discount rate at once.
- "Bad news is good news" happens because weak data raises the odds of rate cuts, lowering the discount rate — but when the market is more worried about growth than inflation, the same weak data can hit stocks directly instead.
- On FOMC day, what usually moves markets is a shift in the expected future rate path (statement language, the dot plot, Fed Chair remarks) rather than the rate decision itself, which is often already priced in.
- Because U.S. rate expectations transmit through the dollar and foreign capital flows, Korean and other non-U.S. markets react to U.S. data surprises as a natural extension of the same mechanism.

## FAQ

### If the data matches consensus exactly, does the market never move?
In theory the reaction should be small since it's already priced in, but in practice sub-components (like a headline/core CPI split, or other data released the same day) can still produce modest moves. When the surprise is genuinely negligible, though, the overall reaction tends to be limited.

### Does a Fed rate cut always push stocks higher?
No. As covered above, if a cut is read as a response to a sharply deteriorating economy, the hit to expected future corporate earnings can outweigh the benefit of a lower discount rate, and stocks can fall even as rates come down. What matters is whether the market interprets the cut as preemptive support or as a recession signal.

### Is the dot plot a binding commitment from the Fed?
No. The dot plot represents each individual official's projection at that point in time, not a binding policy commitment. These projections routinely shift from one quarterly release to the next as conditions change, and the market treats each shift as new information worth repricing.
