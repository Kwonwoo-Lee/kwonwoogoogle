## What Happened

Asian markets opened Tuesday, November 24, and the shockwave from Monday's Nvidia rally in the U.S. hit immediately. Taiwan's Taiex jumped as much as 9% intraday - more than double the roughly 4% gain posted by Japan's Nikkei 225 over the same stretch. The standout detail was that Taiwan's three biggest chip names all opened at their daily trading limit simultaneously: TSMC (NYSE ADR ticker TSM; listed locally as 2330), chip designer MediaTek, and Hon Hai Precision Industry - Apple's largest assembler, better known as Foxconn - all hit the 10% limit-up threshold right at the open. South Korea's Kospi climbed about 6.5% the same morning. All three markets rallied hard, but Taiwan's reaction ran roughly twice as strong as its neighbors' - a genuinely lopsided session.

The trigger traces back to Monday, November 23 in the U.S. Nvidia's board had flatly ruled out a return for Jensen Huang just six days earlier, then abruptly reversed course and reinstated him as CEO. Nvidia shares spiked as much as 36% intraday before closing up 31%, and the Nasdaq, S&P 500, and Dow all posted their best single-day performance since April 2025 (see our earlier coverage: [Nvidia Reinstates Jensen Huang as CEO Days After Board Ruled It Out](/en/news/nvidia-jensen-huang-reinstated-ceo-stock-surge-33-percent/)). Asian markets, which open roughly 13-14 hours after Wall Street closes, had no choice but to catch up to that news all at once when Tuesday's session began - effectively replaying "Nvidia's day" a session late.

## Why Taiwan Rallied Twice as Hard as Korea and Japan

The most basic explanation is index weighting. TSMC alone accounts for more than 40% of the Taiex's total market capitalization. Because TSMC is the actual foundry that manufactures the cutting-edge AI chips Nvidia designs, any piece of good news tied to Nvidia flows into TSMC's share price more directly and more immediately than it flows into almost any other stock on earth. The Kospi, by contrast, carries heavy weight from Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix but isn't nearly as dependent on a single name, and the Nikkei 225 is spread across a far more diversified set of sectors. The same headline simply hits harder when one stock dominates the index that's supposed to absorb it.

Second, this wasn't really a reaction to brand-new information - it was Asian markets catching up, with a time lag, to news that U.S. markets had already priced in the day before. Because roughly half a day separates Wall Street's close from Taiwan's open, Taiwanese investors had no way to respond to Monday's Nvidia news until Tuesday's opening bell, at which point they had to absorb an entire overnight's worth of good news all at once. Viewed purely by the "percent gained in a day" metric, Asia's reaction looks far more dramatic than America's - but it's closer to a delayed reaction to the same underlying information than an independent, fresh catalyst.

Third, a regulatory mechanism is layered on top of the pure sentiment shift. Taiwanese regulators imposed new short-selling curbs starting Monday, November 23, after the Taiex suffered its worst week since 2001 the previous week (a roughly 12% weekly decline that briefly pushed the index into bear-market territory). Under the new rule, any stock that falls 3.5% or more in a session cannot be shorted below the prior day's closing price on the following trading day. Moore Securities analyst Adam Lin had described the prior week's selloff as driven by "heavy selling sparked by tech losses in the United States... amid concerns about global AI development after Huang's departure." In this week's reversal, that same short-selling restriction likely forced existing short positions to cover, mechanically amplifying the rebound on top of whatever genuine optimism was already there. In other words, part of Tuesday's gain reflects real sentiment recovery, and part of it reflects a rule-driven, mechanical bounce.

Fourth, how long this rally holds remains genuinely uncertain. Huang has reportedly agreed to testify before the U.S. Senate Banking Committee, which would mark his first public congressional testimony. Committee Chairman Tim Scott had originally requested Huang's appearance for December 3, with Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick asked to appear December 10. The hearing's central focus is Nvidia's advanced chip export controls to China - a subject tied directly to national security. Some reporting, including from Bloomberg, indicates the exact hearing date hasn't been finalized, which leaves that timeline itself as an open variable to watch.

Notably, U.S. Nasdaq-100 futures were up only around 0.19% ahead of Tuesday's open - a muted response compared to Asia's explosive reaction. That gap is consistent with the idea that U.S. markets had already absorbed most of the news during Monday's regular session. Whether Asia's outsized reaction holds through Tuesday's U.S. cash session, or partially unwinds, is the key thing to watch for the rest of the week.

## What to Take Away From This

- **The same headline can produce very different index moves depending on how concentrated that index is.** TSMC's 40%-plus weighting in the Taiex is, on its own, most of the explanation for why Taiwan reacted roughly twice as hard as Korea or Japan. Before trading any index, check which single names actually move it.
- **A dramatic single-day move in a market that trades hours after your own may be catch-up, not new news.** Taiwan's 9% jump mostly reflected information Wall Street had already priced in the day before. When a market in a different time zone moves sharply, ask whether the catalyst is genuinely new or already reflected elsewhere.
- **Regulatory mechanics, like short-selling curbs, can mechanically amplify a price move.** Taiwan's new rule likely forced short covering on top of organic buying, meaning part of the rebound isn't a pure read on sentiment.
- **Compare the muted U.S. futures reaction against the outsized Asian one.** Nasdaq-100 futures gaining just 0.19% ahead of the open was a useful signal that the bulk of the reaction had already happened a session earlier.
- **Keep upcoming events on your checklist.** A sharp bounce doesn't resolve every pending risk - the December 3 Senate hearing and the still-unfinished Nvidia board investigation remain live variables that could move the stock again.

## FAQ

### Will this Asian rally carry through into Tuesday's U.S. trading session?
It looks unlikely to repeat at the same scale. Nasdaq-100 futures were up only about 0.19% ahead of the open, nowhere near Asia's double-digit moves in some names. That's consistent with U.S. markets having already priced in most of Monday's news during the regular session, leaving less room for a second large leg higher driven by the same catalyst.

### What exactly do Taiwan's new short-selling curbs require?
Any stock that falls 3.5% or more in a single session cannot be shorted below the previous day's closing price on the following trading day. The rule took effect November 23, immediately after the Taiex posted its worst weekly decline since 2001, and regulators framed it as a measure to curb what they called excessive bearish pressure on the market.

### Can U.S. investors trade TSMC directly?
Yes. TSMC trades on the New York Stock Exchange as an American Depositary Receipt under the ticker TSM, separate from its Taiwan-listed shares (code 2330). That gives U.S. investors exposure to the same underlying business during U.S. trading hours without needing to access the Taiwan exchange directly - though ADR pricing doesn't move in perfect lockstep with the local shares, given currency effects and the gap between the two markets' trading hours.
