## Why Would a Loan a Bank Wouldn't Make End Up Paying You a Dividend

"Private credit" has become a recurring phrase in financial news through 2026, usually attached to unsettling headlines: a non-traded BDC run by Blue Owl permanently suspending redemptions, or default rates in the asset class climbing to record highs. Yet this market used to be the exclusive territory of pension funds and insurance companies — and at some point, ordinary retail investors gained a way to buy into it just like a stock. That entry point is the BDC (Business Development Company). Where [a private equity fund (PEF)](/en/basics/private-equity-fund-pef-explained/) buys a company's equity and gets involved in running it, a BDC does something structurally different: it lends money directly to a company instead of buying its shares, collects interest, and passes most of that income straight back to shareholders as dividends. This lesson covers why private credit grew so fast, what regulatory framework channels investor money into those loans through a BDC, and how the risk an investor actually carries changes depending on whether the BDC is listed or not.

## What Private Credit Is: Corporate Lending Without a Bank in the Middle

Private credit refers to loans made directly to mid-sized companies by non-bank lenders — private credit funds, BDCs, and similar vehicles — rather than by a bank. Traditional corporate lending often ran through syndicated loans, where a bank originates a loan and then slices it up to sell to a group of investors. Private credit cuts the bank out of that chain entirely: the lender negotiates terms directly with the borrower from start to finish and typically holds the loan to maturity rather than trading it. Pricing is almost always floating-rate, set as a spread over a reference rate like SOFR, and secured against the borrower's assets or cash flow. As of 2026, industry sources commonly cite spreads in the range of roughly 500–600 basis points over the reference rate, putting all-in loan yields somewhere around 8–10% — though that is a commonly cited industry range, not a fixed figure that applies to every deal.

Why did this kind of lending push bank lending aside and grow so quickly? After the 2008 financial crisis, tightening bank regulation under frameworks like Basel III raised the capital banks had to hold against loans to lower-rated or smaller mid-sized companies. In practice, a company with a marginal [interest coverage ratio](/en/basics/interest-coverage-ratio-zombie-company/) or modest revenue found it harder to clear a bank's lending bar. Private credit funds stepped into that gap, and the market grew further as private credit became a go-to source of financing for [leveraged buyouts (LBOs)](/en/basics/leveraged-buyout-lbo-explained/) run by private equity sponsors. Estimates of the market's total size vary widely across sources, but most agree global private credit assets under management now run well past $1 trillion.

One feature worth flagging here is the floating-rate structure itself. Unlike much of the traditional corporate bond or bank-loan market, which skews fixed-rate, private credit resets with the reference rate almost immediately, so lenders capture higher income as rates rise. That's part of why private credit returns looked attractive relative to fixed-rate assets during the rapid rate hikes of 2022–2023. The flip side is just as mechanical: when rates come back down, the interest income lenders collect falls right along with them.

## What a BDC Is: The Legal Channel That Lets Retail Investors In

Most private credit funds themselves are private placements open only to institutions or accredited investors. In the US, though, a special type of investment company created under the Investment Company Act of 1940 — the BDC — opens an indirect path into this market for ordinary retail investors. A BDC must keep at least 70% of its assets invested in (mostly through lending to) private, smaller US companies, and in exchange for distributing most of its income to shareholders, it qualifies for RIC (Regulated Investment Company) tax treatment that lets it avoid corporate-level income tax. That's the mechanical reason BDC distributions look unusually high: the interest a borrower pays flows through to shareholders almost untouched by corporate tax, rather than being taxed once at the fund level and again at the shareholder level.

That said, a BDC can't borrow and invest without limit. Leverage caps are written into the law, capping debt relative to equity at a fixed ratio. Using leverage works the same way [operating leverage](/en/basics/operating-leverage-explained/) does in a different context: when the return on the loan portfolio exceeds the cost of the borrowed money, leverage amplifies the return that flows to shareholders — but when loans go bad, the losses land disproportionately on that same equity cushion.

## Listed vs. Non-Traded BDCs: Same Structure, Very Different Liquidity

BDCs split into two camps: listed BDCs that trade on an exchange like any other stock, and non-traded BDCs that only accept investor redemptions on a quarterly basis.

A listed BDC can be bought and sold at any time, but that same exchange listing exposes it to trading at a premium or discount to net asset value (NAV) — essentially the same dynamic covered in [Closed-End Fund (CEF) Discounts](/en/basics/closed-end-fund-discount-explained/). A listed BDC behaves structurally like a closed-end fund: its share count doesn't expand or contract routinely, so there's no strong arbitrage mechanism pinning the market price to NAV the way there is for an ETF. When broad credit anxiety spikes, a listed BDC's share price can fall well below its reported NAV even before the actual default rate on its underlying loans has moved at all.

A non-traded BDC markets the absence of that price volatility as a selling point, but in exchange, investors can only request redemptions within a quarterly cap, usually set as a percentage of net assets. That cap is invisible in calm markets, but when systemic credit stress pushes redemption requests well above it, the manager can prorate redemptions — or suspend them outright. In February 2026, Blue Owl permanently closed redemptions on a $1.6 billion non-traded BDC, a case that illustrated exactly this structural limit in practice. The lesson is that a stable-looking share price is not the same thing as the ability to cash out on demand.

## A Worked Example: How Leverage Amplifies Returns

Suppose a BDC funds a $1 billion loan portfolio with $500 million of equity and $500 million of borrowed money. If that portfolio earns 9% annually, it generates $90 million in interest income. If the cost of the $500 million borrowed is 5%, interest expense comes to $25 million. Ignoring operating expenses for simplicity, $65 million is left over for the $500 million of equity — a 13% return on equity, well above the portfolio's own 9% yield. That gap is the leverage effect at work.

| Item | Amount | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Equity | $500 million | 50% |
| Borrowed funds (5%/yr) | $500 million | 50% |
| Loan portfolio (9%/yr yield) | $1,000 million | — |
| Interest income | $90 million | — |
| Interest expense | $25 million | — |
| Return to equity | $65 million | 13% of equity |

Now flip the scenario: if the loan portfolio instead suffers a $75 million loss (7.5% of the total), that entire loss hits the $500 million of equity first, not the borrowed money. On an equity basis, that's roughly a 15% loss. The same leverage that boosted returns on the way up magnifies losses by just as much on the way down.

## Risks Investors Should Understand

A BDC's high distribution rate comes bundled with several structural risks.

- **Credit risk**: Because private credit targets lower-rated or smaller companies, default rates tend to rise earlier and more sharply in a downturn. Industry-reported default rates climbing to record levels through 2026 reflect accumulated repayment strain on mid-sized borrowers after a prolonged period of elevated rates.
- **Leverage amplification**: As the example above shows, leverage magnifies both gains and losses. Leverage ratios vary meaningfully across individual BDCs even within the legal cap, so the same market shock can hit different BDCs very differently.
- **Valuation opacity**: Most loans a BDC holds are private and illiquid, with no daily market price. That means NAV itself rests on the manager's internal valuation models, and outside investors have limited ability to verify how proactively those models reflect real default risk. As covered in [the liquidity premium](/en/basics/liquidity-premium-illiquidity-discount/), illiquid assets like these often look deceptively stable — low apparent volatility is not the same thing as low actual risk.
- **Sustainability of distributions**: Some BDCs post double-digit distribution rates, but because loans are floating-rate, falling reference rates cut interest income and can force distribution cuts. It's also worth checking fund reports to see whether any portion of a distribution is a return of capital rather than actual interest income.

## Korea's Proposed K-BDC: A Different Starting Point

South Korea has been discussing its own "K-BDC" framework to channel growth capital to small and venture companies, but the design differs from the US model at its core. The K-BDC structure under discussion is a closed-end vehicle with no redemption feature, built primarily around equity investment in unlisted companies rather than lending, with direct loans reportedly capped at something like 40% of total investment. That's a materially different design goal from the US BDC's lending-centered private credit model — closer to venture ecosystem funding than to the direct-lending vehicle described above. The details remain unsettled, so anyone evaluating an eventual K-BDC product should check the finalized rules and structure at the time it launches.

## Key Takeaways

- Private credit is floating-rate corporate lending made directly to mid-sized companies by non-bank lenders, a market that grew rapidly by filling the gap left as tightening bank regulation pushed banks away from this lending.
- A BDC must keep at least 70% of assets in private, smaller US companies under US investment-company law, and in exchange gets favorable tax treatment — making it the main retail access point to the private credit market.
- Listed BDCs trade continuously but can diverge from NAV, while non-traded BDCs avoid daily price swings but are bound by quarterly redemption caps that can be prorated or suspended entirely during credit stress.
- Leverage amplifies returns on the way up and losses on the way down by the same mechanism, and the valuation of illiquid loan assets depends heavily on the manager's own internal models.
- High distribution yields may reflect a genuine illiquidity premium, but that premium is never free — it's compensation for credit, leverage, and liquidity risks the investor is actually taking on.

## FAQ

### How is a BDC different from a private equity fund (PEF)?
[A private equity fund](/en/basics/private-equity-fund-pef-explained/) buys a company's equity, gets involved in running it, and aims to sell it years later for a gain. A BDC instead earns bond-like interest income mostly through lending rather than equity ownership, and passes that income back as dividends — a fundamentally different kind of return.

### If a listed BDC trades below NAV, is that a buying opportunity?
Not necessarily. As with [closed-end fund discounts](/en/basics/closed-end-fund-discount-explained/), a wide discount can simply mean the market is pricing in concern about the quality of that BDC's loan book. It's worth checking whether the discount is unusually wide relative to its own historical range, and reviewing the underlying default rate and leverage ratio before treating it as cheap.

### Does private credit's higher yield just mean it's riskier than a bank loan?
Largely, yes. The combination of lending to smaller or lower-rated borrowers and holding assets that can't be traded daily means the higher yield should be read as compensation for both credit risk and illiquidity — a risk premium and an illiquidity premium rolled into one number.
