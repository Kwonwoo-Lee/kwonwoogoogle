## The Peg Itself Is What Makes a Currency a Target

[How exchange rates affect foreign investor flows](/en/basics/exchange-rate-foreign-investor-flows/) covered how a currency behaves when it's allowed to float — if the won weakens, foreign capital reacts in fairly predictable, gradual ways. But some countries don't let their currency float at all: they fix it, or confine it to a narrow band, against another currency like the US dollar. Ironically, that choice to "stabilize" the exchange rate is exactly what makes it a target for speculative attack. In a floating-rate country, selling pressure on a currency just pushes the rate down and the trade is done. Under a peg, the central bank is committed to buying back its own currency at a fixed price no matter what, which means an attacker can bet on a very specific moment: the point at which the central bank runs out of reserves to keep doing that. This lesson walks through how that speculative attack mechanism works, and how a collapsing peg spreads beyond the currency market into a full stock market crash — using Korea's 1997 crisis as the concrete case. It's a fundamentally different mechanism from the ordinary, peacetime relationship between rates and stock prices covered in [how interest rates affect stock valuations](/en/basics/interest-rates-and-stock-valuations/).

## The Mechanics of a Speculative Attack: A Bet Against the Reserves

When a central bank commits to holding its currency at a fixed rate, every wave of selling in the market forces it to spend foreign reserves (mostly dollars) buying its own currency back at that rate. As long as that defense works, the peg holds. The problem is that the reserves available for defense aren't infinite. Speculators watch indicators like the current account balance, the stock of short-term foreign debt, and the trend in reserves, and once they conclude the peg can't hold much longer, they sell the currency aggressively. That selling itself drains reserves faster, and the more visibly reserves shrink, the more speculators pile in — a self-reinforcing dynamic. Economist Paul Krugman's 1979 "first-generation" model formalized this: a government running persistent deficits or over-issuing currency sees its reserves erode gradually, and once they fall below some threshold, speculators attack en masse and the peg breaks sharply. Defenses do sometimes succeed — in 1997, Hong Kong held its peg with backing from mainland China and by briefly pushing short-term rates as high as 150%. The UK tried to defend sterling the same way in 1992 — buying the currency and raising rates — but still exited the European Exchange Rate Mechanism on what became known as Black Wednesday. The common lesson: whether a rate defense succeeds often comes down to how much fiscal room the country actually has. Later "second-generation" models went further, showing that a peg can collapse even when fundamentals aren't obviously broken — if the market simply comes to expect the government will abandon the peg, that expectation alone can become self-fulfilling. Whether a country survives an attack or not can hinge as much on which way market expectations tip as on the underlying numbers.

## Three Channels That Turn a Currency Collapse Into a Stock Market Crash

A broken peg typically hits the stock market through three channels working together. First, companies and banks exposed to currency risk face a sudden spike in default risk. Firms that borrowed short-term dollars — trusting the peg — and lent that money out long-term in local currency see the local-currency value of their dollar debt balloon the moment the currency craters. Add that to the maturity mismatch covered in [bank duration mismatch](/en/basics/bank-duration-mismatch-svb-explained/) and you get a "double mismatch" — currency and maturity both misaligned at once — a structure that sits invisible in calm times and detonates the instant the exchange rate breaks. Second, foreign investors start dumping local stocks and bonds to convert into dollars before further depreciation, and that very selling weakens the currency further, pulling in more outflows in a self-reinforcing loop. Third, the central bank, trying to defend the currency and often to satisfy IMF bailout conditions, hikes rates sharply — which spikes corporate borrowing costs, triggers a wave of defaults, and tips the real economy into a credit crunch. These three channels aren't independent; they feed each other, which is exactly why currency crises tend to crush equity markets so fast and so far.

## The Numbers Behind Korea's 1997 Crisis

Korea's current account deficit widened through 1996 and corporate reliance on short-term foreign borrowing had grown heavily by the time a speculative attack on the Thai baht in mid-1997 touched off a crisis that spread across East Asia. The Bank of Korea spent down its reserves trying to defend the won, and by the time the government formally requested an IMF bailout on November 21, 1997, usable foreign reserves had reportedly fallen to roughly $3.94 billion. The won first crossed 1,000 per dollar on November 10 that year and kept sliding to around 1,962 won per dollar by December 23. Over the same window, the KOSPI fell from around 646 in October 1997 to 277.37 on June 16, 1998 — a drop of more than 56% in about eight months. The IMF agreed to a bailout package on December 3; figures for the IMF's own contribution are reported inconsistently across sources, ranging from roughly $19.5 billion to $21 billion, with the full package — including the World Bank, Asian Development Bank, and bilateral support — considerably larger still. The sequence — currency collapse, dollar-debt defaults, foreign capital flight, a KOSPI crash — is treated as close to a textbook case of all three transmission channels firing nearly simultaneously.

## Why "Double Mismatch" Is Such a Dangerous Combination

A recurring criticism of Korea's pre-1997 financial system is how Korean banks and merchant banks routinely borrowed short-term dollars offshore and turned around to make long-term won loans to domestic companies. That's a double mismatch: currency (dollar liabilities funding won assets) and maturity (short funding against long lending) misaligned at the same time. In calm conditions, this spread earns a profit. But the moment the assumption that the currency will stay pegged breaks down, both mismatches blow up together. Short-dated dollar debt has to be repaid or rolled over right away, and when foreign lenders — spooked by rising credit risk — refuse to roll it over, borrowers scramble to buy dollars in the open market just to meet those obligations. That scramble itself pushes the exchange rate even higher, which inflates the local-currency value of what's still owed, which drives more scrambling. This mechanism isn't unique to fixed exchange rates specifically — any country where firms and banks lean on unhedged short-term foreign-currency funding carries some version of this structural risk, peg or no peg.

## Is Korea Different Today?

Korea moved to a free-floating exchange rate regime in December 1997, which makes it much harder for speculators to target a single, clearly defined breaking point the way a fixed peg invites — a floating rate absorbs pressure gradually instead of snapping at a threshold. Reserves have also grown enormously: the Bank of Korea reported foreign exchange reserves of roughly $443.16 billion at the end of October 2026, a scale that isn't comparable to the roughly $3.94 billion in usable reserves left in late 1997. On top of that, Korea added a range of safeguards after the crisis — rules limiting short-term foreign debt exposure, currency swap lines with the US, China, and other central banks, and regular foreign-currency liquidity stress tests. None of this means the risk has gone to zero, though — it means the same mechanism is less likely to recur, not that it's been engineered out of existence. If short-term foreign debt were to build back up, or if a global credit crunch hit many countries' funding at once, the same chain of events remains structurally possible.

## What Investors Can Actually Watch For

Three indicators are the standard starting point for gauging this risk in any country. First, the ratio of short-term external debt to foreign exchange reserves — the higher it runs, the greater the odds a country can't roll over maturing debt without a scramble for dollars. Second, the current account balance — a persistent deficit means a steady drain of foreign currency that builds pressure over time. Third, movements in sovereign credit ratings and credit default swap (CDS) spreads on government debt; as covered in [credit default swaps explained](/en/basics/credit-default-swap-cds/), a sudden jump in a country's CDS premium signals that markets have started doubting its ability to pay. Beyond those three, it's also worth checking how much of corporate foreign-currency debt is left unhedged, and how large a share of the local stock and bond market is held by foreign investors — unhedged debt means currency swings hit balance sheets directly, and heavier foreign ownership tends to mean faster, deeper outflows once capital flight starts.

## Key Takeaways

- A fixed exchange rate forces the central bank to keep buying its own currency at a set price, which makes it a specific, identifiable target for speculators betting on the moment reserves run dry.
- A broken peg spreads into a full stock market crash through three reinforcing channels: default risk at firms with currency-mismatched debt, self-reinforcing foreign capital flight, and a credit crunch from emergency rate hikes.
- In 1997, Korea's usable reserves fell to roughly $3.94 billion, the won weakened to about 1,962 per dollar, and the KOSPI fell more than 56% in about eight months before Korea accepted an IMF bailout.
- The especially dangerous combination was a "double mismatch" — currency and maturity both misaligned — at Korean banks and companies that had borrowed dollars short-term to fund won loans long-term.
- Floating the won, a far larger reserve stockpile (about $443.16 billion as of October 2026), and swap lines have added real safeguards, but a renewed build-up in short-term foreign debt could still revive the same underlying mechanism.

## FAQ

### Are all countries with a fixed exchange rate at risk of this?
No. A peg backed by a stable current account, ample reserves, and low short-term foreign debt doesn't automatically invite a crisis. The risk rises specifically when the peg looks unsustainable and the market starts to notice.

### Does floating the exchange rate make this kind of crisis impossible?
Not impossible, but the risk changes shape. A floating rate absorbs pressure gradually as market conditions shift, which reduces the odds of a single sharp break when reserves "run out" at a specific point. A sudden, large capital outflow can still do real damage, though.

### Could Korea face a 1997-style crisis again today?
Korea's reserve stockpile, floating exchange rate regime, and swap-line network are on an entirely different scale than in 1997. That lowers the risk — it doesn't eliminate it — so indicators like the short-term external debt ratio and the current account trend are still worth tracking.

### Is a speculative attack just bad actors causing an otherwise avoidable crisis?
Not really. In first-generation models, a peg that's fundamentally unsustainable tends to break eventually regardless of whether anyone attacks it — speculators mostly pull forward or sharpen the timing of a collapse that was coming either way. That said, second-generation models show that shifting expectations alone can trigger a break, so the act of attacking can genuinely amplify how a crisis unfolds, not just anticipate it.
