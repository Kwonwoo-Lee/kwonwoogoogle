## Why the Market Reacts to a Survey Before the "Real" Data Even Arrives

On the first business day of nearly every month, headlines cross the wire before U.S. markets even settle in: "Manufacturing PMI beats expectations" or "Services PMI slips below the breakeven line." It can feel strange that markets move on a survey of a few hundred corporate purchasing managers before any "real" economic data — GDP, payrolls, industrial production — has even been reported. But that reaction isn't an accident. The **PMI (Purchasing Managers' Index)** doesn't wait for government statisticians to finish tabulating what already happened. It asks the people actually placing orders for raw materials and planning production schedules, right now, whether things got better or worse than last month. GDP arrives well after a quarter closes; employment and industrial production reports can take weeks. PMI's preliminary "flash" reading comes out before the month is even over, with the final figure landing on the first business day of the next one. This lesson covers how PMI is actually built, why it's treated as a leading indicator, and how it connects — mechanically — to stock prices.

## What PMI Actually Is: A Diffusion Index Built From a Survey

PMI is constructed by surveying purchasing managers — the people responsible for ordering raw materials and components — at manufacturing or services firms every month. The questions are simple: compared to last month, did new orders, production, employment, supplier delivery times, and inventory levels increase, decrease, or stay the same? Those responses get aggregated into a single number between 0 and 100 based on the share of firms reporting improvement versus deterioration. Statisticians call this a **diffusion index**, and the key feature is that it captures direction and breadth — how many firms say things improved versus worsened — rather than the magnitude of any single firm's change. That's why the number that actually matters is the line at **50**. Above 50 means more firms reported expansion than contraction versus the prior month; below 50 means the opposite. A reading of 55 doesn't mean "the economy is growing faster" than a reading of 52 in any precise sense — it means a larger share of firms reported improvement. PMI is a gauge of direction and momentum relative to the 50 line, not a measure of absolute output levels.

Inside the headline number sit several weighted sub-indices: new orders, output/production, employment, supplier delivery times, and inventories. Using the ISM manufacturing index's standard weighting as an example, new orders carries 30%, production 25%, employment 20%, supplier deliveries 15%, and inventories 10%. The **new orders** component gets particular attention, because orders booked today tend to flow directly into production and hiring decisions over the following months. For that reason, experienced readers of the data watch whether new orders is turning before output or employment does, rather than treating the single headline figure as the whole story.

## Why It's Called a Leading Indicator

PMI earns its "leading indicator" label for a simple reason. GDP, industrial production, and payroll data are **lagging or coincident** indicators — they measure economic activity that already happened, and take time to collect and verify. PMI instead asks purchasing managers what they're seeing in their order books and inventories right now, which means it often picks up a shift in sentiment months before that shift shows up in official statistics. There's typically a lag between the moment a company feels orders slowing and the moment that slowdown is reflected in hard production or employment numbers — PMI is designed to fill exactly that gap. That's why central banks and sell-side economists routinely treat PMI as one of the first indicators they check when assessing where the business cycle stands. Much like the inverted yield curve discussed in [What Is Yield Curve Inversion?](/en/basics/yield-curve-inversion/), which is another forward-looking recession signal, PMI plays a different but complementary role: it updates monthly, far more frequently, and closer to real time.

One distinction worth noting: there's a **flash (preliminary) PMI**, released before the month ends based on a partial sample (typically 75-85% of responses), and a **final PMI**, released early the following month once all responses are in. S&P Global (formerly Markit) publishes flash readings for major economies specifically because speed matters to markets — and because of the partial sample, the flash and final figures can differ slightly, with markets often reacting to the flash number regardless.

## Manufacturing, Services, and Composite PMI — The Divergence Matters Most

PMI isn't a single number; it's published separately by sector, most commonly as **manufacturing PMI** and **services PMI**, which are combined into a weighted **composite PMI**. Manufacturing used to dominate how analysts read the overall cycle, but in modern developed economies where services make up the much larger share of output, it's often the divergence between the two that carries the most information. It's not unusual to see manufacturing PMI hit a multi-year high above 50 in the same month that composite PMI — pulled down by a weak services reading — slips below the line. That kind of split is a signal in itself: it means you shouldn't read either number alone as "the economy is accelerating" or "decelerating," and it often points to exactly which sector is leading a cyclical turn and whether that weakness is starting to spread. As covered in [Sector Rotation and the Business Cycle](/en/basics/sector-rotation-business-cycle/), different sectors lead at different points in the cycle — a manufacturing/services PMI split is frequently the earliest readable sign that such a rotation is underway.

## PMI and Stock Prices: It's the Surprise, Not the Level, That Moves Markets

Analyses of PMI's relationship to stock prices consistently point to one mechanism: because markets have already priced in a consensus estimate ahead of the release, what actually moves prices is usually whether the reported figure beat or missed that consensus — the **surprise** — rather than the absolute level or whether it crossed 50. A PMI print that comes in well above expectations can lift stocks even if the number is still below 50, read as "conditions are bottoming faster than feared." Conversely, a strong reading above 50 can disappoint and sell off if the market had priced in something even stronger. This is the exact same mechanism covered in [Analyst Consensus and Target Prices](/en/basics/analyst-consensus-target-price/), where it's the surprise relative to consensus — not the earnings figure itself — that moves a stock after a results release. The difference is scope: an earnings surprise moves one company's stock, while a PMI surprise shifts expectations about the whole economy, which tends to show up as rotation between cyclical and defensive sectors rather than a move in any single name.

## Reading PMI Correctly: Distortions and Differences Across Publishers

A few traps come up repeatedly when interpreting PMI. The first is **inventory-driven distortion**: ahead of anticipated tariff hikes or supply disruptions, firms sometimes stockpile raw materials and components before prices rise or availability tightens, which can temporarily inflate the new orders and production sub-indices without reflecting a genuine recovery in underlying demand. In periods like this, it's worth checking the inventory and new orders sub-components separately rather than taking the headline number at face value. The second trap is **publisher methodology**. The same "U.S. manufacturing PMI" can mean two different things depending on whether it comes from the Institute for Supply Management (ISM) or S&P Global (formerly Markit) — different survey panels and calculation methods can produce different readings, occasionally pointing in opposite directions in the same month. Neither is simply "wrong"; they're separate indicators built on different samples. Korea illustrates this well too: S&P Global publishes a monthly Korea manufacturing PMI, while the Bank of Korea separately compiles its own **Business Survey Index (BSI)**, centered around 100 rather than 50 and built on a different methodology. Both are survey-based sentiment gauges serving a similar purpose, but they aren't directly comparable figures.

## Takeaway

- PMI is a diffusion index built from a purchasing-manager survey. Above 50 means more firms reported expansion than contraction; below 50 means the reverse — it measures direction and momentum relative to that line, not absolute output levels.
- It's classified as a leading indicator because it arrives monthly, well before lagging official statistics, with the new orders sub-index typically turning first and getting the closest attention.
- Manufacturing and services PMI are published separately, and a divergence between them is often a more useful signal of a cyclical turn than either reading alone.
- Stock prices tend to react to the surprise relative to consensus expectations rather than the absolute PMI level — the same mechanism that drives stock reactions to earnings surprises.
- Temporary distortions like pre-emptive inventory stockpiling can inflate headline readings, and different publishers (ISM vs. S&P Global, or Korea's BSI) use different methodologies that can disagree in the same month.

## FAQ

### What's the difference between ISM PMI and S&P Global PMI?
Both measure U.S. manufacturing or services activity through surveys, but they're separate indicators with different respondent panels, calculation methods, and release timing. ISM's index has traditionally been the more widely cited U.S. figure, while S&P Global applies a consistent methodology across many countries, making its PMI easier to compare internationally. The two can diverge in the same month, so it's worth checking which publisher's figure you're looking at.

### Where is Korea's PMI published?
S&P Global publishes a Korea manufacturing PMI every month. Separately, the Bank of Korea compiles its own Business Survey Index (BSI), a Korea-specific sentiment gauge centered on 100 rather than 50, using its own survey methodology. Both serve a similar purpose — turning surveyed business sentiment into a number — but they aren't the same indicator and shouldn't be compared directly.

### If PMI crosses above 50, is that a buy signal?
No — this lesson explains how and why PMI is constructed and used as a leading indicator, not a trading rule tied to any specific threshold. As covered above, what tends to move stock prices is the surprise relative to consensus rather than the absolute level, and PMI is generally read alongside other economic and rate indicators rather than used as a standalone signal.

### Is the flash PMI or the final PMI more accurate?
The flash PMI is a preliminary estimate based on a partial sample (typically 75-85% of that month's responses), while the final PMI reflects the complete sample. The final figure is technically more complete, but markets often react to the faster-arriving flash reading regardless. A large gap between the flash and final figures is sometimes itself read as a signal that conditions shifted later in the month.
