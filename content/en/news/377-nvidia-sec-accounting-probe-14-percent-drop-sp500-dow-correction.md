## What Happened

All three major U.S. indexes closed sharply lower on Friday, November 20, capping off a brutal week on Wall Street. The S&P 500 dropped 265.44 points, or 3.70%, to finish at 6,910.39 - its seventh consecutive losing session and its worst week since April 2025, with the index down 8.6% for the five trading days. The Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 949.73 points, or 1.93%, to 48,147.33, while the Nasdaq Composite tumbled 1,261.47 points, or 5.28%, to close at 22,617.73. Perhaps the most striking detail buried in those numbers: that single session was enough to push the Nasdaq Composite into negative territory for all of 2026, wiping out a year's worth of AI-driven gains in one trading day.

The number that mattered most was 10%. Both the S&P 500 and the Dow fell more than 10% below their recent highs, which by Wall Street's standard definition means both indexes officially entered a "correction." That's not a loose figure of speech - a correction specifically refers to a decline of 10% to 20% from a recent peak, as distinct from a "bear market," which starts at a 20% drop. Crossing that line matters because it signals a shift in how investors are framing this year's AI-led rally: no longer a temporary wobble, but something that might represent an actual change in trend.

The proximate trigger was Nvidia (Nasdaq: NVDA). Reuters reported Friday, citing people familiar with the matter, that the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission is examining Nvidia's accounting practices. Reuters said it could not determine when the inquiry began or precisely which accounting items are under scrutiny, and neither the SEC nor Nvidia offered an on-record comment. That single report was enough to send Nvidia shares down $20.45, or 14%, to close at $125.53 - erasing roughly $500 billion in market capitalization in one day. The irony is hard to miss: just three days earlier, on November 17, Nvidia had reported quarterly revenue up more than 100% year-over-year, comfortably beating Wall Street's estimates. This selloff wasn't about the size of the numbers Nvidia reported - it was about whether investors can still trust how those numbers are being reported.

## Why One Accounting Report Took Down the Whole Market

To understand why this hit so hard, it helps to know that Friday's Reuters report didn't appear out of nowhere. The Wall Street Journal had already reported on November 17 that the SEC had opened a civil investigation into Nvidia, examining whether the company's disclosures gave investors an accurate picture of its business - though that earlier report didn't specify an accounting focus. That inquiry traces back to November 12, when Nvidia founder and CEO Jensen Huang abruptly stepped down after a board investigation into his personal relationship with a former executive at Singapore-based Megaspeed, a relationship U.S. intelligence officials reportedly believed carried ties to Chinese intelligence. The board named CFO Colette Kress as interim CEO in Huang's place.

That backdrop matters because Friday's Reuters report didn't just add a new piece of bad news - it shifted the center of investor anxiety from a leadership scandal to something more fundamental: the reliability of the financial statements themselves. A CEO's personal conduct can, at least in theory, be separated from a company's underlying business performance. Questions about accounting practices cut much deeper, because they call into question the revenue and profit figures investors have been relying on all along. Some reporting has suggested the probe may be connected to a recent change in how Nvidia discloses revenue by geography - notably a shift in which Singapore now accounts for nearly half of reported global sales - a detail that could also intersect with long-running concerns about whether chips are reaching China through indirect channels in violation of export controls. None of that connection has been officially confirmed by the SEC or by Reuters, and it's worth being explicit about that uncertainty rather than treating it as settled fact.

The reason a single stock's trouble could pull the entire S&P 500 and Dow into correction territory comes down to index weighting. Nvidia is one of the largest-weighted names in the S&P 500, sitting alongside the handful of "Magnificent Seven" megacap tech stocks that collectively exert outsized influence over how the broader index moves on any given day. Beyond its direct index weight, Nvidia has functioned all year as the symbolic face of the AI rally, so when confidence in Nvidia wavers, it tends to ripple outward - into the "neocloud" companies like CoreWeave and Nebius that depend on Nvidia financing, and into AI infrastructure spending more broadly. That ripple effect was visible the same day: Anthropic (Nasdaq: ANT), which had gone public just one day earlier, fell more than 9% further on its second trading day, extending a selloff that had nothing directly to do with Nvidia's accounting but everything to do with broad-based nervousness about AI valuations.

There's also a structural reason markets reacted this forcefully to a story with so many unconfirmed details: the uncertainty itself is the problem. A civil SEC inquiry can take months or years to resolve, and it can end with no finding of wrongdoing at all. Facing that kind of open-ended timeline, investors tend to price in worse-case scenarios first and ask questions later, especially for a stock like Nvidia that has carried an unusually high valuation built on assumptions about years of continued hypergrowth. When a crack appears in the trust underlying that valuation story, the price can move far more violently than the probability of an actual adverse outcome might justify on its own.

## What to Take Away From This

- **"Correction" is a defined threshold, not a vibe.** It specifically means a 10% to 20% decline from a recent high; cross 20% and the label changes to "bear market." Knowing which line an index has actually crossed helps you separate real regime-change signals from ordinary headline noise.
- **A credibility question is a different kind of risk than an earnings miss.** Nvidia beat estimates just three days before this plunge, yet a single report about how its numbers are being accounted for did more damage than the earnings reaction itself. The size of the numbers and investors' trust in those numbers are two separate variables - and when trust cracks, strong headline figures won't hold the stock up.
- **An SEC probe report means an inquiry has opened, not that wrongdoing has been found.** Many civil investigations close with no enforcement action at all. That said, the open-ended uncertainty itself - not knowing how long the process will take or how it will end - can act as a persistent drag on a stock regardless of the eventual outcome.
- **Heavy index weighting means single-stock risk becomes portfolio-wide risk.** The fact that one company's accounting headline pulled both the S&P 500 and the Dow into correction illustrates that owning a broad index fund doesn't fully insulate you from concentrated risk in a handful of megacap names.
- **Stocks in the same sector can fall together without any company-specific news.** Anthropic had no fresh negative headline of its own on Friday, yet it dropped sharply anyway as nervousness about Nvidia spilled over into the broader AI trade. When a position you hold drops, it's worth asking whether something actually happened to that specific company, or whether it's simply being dragged down by sector-wide sentiment.

## FAQ

### What's the actual difference between a "correction" and a "bear market"?
Both terms describe how far a stock index has fallen from its most recent high. A decline of 10% to 20% is classified as a correction; a decline of 20% or more is classified as a bear market. As of November 20, the S&P 500 and the Dow had crossed into correction territory but had not yet reached the deeper bear-market threshold.

### How is an SEC civil investigation different from a criminal case?
SEC investigations are typically civil proceedings that, if they result in any finding at all, usually end in fines or orders requiring a company to change specific practices going forward. Criminal charges are handled separately by the Department of Justice and generally require evidence of intentional fraud. What has been reported so far concerning Nvidia is a civil inquiry at the SEC, and it remains entirely unclear what, if anything, that inquiry will ultimately find.

### How far is Nvidia stock down from its 2026 high?
Nvidia was already more than 30% below its October peak when it reported earnings on November 17. Friday's additional 14% drop widens that cumulative decline from the October high considerably further. For the precise current percentage, it's best to check Nvidia's October closing high against its latest quote directly, since both figures continue to move.

### What does it mean that the Nasdaq Composite "turned negative for the year"?
It means the index's November 20 closing level fell below where it stood at the close of trading on the last day of 2025. In other words, a year's worth of gains built up through the AI-driven rally were entirely offset by the string of declines through November - Nvidia's leadership turmoil, renewed worries about chip export restrictions, and now this accounting-probe report - raising the real possibility that 2026 could end as a losing year for the index after being solidly positive for most of it.
