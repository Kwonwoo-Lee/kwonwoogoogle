## What Happened

US stocks closed out a heavy week on Friday, September 18, absorbing the second-largest "triple witching" expiration on record without much drama. The Dow Jones Industrial Average slipped 0.18% to 51,682.64, the S&P 500 edged up 0.17% to 7,650.50, and the Nasdaq Composite gained 0.39% to 26,522.55. According to Citadel Securities, roughly $7 trillion in notional options value - about a quarter of the entire US options market - expired simultaneously that day, topping the prior record of $5.7 trillion set back in March by more than $1.3 trillion. That expiration landed just two days after the Federal Reserve raised its benchmark rate a quarter point to a range of 3.75%-4.00% on September 16-17, its first hike since July 2023, while the Bank of Japan lifted its own policy rate to 1.25%, the highest level since 1995. With two major central banks tightening in the same week, bond-market volatility was already elevated heading into a session that also had to digest a record-setting options unwind.

Against that backdrop, the single biggest catalyst for the week of September 21-25 is Thursday's White House summit between President Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping. Timed around the UN General Assembly, the meeting marks Xi's first state visit to Washington in more than a decade, and reports indicate Trump himself plans to greet Xi at the airport - an unusually high-profile gesture that mirrors the red-carpet treatment Xi extended during Trump's own state visit to Beijing in May. Bloomberg has reported that Washington is likely to delay announcing a new round of tariffs targeting Chinese "excess capacity" goods until after the summit; those tariffs, reportedly around 7.5 percentage points, would push total US duties on Chinese imports to roughly 20% if layered on top of existing levies. On paper, the summit's central question is whether the two sides will extend the trade truce struck in Busan last October - currently set to expire November 10 - but the AI-slowdown debate that erupted among Silicon Valley's top executives over the past week is shaping up to be a bigger factor in the conversation than originally expected. Rounding out the week, Friday, September 25 brings August durable goods orders, the final September University of Michigan consumer sentiment reading, and earnings from Costco Wholesale.

## Why This Week Carries Outsized Weight

The Trump-Xi meeting matters to markets because it isn't just ceremony - real trade-policy decisions are riding on it. The truce that began at last October's Busan summit and continued through Trump's May state visit to Beijing rests on China resuming rare-earth exports and boosting US soybean purchases in exchange for American tariff exemptions and permission for some AI chip exports. With that truce's November 10 expiration now just five weeks away, whether this week's summit produces a signal of extension is the single question investors are most focused on. Three unresolved flashpoints - Taiwan arms sales, the Scarborough Shoal dispute, and Japan's Taiwan-related security doctrine - remain unaddressed beneath the surface, so a smooth outcome is far from guaranteed. Traders say they'll be watching Treasury yields, the dollar, and semiconductor stocks in the summit's aftermath as the cleanest read on sentiment: falling yields, a softer dollar, and strength in China-exposed chip names would suggest the market sees lower trade and inflation risk, while the opposite combination would suggest the summit fell short of expectations.

What makes this particular summit more charged than a typical trade check-in is the AI-slowdown fight that has consumed Wall Street over the past week. On Saturday, September 12, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei published a lengthy essay arguing "we must slow the pace at which we improve the capabilities of AI models," and OpenAI's Sam Altman and xAI's Elon Musk both publicly voiced agreement in the days that followed - a convergence that briefly sent Nasdaq 100 futures down more than 1.2% and coincided with OpenAI shelving its planned 2026 IPO. That debate hasn't cooled off this week; if anything, it has become one of the agenda items expected to come up between the two presidents. The complication is that Washington and Beijing don't even agree on what the biggest AI risk actually is. China's state-run Global Times has already dismissed the American AI executives' slowdown calls as a "Cold War playbook" aimed at throttling China's technological progress. If the summit exposes that gap rather than bridging it - producing friction on AI policy instead of even a token gesture of cooperation - megacap AI stocks that are already carrying stretched valuations could see another bout of volatility.

On the data side, two Friday releases stand out. The preliminary September University of Michigan consumer sentiment reading came in at 47.8, well below the 51.0 consensus estimate, marking a second straight monthly decline and a drop of 7.3 points from a year earlier. Rising fuel prices and trade-related uncertainty appear to be weighing heavily on how households view their near-term finances and the broader economy. The question for Friday's final reading is whether it confirms that deterioration or ticks back up modestly. The same day's August durable goods orders report will show whether the strength seen in July - a 1.1% month-over-month increase that comfortably beat the 0.4% consensus - carried into August. Individual earnings also dot the calendar: AutoZone, KB Home, and Thor Industries report Tuesday, and Costco Wholesale reports Thursday, meaning a marquee geopolitical event and a cluster of consumer-facing earnings land in the same five trading days.

Taken together, this week has a structure where a single Thursday event - the Trump-Xi summit - is likely to set the tone for the days around it. Expect a more cautious, wait-and-see tenor through Monday to Wednesday as positioning ahead of the summit dominates, followed by a Friday where the market's initial read on the summit gets cross-checked against hard data on consumer sentiment, durable goods, and Costco's read on the health of the American shopper. Layered on top of last week's synchronized Fed-BOJ tightening and a record options unwind, this week adds a geopolitical and trade overlay - raising the odds that accumulated volatility doesn't fully resolve within the week and instead carries into the one that follows.

## What to Take Away From This

- **When an event's timing is locked in well in advance, watch the expectation-setting that happens beforehand more than the event itself.** Just as the tariff-delay report leaked out ahead of the summit, markets typically price in expectations through the headlines that surface in the days before a scheduled event, and the actual outcome's size relative to those expectations - not the outcome in isolation - tends to drive the price reaction.
- **When two counterparties frame the same issue in fundamentally different terms, "agreement" often ends up being two sides talking past each other.** The gap between how Washington and Beijing each characterize the AI risk illustrates why headline talks on a shared agenda item don't guarantee the substantive progress markets might be hoping for.
- **Sentiment surveys like Michigan's consumer index tend to lead hard consumption data rather than confirm it.** A reading of 47.8 reflects how households feel before that pessimism (or its absence) shows up in actual retail sales or personal consumption expenditure figures, making it a useful early signal for interpreting whatever consumption data follows.
- **In weeks where several catalysts stack up at once, resist the temptation to let any single event define the whole week's narrative.** With a Fed and BOJ hike, a record options expiration, a presidential summit, and consumer data all landing within roughly seven trading days, volatility is more likely to persist across the full stretch - or spill into the following week - than to resolve cleanly around one date.

## FAQ

### Is there a real chance tariffs come down after the Trump-Xi summit?
Nothing is confirmed yet. Reports that Washington plans to delay a new round of tariffs on Chinese "excess capacity" goods until after the summit suggest trade tensions are unlikely to escalate further in the immediate run-up, at least. The bigger question is whether the summit produces any signal that last October's Busan truce - due to expire November 10 - will be extended.

### What does the "AI slowdown" debate have to do with this trade summit?
Over the past week, CEOs at Anthropic, OpenAI, and xAI all voiced support for slowing the pace of AI capability development, a shift that briefly hit Nasdaq 100 futures. That debate has become entangled with the broader US-China tech rivalry, so how the two presidents handle AI policy at Thursday's summit could influence near-term volatility in megacap AI names regardless of what happens on tariffs.

### Why does a consumer sentiment reading of 47.8 matter for the stock market?
Consumer sentiment surveys function as a leading indicator of household spending intentions before that shows up in hard data. A reading of 47.8, well below the 51.0 consensus, signals households are growing more pessimistic about their near-term financial position amid rising fuel costs and trade uncertainty - a signal worth weighing against Friday's final reading and the actual consumption data that follows in subsequent weeks.
