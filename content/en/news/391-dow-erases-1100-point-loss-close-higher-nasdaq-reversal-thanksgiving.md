## What Happened

Wall Street showed two completely different faces in a single session on Wednesday, November 25, the day before Thanksgiving. As we reported earlier ([Dow Craters 1,000+ Points, Nasdaq Slides 4% as Hot October PCE Meets Trump's New Tariffs](/en/news/stocks-sink-october-pce-hot-trump-tariffs-nvidia-nasdaq-correction/)), the morning session opened in near-panic mode after October core PCE inflation came in at 3.3%, hotter than the 3.2% economists expected, compounded by a White House announcement of new 20% tariffs on 14 trading partners including China and the EU. The Dow Jones Industrial Average plunged more than 1,100 points intraday - over 2% - while the S&P 500 fell as much as 3.2% and the Nasdaq Composite slid as much as 4.6%.

By the closing bell, though, the scorecard looked nothing like the morning's panic. The Dow actually finished up 164.25 points, or 0.33%, at 50,182.95 - erasing its entire 1,100-plus-point intraday loss within a single session. The S&P 500 clawed back most of its losses but still ended the day down 0.25% at 7,182.78, and the Nasdaq Composite closed down 0.62% at 23,585.80. In other words, the Dow flipped to green while the S&P 500 and Nasdaq both finished in the red - three major indexes delivering three different outcomes on the exact same trading day.

What's even more striking is what happened beneath the index-level numbers. According to multiple reports, roughly three-quarters (75%) of S&P 500 constituents actually rose on the day. That means the headline index fell even though most individual stocks gained - a sign that a small handful of mega-cap technology names, not the broader market, drove the index-level decline. Semiconductor and AI megacaps struggled the hardest. Nvidia (NASDAQ: NVDA) sank as much as 8% intraday before paring more than half that loss to close down roughly 4%. Memory chipmaker Micron (NASDAQ: MU) also finished down around 4%, and enterprise software giant Oracle (NYSE: ORCL) closed down roughly 4-5%.

## Why the Dow Erased 1,100 Points in a Matter of Hours

A reversal of this scale within a single session is rarely explained by one factor alone. In this case, three mechanisms worked together.

The first was sector rotation. The true epicenter of the morning panic was high-multiple semiconductor and AI growth stocks. As the afternoon progressed, investors pulled money out of those names and rotated into sectors less sensitive to rate and tariff worries - energy, industrials, healthcare, and consumer staples. This is essentially a continuation of the Dow-Nasdaq decoupling we covered the day before ([Dow Reclaims 50,000 - Why Micron Fell 11% While Nvidia Dropped Only 6%](/en/news/dow-reclaims-50000-micron-nvidia-chip-selloff-rotation/)), extending for one more day. Because the Dow is price-weighted and carries heavier exposure to industrial and healthcare names than to chipmakers, it was positioned to benefit disproportionately from this rotation even as semiconductor stocks stayed weak.

The second factor was thin pre-holiday trading volume. In the session right before Thanksgiving, many institutional traders square their positions early and head out, leaving overall market volume noticeably lighter than usual. In a thin market, both buying and selling pressure move prices more per dollar traded than they would on a normal day - which likely amplified both the morning's plunge and the afternoon's sharp rebound. Had volume been at its usual level, neither the morning panic nor the afternoon reversal might have been quite this dramatic.

The third factor was bargain-hunting at oversold levels. An intraday drop of 8% in Nvidia and 3.2% in the S&P 500 within a few hours tends to signal that a large chunk of near-term selling pressure has already been exhausted. Given that both the Nasdaq and S&P 500 are on pace for their worst November since 2008, traders betting on a bounce rather than further downside appear to have stepped in aggressively near the intraday lows. Together, rotation, thin volume, and oversold bargain-hunting let the Dow erase its entire 1,100-point loss within a single trading day.

## November's Numbers, and What Comes Next

Despite the dramatic reversal, November as a whole still looks rough. The S&P 500 is down roughly 5% for the month, and both the S&P 500 and Nasdaq remain on pace for their worst November since the 2008 financial crisis - meaning one day's sharp rebound wasn't nearly enough to undo a month's worth of losses. U.S. markets were fully closed on Thursday, November 26, for Thanksgiving, and will close early at 1 p.m. Eastern Time on Friday, November 27. That half-day session, which coincides with Black Friday, is traditionally one of the lowest-volume trading days of the year - worth keeping in mind, since prices can swing more than usual on relatively light buying or selling.

## What to Take Away From This

- **An index and "the overall market" can tell different stories.** The S&P 500 fell, yet roughly 75% of its constituent stocks rose. When a handful of mega-cap names dominate an index's direction, reading the headline number alone can give a misleading picture of how most stocks actually performed.
- **Different indexes can move in opposite directions on the same day.** The Dow rose while the Nasdaq and S&P 500 fell, reflecting differences in sector composition and index construction (price-weighted versus market-cap-weighted). This kind of decoupling isn't unusual during rotation-driven sessions.
- **Thin trading volume amplifies reversals in both directions.** Around holidays, when volume is unusually light, smaller buy or sell orders can move prices more than they would on a typical day. Treating a holiday-period plunge or rebound with the same weight as a normal trading day's move risks overreacting to noise.
- **Extreme oversold conditions can seed sharp rebounds.** Nvidia's round trip from an 8% intraday drop to a roughly 4% close shows how quickly a beaten-down stock can claw back ground on modest good news or opportunistic buying. Whether that marks a genuine trend change or just a one-day bounce can only be judged against the bigger picture - in this case, a month that is still shaping up as one of the worst Novembers in over a decade.

## FAQ

### Why did the Dow rise while the Nasdaq and S&P 500 fell on the same day?
The Dow Jones Industrial Average is price-weighted and carries relatively heavier exposure to industrial and healthcare names, while the Nasdaq and S&P 500 are market-cap-weighted and far more influenced by mega-cap tech stocks like Nvidia. When investors rotated out of semiconductor and AI growth names into energy, industrials, and healthcare during the afternoon of November 25, that structural difference let the Dow alone flip positive.

### If 75% of S&P 500 stocks rose, why did the index itself fall?
The S&P 500 is weighted so that larger companies have a bigger impact on the index's movement. Because a handful of mega-cap names like Nvidia, Micron, and Oracle still fell sharply (roughly 4-5%), the index closed lower even though most of its smaller-weighted constituents gained. It's a clear example of how an index's headline move and the market's overall "breadth" can send conflicting signals.

### Does this rebound signal a trend reversal?
It's too early to say. Despite the sharp one-day bounce, the S&P 500 is still down roughly 5% for November, and both the S&P 500 and Nasdaq remain on pace for their worst November since 2008. Since thin pre-holiday trading volume likely exaggerated the size of the rebound, it's worth watching how the market behaves once volume normalizes in the following week.
