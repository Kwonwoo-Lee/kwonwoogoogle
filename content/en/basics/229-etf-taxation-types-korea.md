## Same S&P 500 Exposure, Three Different Tax Bills

As covered in [What Is an ETF?](/en/basics/etf-basics/), an ETF packages a basket of holdings into something that trades like a single stock on an exchange. That resemblance to a stock leads a lot of investors to assume ETF taxation works just like stock taxation — but it doesn't. Two ETFs that both track the S&P 500 can face completely different tax treatment depending on whether one is listed on a Korean exchange and the other directly on a U.S. exchange. Even among Korean-listed ETFs, the rules split again depending on what the fund actually holds underneath. This lesson walks through the three tax tracks an ETF investor in Korea actually runs into — domestic-equity ETFs, Korea-listed ETFs holding foreign/bond/commodity assets, and foreign-listed ETFs — and shows, with real numbers, how differently they land.

## Track 1 — Domestic-Equity ETFs: Trading Gains Are Tax-Exempt

An ETF that holds at least 60% Korean-listed stocks (a KOSPI 200 ETF, a KOSDAQ 150 ETF, and similar funds) is treated exactly like an individual Korean stock for tax purposes. As covered in [Korea's Capital Gains Tax on Stocks](/en/basics/capital-gains-tax-stocks-korea/), trading gains on domestic-listed shares are tax-exempt for ordinary retail investors, and domestic-equity ETFs inherit that same exemption. Sell a KOSPI 200 ETF at a profit and you owe nothing on the gain, and ETFs are also exempt from the securities transaction tax that applies to selling individual stocks. The one tax that does apply regardless is on distributions: any dividend-like payout the ETF makes is subject to a flat 15.4% dividend withholding tax. So the shorthand for this track is: tax-free on trading gains, taxed on distributions — the same picture as holding Korean dividend stocks directly.

## Track 2 — Domestic-Listed "Other" ETFs: Trading Gains Get Taxed as Dividend Income

The complexity starts with ETFs that are listed on a Korean exchange but hold foreign equities, bonds, commodities, or derivatives underneath — a Korea-listed S&P 500 fund, a Korea-listed Nasdaq 100 fund, a gold futures ETF, and the like. The listing venue is identical to Track 1, but the tax treatment goes in a completely different direction. Trading gains here are classified not as capital gains but as **dividend income**, taxed at a flat 15.4% withholding rate instead of being exempt. Distributions are taxed the same way, at 15.4%. In other words, listing on a Korean exchange doesn't by itself buy you favorable tax treatment — the moment the underlying holding shifts to foreign equities, bonds, or commodities, trading gains get pulled into the taxable bucket too.

The concept worth understanding here is **holding-period taxation**. Tax authorities calculate a separate daily benchmark value for each ETF (its "tax base NAV"), and the taxable amount is whichever is **smaller**: the actual gain realized at sale, or the increase in that benchmark value over the holding period. It might seem like an unnecessary extra step, but the point is to avoid double-taxing income the fund already paid out — and already withheld tax on — through distributions during the holding period. In practice the two figures are often close, but funds that distribute more along the way tend to show a smaller benchmark-value increase than the raw price gain, which can work in the investor's favor. One more structural difference matters here too: because this trading gain is classified as dividend income, the ₩2.5 million annual deduction available to overseas-listed-stock gains does not apply at all.

It's worth understanding why this split exists in the first place. The underlying policy logic is roughly: if a product gives you exposure that's economically equivalent to holding the underlying asset directly, it should be taxed similarly to holding that asset directly. A Korean investor who buys foreign stocks or bonds directly pays capital gains tax or interest income tax on the proceeds. If a Korea-listed ETF wrapping those same foreign assets fully exempted trading gains the way a domestic-equity ETF does, investors could effectively launder foreign or commodity exposure through an ETF wrapper to dodge that tax. So the tax code classifies ETFs by what they actually hold, and funds holding non-domestic-equity assets get their trading gains swept into dividend income under the provision that treats collective investment vehicle profits that way.

## Track 3 — Foreign-Listed ETFs: Capital Gains Tax at 22%

ETFs listed directly on a foreign exchange — SPY, QQQ, VOO, and the rest of what Korean retail investors who trade U.S. markets ("seohak-gaemi") typically buy — fall under yet another framework. The same rule covered in [Korea's Capital Gains Tax on Stocks](/en/basics/capital-gains-tax-stocks-korea/) for overseas stocks applies here: annual trading gains get a ₩2.5 million deduction, and the remainder is taxed at a flat 22% (including local tax). Unlike the automatic withholding on domestic dividend income, no brokerage withholds this for you — the investor has to calculate their own annual gain or loss (January 1 through December 31) and file a self-assessment return the following May through Hometax. Missing that filing triggers a penalty, so this is a filing obligation foreign-ETF traders need to actually remember. Distributions from these ETFs are treated separately as dividend income and withheld at 15.4% (or adjusted after U.S.-side withholding).

## The Numbers Side by Side: The Same ₩5 Million Gain, Three Different Bills

The clearest way to see how different these tracks really are is to run the same gain through all three. Say an investor earns exactly ₩5,000,000 in trading gains over a year.

- **KOSPI 200 ETF (Track 1)**: trading gain is tax-exempt → tax owed: ₩0
- **Korea-listed S&P 500 ETF (Track 2)**: ₩5,000,000 × 15.4% ≈ **₩770,000**
- **U.S.-listed VOO (Track 3)**: (₩5,000,000 − ₩2,500,000 deduction) × 22% ≈ **₩550,000**

Zero, 770,000, and 550,000 won on an identical gain — purely because the three tracks start from different definitions of what counts as taxable. The crossover point between Track 2 and Track 3 is simple algebra: let x be the gain. Track 2's tax is 0.154x, and Track 3's tax is (x − 2,500,000) × 0.22. Setting them equal — 0.154x = 0.22x − 550,000 — solves to x ≈ ₩8,330,000. Below that gain level, the ₩2.5 million deduction gives the foreign-listed track (Track 3) the edge; above it, the deduction's relative benefit shrinks and the flat 15.4% rate on the domestic-listed foreign ETF (Track 2) starts to win out. One brokerage has cited roughly this ₩8.33 million figure as a rule of thumb, though it's a back-of-envelope estimate from the tax formulas rather than an official threshold, so investors trading larger amounts should run their own numbers. Either way, the takeaway is that "Korea-listed ETFs are always the tax-friendlier choice" simply isn't true once the underlying holding stops being Korean equities.

## Distributions Run on a Separate Track, No Matter Which ETF You Hold

A common point of confusion is lumping trading gains and distributions together. They don't move together at all. The tax treatment of trading gains (exempt, dividend tax, or capital gains tax) differs by track, but **distributions** are taxed at a flat 15.4% dividend tax rate in both Track 1 and Track 2, with no exception, and distributions from Track 3 (foreign-listed) ETFs are likewise classified as dividend income. So don't assume a tax-exempt trading gain means tax-free distributions, and don't assume a taxed trading gain means distributions get some kind of offset. The two run on entirely independent rule sets.

## Where This Meets the ₩20 Million Financial-Income Threshold

As covered in [Dividend Income Tax and Comprehensive Financial Income Taxation](/en/basics/dividend-income-tax-explained/), once an individual's combined interest and dividend income exceeds ₩20 million a year, the excess gets folded into other income and taxed at progressive rates up to 49.5%. This threshold matters for ETF investors because Track 2's trading gains and distributions — along with distributions from every track — are classified as dividend income and count toward that ₩20 million calculation. Track 3's trading gains, by contrast, are taxed separately as capital gains and don't count toward the threshold at all, though distributions from Track 3 ETFs still do, since those are dividend income regardless of listing venue. For an investor already close to the ₩20 million line from other financial income, the choice of which track to hold the same underlying exposure in can meaningfully change how fast they cross into the higher progressive bracket.

## Sidestepping the Complexity With Tax-Advantaged Accounts

It's also worth knowing that account choice can bypass much of this complexity. Inside an [ISA account](/en/basics/isa-account-korea/), gains and losses across different holdings — including ETF trading gains and distributions — are netted together, taxation is deferred until withdrawal at maturity, and a portion is tax-exempt or taxed at a reduced flat rate up to certain limits. Trading ETFs inside a pension account — [pension savings or an IRP](/en/basics/pension-savings-irp-tax-credit-korea/) — similarly defers taxation until withdrawal rather than triggering Track 2's holding-period tax on every sale, and withdrawals are generally taxed at a lower pension-income rate than ordinary dividend income. For an investor who trades Track 2 ETFs frequently — where every trading gain gets taxed as dividend income and counted toward the financial-income threshold — holding those same ETFs inside an ISA or pension account rather than a regular brokerage account can meaningfully reduce the long-run tax drag.

## Takeaway

- ETF taxation in Korea isn't a single rulebook — it splits into three tracks depending on what the fund holds and where it's listed, not just whether it "trades like a stock."
- Domestic-equity ETFs owe no tax on trading gains; Korea-listed ETFs holding foreign/bond/commodity assets owe 15.4% dividend tax on trading gains too (holding-period taxation); foreign-listed ETFs owe 22% capital gains tax after a ₩2.5 million deduction.
- Distributions are taxed at 15.4% across every track, independently of how trading gains are taxed — don't assume the two move together.
- Below roughly ₩8.3 million in annual gains the foreign-listed track tends to come out ahead on tax; above it, the domestic-listed foreign-ETF track can win out — so "direct overseas investing is always more tax-efficient" isn't a safe assumption at every gain level.
- ISA and pension accounts can sidestep much of this complexity and meaningfully lower the long-run tax burden on frequently traded ETFs.
- This lesson explains the mechanics of how ETF taxes are structured — it isn't a recommendation to buy any specific fund. Always confirm the current rules before filing or making tax-driven decisions.

## FAQ

### How do I find out which tax track my ETF falls into?
Check the ETF's detail page on your brokerage app or the fund's prospectus/fact sheet from the asset manager, which typically labels the fund as domestic-equity, foreign, or derivatives-based and states the applicable tax treatment. Many fund managers also post this directly on their website, so it's worth checking before buying.

### If a domestic-listed foreign ETF loses money, do I still owe tax?
No — if you actually realize a loss at sale, there's no taxable gain on that ETF for the year. But keep in mind that trading gains on Track 2 ETFs (classified as dividend income) can't be netted against losses on other stocks in the same account and year. That kind of loss-netting is only available inside tax-advantaged accounts like an ISA.

### Are leveraged and inverse ETFs taxed differently?
Most leveraged and inverse ETFs use derivatives and fall under the "other" classification, which usually means Track 2's holding-period taxation and 15.4% dividend tax apply. That said, the exact classification can still depend on whether the underlying index is domestic equities or foreign/commodities, so check the specific fund's prospectus.

### Do [currency-hedged (H) and unhedged (UH)](/en/basics/currency-hedged-vs-unhedged-etf/) ETFs get taxed differently?
Currency hedging itself isn't what determines the tax track — what matters is whether the underlying holding is domestic equities or foreign/bond/commodity/derivative assets. So a hedged and an unhedged version of the same foreign-index ETF are typically both classified under Track 2 regardless of the hedging difference.
