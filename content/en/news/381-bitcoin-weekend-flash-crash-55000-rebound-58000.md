## What Happened

In the early hours of Saturday, November 21 (U.S. Eastern Time), bitcoin went through another violent swing. After trading near $59,000 the previous evening, it slid to roughly $55,100-$55,326 within a matter of hours - depending on the exchange and the moment measured - its lowest level since September 2024. What stands out even more than the drop itself is what happened immediately after: within just a few hours of bottoming out, bitcoin clawed its way back above $58,000, with some venues showing prices brushing against $60,000. A sharp crash and a sharp rebound, both inside the same half-day window - a textbook "flash crash."

The move was driven by measurable forced selling. Leveraged futures and margin positions get automatically liquidated by exchanges once price falls past a trader's maintenance margin, and in this case roughly $2.4 to $2.7 billion worth of positions were wiped out within 24 hours, affecting more than 340,000 accounts. This is already the latest in a string of similar liquidation events this month: a cascade on November 16 pushed bitcoin down to around $62,000, and on November 20 alone it fell 5.6% to close near $59,326. Saturday's drop to the $55,000s extends that same downward trajectory one notch further, and puts bitcoin more than 56% below the roughly $126,200 record it set in October 2025.

What makes this particular move worth noting is that it happened while the conventional stock market was entirely closed. Unlike the NYSE or Nasdaq, bitcoin trades every hour of every day, including weekends. Trading volume during the overnight weekend window is typically far thinner than during weekday sessions, and in that kind of thin-liquidity environment, a relatively modest sell order can move the price disproportionately - and once the price does move, cascading liquidations of leveraged positions can turn that initial move into something much larger. The fact that the rebound came almost as fast as the crash supports a reading that much of the move reflected mechanical liquidations in thin liquidity rather than a genuine shift in conviction. Analyst reaction was split: some dismissed the swing as little more than a "rounding error" against the broader trend, while others treated the weekend's selling as a warning sign of more to come.

## Why a Crash and a Rebound Happened in the Same Few Hours

To understand this properly, it helps to start with a structural fact about crypto markets. The NYSE and Nasdaq open only during specific weekday hours and close entirely on weekends and holidays, but bitcoin and other major cryptocurrencies trade continuously, across both centralized and decentralized exchanges, with no closing bell. That means any shock that would otherwise hit stocks between Friday's close and Monday's open - a window when equity investors have no way to react - tends to get absorbed first by bitcoin's price. For investors holding equity positions over a weekend, bitcoin's price action can function as an early, imperfect read on how risk appetite is shifting before the stock market even opens.

The liquidation mechanism itself is fairly mechanical. In futures and margin trading, a trader can take on a position far larger than their own capital using borrowed funds, and the exchange is contractually required to force-close that position once the price breaches a maintenance-margin threshold - regardless of what the trader wants to do. Once price slips below a key support level, leveraged long positions clustered near that level get liquidated simultaneously, adding fresh sell orders that push price down further and trigger the next wave of liquidations at the next support level. When this compounds quickly, it produces the kind of rapid drop known as a liquidation cascade. The same logic explains why the move eventually reverses: once price falls far enough, fast enough, leveraged short positions betting on further declines start getting liquidated too, which mechanically becomes buying pressure, and dip-buyers waiting on the sidelines step in at the same time, pushing the price back up quickly. Saturday's crash and rebound, in other words, were not two separate events - they were one continuous move, amplified in both directions by leverage operating in thin weekend liquidity.

This matters for U.S. equity investors, not just crypto traders. Strategy (Nasdaq: MSTR), which holds a large bitcoin treasury, and Coinbase (Nasdaq: COIN), the largest U.S.-listed crypto exchange, have both been trading in near lockstep with bitcoin's decline throughout November. The weekend's additional drop to the $55,000s and subsequent rebound functions as a leading indicator for how crypto-exposed names are likely to open when Monday's session begins. The timing adds another layer: this week is a Thanksgiving-shortened trading week in which October's PCE inflation reading, the revised Q3 GDP figure, and Deere's earnings all land on a single day, November 25, with the results widely viewed as a key input into whether the Fed raises rates at its December 9-10 FOMC meeting. Markets are already pricing in a meaningful probability of a December hike, and an asset like bitcoin that generates no yield at all tends to become relatively less attractive as that probability rises - a dynamic that analysts flagged as part of the backdrop to this weekend's volatility, alongside a dollar index that has strengthened in recent months and continued outflows from U.S. spot bitcoin ETFs.

## What to Take Away From This

- **A crash that happens in thin overnight liquidity can be an exaggerated signal, not a clean read on sentiment.** When trading volume is light, a relatively small sell order can move the price disproportionately - and the same thinness that amplifies the drop can amplify the bounce back. Before reacting to an extreme price printed in the dead of a weekend night, it's worth comparing it against regular weekday trading levels.
- **Leverage liquidation cascades work the same way on the way up and the way down.** A break below a key support level triggers forced selling from long positions, which pushes price lower; an oversold bounce triggers forced buying from liquidated shorts, which pushes price higher just as mechanically. Recognizing that a large share of sharp moves comes from position structure rather than new information helps avoid over-interpreting every spike as meaningful news.
- **A 24/7-traded asset can offer an early, if imperfect, read on risk appetite while the stock market is closed.** What happens to bitcoin over a weekend can hint at how crypto-adjacent stocks might open on Monday, but it's a reference point, not a guarantee that the two markets will move in the same direction.
- **When analysts openly disagree about what a move means, don't anchor to either take too firmly.** The fact that this weekend's crash was simultaneously called "noise" by some and a "warning sign" by others tells you that conviction either way is premature right now. When credible voices disagree, it's reasonable to weigh both arguments while keeping position sizes conservative rather than betting heavily on one interpretation.
- **Monetary policy expectations directly affect the relative appeal of yield-free assets like bitcoin.** As the December FOMC decision approaches, volatility tends to rise in rate-sensitive assets. Anyone holding bitcoin should recognize that its price is exposed to macro variables like interest rates and the dollar, not just crypto-specific headlines.

## FAQ

### Why did bitcoin crash and then rebound within the same few hours?
Overnight weekend hours are among the thinnest-liquidity windows in crypto trading. When bitcoin broke below a key support level during that thin window, leveraged long positions were liquidated in a cascade that pushed the price down further. Once the decline became extreme enough in a short span, the dynamic flipped: short positions betting on continued declines were themselves liquidated, and bargain-hunting buyers stepped in, pushing the price back up quickly. Both the crash and the rebound stemmed from the same combination of thin liquidity and leverage.

### How far below its all-time high is bitcoin after this crash?
Measured against the roughly $126,200 record set in October 2025, Saturday's low in the $55,000s represents a decline of more than 56%. Since bitcoin has since rebounded into the high $50,000s/low $60,000s, the exact current percentage should be checked against the latest live price.

### Does this bitcoin crash affect the U.S. stock market?
The direct impact is concentrated in U.S.-listed companies with explicit bitcoin exposure, such as Strategy and Coinbase, along with bitcoin mining stocks and ETF holders. The broader S&P 500 and Nasdaq are affected more indirectly, through shared risk sentiment. How crypto-exposed stocks react right at Monday's open, November 23, is the most immediate thing to watch.

### What does the December FOMC meeting have to do with bitcoin?
Bitcoin generates no dividends, interest, or other cash flow. When interest rates rise, safer yield-bearing alternatives like Treasuries become more attractive by comparison, which raises the opportunity cost of holding a zero-yield asset like bitcoin. As markets price in a higher probability of a rate hike at the December 9-10 FOMC meeting, that tends to add selling pressure on bitcoin and other non-yielding assets.
