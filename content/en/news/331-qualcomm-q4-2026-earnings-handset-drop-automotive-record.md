## What Happened

After the closing bell on Wednesday, November 4, Qualcomm (NASDAQ: QCOM) released its fiscal fourth-quarter and full-year 2026 results, and on paper the headline numbers looked solid. Adjusted earnings per share came in at $2.17, edging past a consensus of roughly $2.16, while non-GAAP revenue of about $10.44 billion topped Wall Street's estimate in the $10.1-10.2 billion range. And yet the stock sold off sharply in after-hours trading. Different outlets put the drop anywhere from 2% to 9%, but the figure that showed up most consistently was a decline of roughly 6-7% from a regular-session close near $173, pushing shares down toward the $160 level. A beat that sends the stock falling is always worth a second look, and in this case the answer was buried inside the revenue mix, not the headline.

The culprit was Qualcomm's largest business line: chips for handsets. QCT handset revenue fell 26% year over year to about $5.14 billion, missing the roughly $5.25 billion analysts had modeled. In the very same quarter, automotive chip revenue told the opposite story, surging 89% year over year to nearly $1.99 billion - the company's first-ever quarter above the $2 billion mark - comfortably beating the $1.69 billion estimate. The IoT segment added a smaller but still positive data point, up 4% year over year to $1.888 billion. For the full fiscal year, Qualcomm's revenue came in around $43.1 billion, down about 3% from $44.28 billion in fiscal 2025.

Guidance for the new fiscal year split the same way. Qualcomm guided first-quarter fiscal 2027 revenue to a range of $10.4 billion to $11.2 billion, with the roughly $10.8 billion midpoint coming in above the roughly $10.55 billion consensus. But adjusted EPS guidance of $2.15 to $2.35, with a $2.25 midpoint, landed below the roughly $2.27-2.32 analysts had expected. In short: more revenue, but thinner profit per share. The company reiterated its $5 billion data-center AI chip revenue target for fiscal 2027 and said it expects data-center revenue above $650 million in the first quarter alone.

## The Mechanism Behind the Split

The single thread connecting every one of these numbers is the global memory chip shortage that has run through nearly all of 2026, and Qualcomm's results are its clearest confirmation yet. Surging demand for high-bandwidth memory used in AI data centers has pushed memory makers like Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron to redirect production lines away from conventional DRAM and NAND and toward HBM and high-capacity server memory instead. That left ordinary memory used in smartphones and PCs tight in supply and sharply more expensive - TrendForce has tracked contract prices for DRAM and NAND rising multiple times over in the past year, with the first quarter of 2026 alone producing the steepest quarterly price jump on record.

That squeeze hit Qualcomm through two separate channels. First, as the cost of the memory chips that go inside a finished phone climbed, handset makers like Samsung and Xiaomi pulled back on chipset orders to protect their own margins, which flowed straight through to Qualcomm's handset revenue line. Second, this coincided with Apple's continuing push to replace Qualcomm modems with its own in-house silicon, meaning the memory-driven demand slowdown and an ongoing loss of Apple business landed on the handset unit at the same time. Gartner has projected that memory-driven cost pressure could pull global smartphone shipments down roughly 8% in 2026, while Counterpoint Research expects the shortage to push average smartphone selling prices up by about 6.9%. When consumers buy fewer, pricier phones, the chipmaker supplying the silicon inside those phones feels it directly.

The automotive unit's record quarter is explained by the mirror-image logic. Semiconductors used in automotive electronics - infotainment systems, digital cockpits, driver-assistance systems - are far less exposed to spot memory pricing, since automakers typically lock in pricing through long-term supply contracts negotiated well in advance. On top of that, the ongoing shift toward electric and connected vehicles is structurally increasing the amount of semiconductor content per car, which let the automotive segment keep growing even while the handset business buckled. Put together, what actually spooked investors wasn't the quarter itself - it was the signal that weakness in Qualcomm's single largest business is going to keep weighing on next quarter's profitability. A guidance combination of higher revenue but lower EPS reads as "revenue keeps growing, but margins keep getting squeezed," and that's the interpretation that drove the after-hours selloff.

## What to Take Away From This

- **Headline EPS and revenue beats can mislead if you stop there.** Qualcomm cleared both consensus numbers, and the stock still fell. What mattered more was the composition underneath: which segment missed, and whether next quarter's guidance pairs higher revenue with lower profitability.
- **The same macro variable can cut opposite ways inside one company.** The identical memory-price spike that crushed Qualcomm's handset unit barely touched its automotive business, which runs on long-term contracts and structurally rising content-per-vehicle demand. Break a company's exposure down by business line rather than treating "the company" as a single unit facing a single macro force.
- **Read revenue guidance and margin guidance separately.** A combination of raised revenue guidance and lowered EPS guidance can look like "growth continues" on the surface, but it's often a warning that cost pressure is building. Markets tend to react more sharply to the EPS side of that pairing, as they did here.
- **Structural supply-chain shifts play out over several quarters, not one.** Research firms including Gartner and Counterpoint expect the AI-driven memory shortage to persist well into 2027. Qualcomm's handset weakness this quarter is likely one data point in a longer-running trend rather than a one-off event.

## FAQ

### If Qualcomm beat on both EPS and revenue, why did the stock fall?
The headline EPS and revenue figures did clear consensus, but the company's largest business line - handset chips - fell 26% and missed its own revenue estimate, while next quarter's EPS guidance midpoint of $2.25 came in below the roughly $2.27-2.32 analysts expected. Investors reacted more to the forward profitability signal than to the backward-looking beat.

### Is the memory chip shortage really the main reason handset revenue fell 26%?
It's the biggest single factor cited across the reporting, but not the only one. Surging demand for AI data-center memory pushed up prices for the ordinary DRAM and NAND used in phones, leading handset makers to trim chip orders. That's compounding a separate, longer-running trend: Apple's continued shift toward designing its own modem chips instead of buying Qualcomm's.

### Why didn't the automotive chip business get hurt by the same memory shortage?
Automotive semiconductor pricing is typically locked in through long-term supply contracts negotiated well ahead of delivery, insulating it from the kind of spot-price spikes that hit consumer electronics. Combined with structurally rising chip content per vehicle as cars become more electrified and connected, that let the automotive unit post its first-ever $2 billion quarter even as the handset business struggled.
