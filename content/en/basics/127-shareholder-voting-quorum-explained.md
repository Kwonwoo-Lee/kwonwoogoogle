## Why Does a Proposal Fail Even When Almost Everyone Who Voted Said Yes?

Anyone who has read a Korean company's shareholder meeting notice has probably run into this puzzle: one proposal with overwhelming support still gets flagged as "failed for lack of quorum," while another with plenty of dissent sails through. The difference isn't the yes-to-no ratio — it's a completely separate question of how many shares actually showed up to vote in the first place. Every agenda item at a Korean listed company's shareholder meeting has to clear two tests at once: what share of the votes actually cast said yes, and what share of all outstanding shares that "yes" represents. On top of that, Korea abolished "shadow voting" entirely in 2018, which briefly turned hitting quorum into a real headache for listed companies, and electronic voting stepped in to fill that gap. Then in 2025, lawmakers passed an amendment making cumulative voting mandatory for large companies — changing the arithmetic behind how minority shareholders can actually get a director elected. This lesson walks through how the voting math works, why quorum nearly broke down, and what these rules mean for investors today.

## The Basic Unit — "One Share, One Vote," and the Second Test Called Quorum

Under Korean corporate law, voting rights are allocated one vote per share as a baseline rule: the more shares you hold, the more weight your vote carries. But not every issued share counts toward that total. Treasury shares the company itself holds, non-voting preferred shares, and cross-held shares between affiliated companies whose voting rights are restricted are all excluded from the share count used in quorum calculations — for voting purposes, it's as if they were never issued. For a resolution to pass, two conditions have to be satisfied simultaneously: what fraction of the votes actually cast (in person, by proxy, or electronically) voted yes, and what fraction of all eligible outstanding shares that "yes" vote represents. The first measures agreement among those who showed up; the second is a hard floor on how much of the whole company actually had to participate. That second condition is quorum, and no matter how unanimous the room is, a resolution simply doesn't pass if too few shares took part relative to the legal threshold.

## Ordinary vs. Special Resolutions — Different Bars for Different Stakes

Korean law sets two tiers of resolution depending on how consequential the agenda item is. Routine matters — electing directors or auditors, approving financial statements, setting dividends — go through as an "ordinary resolution," requiring a majority of votes cast plus support from at least one-quarter of all outstanding shares (Commercial Act Article 368). Matters that reshape the company itself — amending the articles of incorporation, a merger or spin-off, transferring a major part of the business, or [a capital reduction](/en/basics/capital-reduction-explained/) — require a "special resolution": at least two-thirds of votes cast and at least one-third of all outstanding shares (Article 434). That's a meaningful jump on both axes — from a simple majority to roughly two-thirds on the votes-cast side, and from one-quarter to one-third on the total-shares side.

| | Votes cast threshold | Outstanding shares threshold | Typical agenda items |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ordinary resolution | Simple majority | At least 1/4 | Director/auditor elections, financial statement approval, dividends |
| Special resolution | At least 2/3 | At least 1/3 | Charter amendments, mergers/spin-offs, [capital reduction](/en/basics/capital-reduction-explained/), business transfers |

This gap matters most at companies where the controlling shareholder's stake is relatively thin. A controlling shareholder sitting around 20% can usually push ordinary resolutions through without needing other shareholders to show up, but a special resolution requires building a coalition — the higher bar forces in other institutional or minority votes almost by design. It's no accident that items like [merger exchange ratios](/en/basics/merger-swap-ratio-explained/), where minority interests can diverge sharply from the controlling shareholder's, are routed through the special-resolution track.

## The Gap Left by Abolishing Shadow Voting

Even with reasonable thresholds on paper, actual retail shareholder turnout at Korean AGMs has historically run low. The mechanism that quietly covered for that was "shadow voting": the Korea Securities Depository would cast absent shareholders' votes in the same yes/no proportion as the shareholders who actually showed up, which in effect auto-filled quorum. The catch was obvious — it let someone else's vote stand in for shareholders who never expressed an opinion at all — so regulators decided in late 2014 to abolish it, with a grace period to let companies adjust before it was fully phased out starting with the 2018 annual meeting season. A study commissioned by Korea's Ministry of Justice around that transition found that a large share of resolutions processed via shadow voting in the lead-up to abolition would not have cleared quorum at all without it. Once it was gone, smaller listed companies with widely dispersed share registers started actually failing to seat directors or auditors for lack of quorum, sometimes forcing a second meeting. The gap showed up most sharply for auditor elections, where a separate rule already caps how much of a controlling shareholder's stake counts toward the vote.

## Electronic Voting — Filling the Gap, With Limits

Electronic voting became the main substitute. Once a company writes the option into its articles of incorporation, shareholders can authenticate through the Korea Securities Depository's online system and cast a real yes/no vote on each agenda item without attending in person. It is fundamentally different from shadow voting: shadow voting stood in for shareholders who said nothing, while e-voting records an actual decision by the shareholder themselves, so it counts as genuine participation toward quorum. But it's opt-in per company, not mandatory, and adopting it doesn't automatically translate into higher turnout — early on, only roughly half of listed companies offered it, and even among those, the share of retail shareholders who actually used it stayed modest. To close that remaining gap, the 2025 Commercial Act amendment requires listed companies with total assets of at least 2 trillion won to hold a hybrid "electronic shareholder meeting" — an in-person meeting held alongside real-time online participation — starting in January 2027. A fully online meeting with no physical venue still isn't permitted; only the hybrid format is. As of the end of 2025, roughly 210 listed companies (201 on the KOSPI, 9 on KOSDAQ) met that asset threshold.

## Cumulative Voting — How the 2025 Amendment Changes the Math for Minority Shareholders

If e-voting addressed the participation gap, the 2025 amendment that passed the National Assembly in August also changed something more structural: how votes get allocated once cast. The key piece is mandatory cumulative voting. Under the standard one-share-one-vote system, if three director seats are up for election at once, a shareholder can only cast shares-worth of votes for each individual candidate — meaning a controlling shareholder with a simple majority can usually push through every nominee on their own slate. Cumulative voting changes that: each shareholder instead receives a pool of votes equal to shares held multiplied by the number of seats being filled, and can concentrate that entire pool on a single candidate. A shareholder with 100 shares voting on three seats gets 300 votes to allocate however they like — including putting all 300 behind one nominee. That opens a path for minority shareholders to pool their votes and seat at least one director of their choosing, even without a majority stake. The mechanism isn't new — companies could already opt in through their own articles — but almost none did voluntarily. The 2025 amendment makes it mandatory for listed companies with at least 2 trillion won in assets, effective one year after promulgation. The same package expands mandatory separate election of audit committee members from one seat to two, and tightens the existing "3% rule" that caps how much of a controlling shareholder's and related parties' combined stake counts toward audit-committee votes. How the details play out in practice is still something to watch as implementing regulations are finalized.

## What to Check in a Shareholder Meeting Notice

When a notice for a stock you own lands in your inbox, start by sorting each agenda item into ordinary or special resolution. For special resolutions, check whether the controlling shareholder's stake alone is enough to clear the two-thirds/one-third bar, or whether other institutional or minority votes are actually needed. Next, check whether the company offers electronic voting and, if so, the window and method specified in the notice. For director elections, check whether cumulative voting applies and, if it does, think through where you'd want to concentrate your own shares-times-seats vote pool. For stocks caught up in an [activist campaign](/en/basics/shareholder-activism-explained/) or a [takeover defense](/en/basics/anti-takeover-defenses-explained/) situation, proxy advisors' recommendations and the stance of large institutional holders like the national pension fund can swing both quorum and the final vote. And even a small individual stake voted electronically genuinely adds to the participation total that quorum is measured against — it's worth checking how much of the stock's free float sits with the controlling shareholder's group versus large outside institutions, since that balance is often the clearest signal of whether a given vote will actually be close.

## Key Takeaways

- A shareholder resolution needs both a sufficient yes-share among votes actually cast and a sufficient yes-share of all outstanding shares — falling short on either one fails the resolution.
- Ordinary resolutions (majority of votes cast, 1/4 of outstanding shares) and special resolutions (2/3 of votes cast, 1/3 of outstanding shares) set different bars depending on how significant the agenda item is.
- Abolishing shadow voting in 2018 left some companies unable to hit quorum, and electronic voting became the main fix.
- Companies with at least 2 trillion won in assets must hold hybrid electronic meetings starting January 2027, and the same size threshold now makes cumulative voting mandatory under the 2025 amendment.
- Cumulative voting lets a shareholder pool "shares held × seats being filled" votes behind a single candidate, widening the path for minority shareholders to influence director elections.

## FAQ

### Does one small shareholder's electronic vote actually matter for quorum?
Yes. Quorum is measured against the share of all outstanding shares that actually participated, not the yes/no ratio among those who showed up, so every share voted electronically adds directly to that participation total. The effect is largest at companies where the controlling shareholder's stake is relatively low and retail ownership is high.

### If I vote electronically, do I still need to attend the meeting in person?
No — electronic voting lets you cast your vote online within a set window before the meeting, and once you've voted that way, there's no need to attend in person. The availability and window for e-voting vary by company, so check the specific meeting notice first.

### Does cumulative voting guarantee minority shareholders can elect a director?
Not automatically. Cumulative voting provides a mechanism for concentrating votes — it doesn't increase the total number of votes a minority coalition has to work with. If the combined votes of minority shareholders are smaller than what the controlling shareholder can spread across several candidates, the outcome can still go the controlling shareholder's way. The real determinant is how much stock minority holders can coordinate and how unified their voting intent actually is.
