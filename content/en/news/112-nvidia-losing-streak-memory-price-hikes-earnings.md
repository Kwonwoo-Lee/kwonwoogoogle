## What Happened

Nvidia (NASDAQ: NVDA) shares fell more than 2% intraday on Monday, August 24, extending a losing streak that has now run six straight sessions. The stock last closed higher on August 13, and from August 14 through August 21 it fell every single day, sliding from $225.30 to $214.75 - a drop of roughly 4.7%. That six-session skid is Nvidia's longest run of consecutive declines since September 2022, when the stock fell for seven straight sessions and shed 24% of its value, hitting its lowest level since March 2021. This year's decline is far milder in magnitude, but Monday's continued weakness raised the possibility that the streak could stretch to a seventh day and match the length of the 2022 run.

Nvidia's slide isn't happening in isolation - it's part of a broader chip-sector pullback. The iShares Semiconductor ETF (SOXX), which tracks the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index, fell nearly 3% on Monday alone, and peers including AMD and Qualcomm dropped more than 9% the same day. The weakness in chip stocks weighed on the Dow, S&P 500, and Nasdaq, all of which traded mixed to lower as a result. What makes the timing especially notable is that Nvidia reports fiscal second-quarter earnings after the close on Wednesday, August 26 - just two days away. Company guidance calls for revenue of $91 billion, plus or minus 2%, which would represent roughly 80% year-over-year growth from $46.7 billion a year earlier. Wall Street's consensus sits slightly above that midpoint, at around $91.7 billion in revenue and non-GAAP EPS near $2.08. A six-day losing streak heading into a quarter that guidance already points to strong growth is an unusual combination worth understanding.

## Why Good News Turned Into a Headwind

Two related forces are driving the pullback. The first is that surging memory-chip costs are squeezing the economics of the very systems Nvidia's chips go into. Bloomberg reported on August 22 that some of Nvidia's largest customers have been notified that prices for servers built around its AI chips will rise more than 15% in many cases, effective on systems shipped starting early next year. The notice came from the contract manufacturers that assemble Nvidia-based servers for hyperscale data-center operators including Microsoft, Google, and Oracle. The size of the increase depends on chip generation and memory configuration, and it hits both the current Grace Blackwell platform and the upcoming Vera Rubin platform. According to industry estimates, the combined cost of HBM4 and LPDDR5X memory as a share of a server rack's total bill of materials has jumped from roughly 5-10% under Blackwell to 25-30% under Vera Rubin - a 435% increase in dollar terms, from about $370,000 to roughly $2 million per rack.

That number matters because Nvidia designs and sells GPUs - it doesn't manufacture the memory chips going into the systems around them. Rising memory costs don't add to Nvidia's own revenue; instead, they raise the total cost of the finished server systems that data-center operators actually have to buy, squeezing those customers' budgets. If server prices rise, hyperscalers may be able to purchase fewer units within a fixed capital-expenditure budget, which raises the possibility of softer demand for Nvidia's chips down the line. What the market is really reacting to isn't a near-term revenue hit - it's rising doubt about whether the AI infrastructure buildout is peaking sooner than investors had assumed.

The second factor traces back to a major financing announcement on August 10. Nvidia said it was partnering with six major Wall Street firms - Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs, and KKR - to build a financing platform capable of raising more than $500 billion in outside capital to help customers purchase its computing hardware. On the surface, that looks like a demand-supportive move. But it has also revived concerns about "circular financing" - the idea that if Nvidia is directly or indirectly helping fund its own customers' purchases, the resulting revenue growth may partly reflect financing engineering rather than pure end-market demand. Nvidia's six-day losing streak began right around this announcement, suggesting the two threads - rising input costs squeezing customer economics, and questions about the quality of demand behind the growth numbers - are compounding each other in investors' minds heading into earnings.

## What to Take Away From This

- **News that looks positive for one link in a supply chain can be negative for another.** Rising memory prices are good news for memory makers like Micron, but bad news for the Nvidia ecosystem that has to buy that memory to build finished servers. The same price move can send opposite signals depending on where a company sits in the supply chain.
- **Look past the "longest streak in years" headline to the actual magnitude.** This six-day, 4.7% decline is roughly one-fifth the size of the 24% drop Nvidia suffered over seven days in 2022. A record-length losing streak by session count doesn't automatically mean a comparably severe decline - check the percentage move before assuming the worst.
- **Large financing deals can carry both a demand-supportive story and a structural red flag at the same time.** Nvidia's $500 billion financing platform widens the pool of buyers who can afford its hardware, but it has also opened the door to circular-financing scrutiny. The bigger the deal, the more worth watching whether follow-up analysis picks apart its structure rather than just its headline number.
- **Stock action heading into earnings reveals what the market has already priced in.** Nvidia guided to roughly 80% year-over-year growth, yet shares fell for six straight sessions right before the print. That pattern suggests investors are approaching this earnings report with real caution - and it's a reminder that beating consensus doesn't guarantee a stock will rally if expectations were already stretched or if the market is focused on cost and demand-quality questions instead of the headline growth number.

## FAQ

### Will this losing streak continue after the August 26 earnings report?
No one can say for certain, but earnings reports have historically been a common trigger for breaking a stock's prevailing trend, in either direction. This time, though, the streak is tangled up with structural questions - rising memory costs squeezing server economics and circular-financing concerns around the $500 billion deal - so even a headline beat on revenue and EPS may not be enough on its own. Watch data-center margin trends, forward guidance, and any management commentary on memory costs to judge whether the trend is actually turning.

### If memory prices are rising, why is Nvidia's stock falling instead of memory makers' stock rising more?
Nvidia designs and sells GPUs; it doesn't manufacture the memory chips that go alongside them in a server. When memory costs rise, the total price of a finished AI server system goes up, which pressures the budgets of the data-center operators that are Nvidia's actual customers - a cost headwind rather than a revenue tailwind for Nvidia. Memory producers like Micron and SK Hynix, by contrast, typically benefit directly when memory prices climb.

### What exactly is the risk with "circular financing"?
It refers to a structure where a supplier helps finance its own customers' purchases, and that money then flows back to the supplier as revenue. This isn't inherently improper, but it raises a valid analytical concern: reported revenue growth may partly reflect financing arrangements rather than pure organic demand from end customers. Investors evaluating a company's growth rate in this kind of situation need to try to separate genuine end-customer demand from growth that's supported by the company's own financing activity.
