## Two Companies, the Same 15% ROE — Are They Really the Same?

Once you've learned the [financial statement basics](/en/basics/financial-statement-basics/), it's tempting to assume that a higher ROE (return on equity) always means a better company. But suppose Company A and Company B both post a 15% ROE. Are they making money the same way? Company A might be pocketing a fat margin on every unit it sells. Company B might run on razor-thin margins but turn its inventory over fast enough to make up for it through sheer volume. Or maybe both companies run ordinary operations, and Company B simply borrowed more money to push its ROE number up. A single ROE figure can't tell these three stories apart. **DuPont analysis**, devised in the 1920s by Donaldson Brown, a finance executive at the chemical company DuPont, breaks that one ROE number into three separate gears — net margin, asset turnover, and financial leverage — revealing exactly where that 15% came from.

## The DuPont Formula — Splitting ROE Into Three Pieces

ROE is originally just net income divided by shareholders' equity. DuPont analysis rewrites that ratio by inserting revenue and total assets between the numerator and denominator, turning it into three ratios that appear to cancel out but don't actually change the answer.

**ROE = Net Margin × Asset Turnover × Financial Leverage**

Written out in full, that's (Net Income ÷ Revenue) × (Revenue ÷ Total Assets) × (Total Assets ÷ Shareholders' Equity). Revenue and total assets each cancel out once in the numerator and denominator, so the result is mathematically identical to the original ROE (Net Income ÷ Equity). This isn't a new piece of information pulled out of thin air — it's the same single number viewed through three different lenses. Each piece answers a different question: net margin asks "how much does the company keep from every dollar of sales?", asset turnover asks "how efficiently does it turn its assets into sales?", and financial leverage asks "how much of that result is built on borrowed money rather than its own capital?"

## What Each of the Three Pieces Actually Tells You

**Net margin** is net income divided by revenue after all costs and taxes are subtracted, and it reflects operating efficiency and pricing power. Companies that can command premium prices relative to their costs — luxury brands, software companies — tend to post a high net margin. **Asset turnover** is revenue divided by total assets, and it measures how efficiently a company converts what it owns into sales. Businesses like big-box retailers or convenience store chains, which run on thin margins but cycle inventory and floor space quickly, tend to post a high asset turnover instead. **Financial leverage**, often called the equity multiplier, is total assets divided by shareholders' equity. As covered in [margin trading and leverage](/en/basics/margin-trading-leverage/), borrowing money amplifies the return generated on a given amount of equity for the same operating profit, and the equity multiplier is exactly the size of that amplification. Banks, which effectively run their business on deposits — a liability — as raw material, structurally post a much higher equity multiplier than most other industries.

## Seeing It in Numbers — Same ROE, Different Reasons

Here are three hypothetical companies that all land on the same 15% ROE through very different combinations: a luxury brand (Company P), a big-box retailer (Company Q), and a manufacturer that has been propping up its ROE with debt (Company R).

| Metric | Luxury Brand P | Big-Box Retailer Q | Manufacturer R |
|---|---|---|---|
| Net Margin | 15% (premium pricing) | 2% (thin margin, high volume) | 5% (ordinary) |
| Asset Turnover | 0.5x (asset-heavy relative to sales) | 3.75x (fast inventory cycle) | 1.0x (ordinary) |
| Financial Leverage | 2.0x | 2.0x | 6.0x (heavily levered) |
| ROE | 15% | 15% | 15% (propped up by leverage) |

Company P's ROE is driven almost entirely by one oversized gear — net margin. Company Q gets to the same place through an oversized asset turnover gear instead, despite a thin margin. Both P and Q carry an ordinary 2.0x leverage ratio, so their 15% ROE can be read as a genuine reflection of how their operations perform. Company R is different: its net margin and asset turnover are both clearly weaker than P's or Q's, yet a 6.0x leverage ratio pushes it to the same 15% ROE. Looking at the ROE figure alone, all three companies look equally strong — but once you decompose it, Company R's 15% turns out to be a product of heavy borrowing, not operational strength.

## Calculating the Three Pieces Yourself From the Financial Statements

DuPont analysis requires no special tools — anyone can calculate it directly from numbers already disclosed in a company's financial statements. Net margin is net income (from the income statement) divided by revenue. Asset turnover is revenue divided by total assets (from the balance sheet, typically averaged between the beginning and end of the period). Financial leverage, the equity multiplier, is total assets divided by total shareholders' equity. Multiply the three together and the result should match the company's reported ROE exactly; if it doesn't, check whether you're using period-end figures instead of averages, or whether [non-controlling interest](/en/basics/non-controlling-interest-explained/) is mixed into a consolidated statement you're working from. Tracking the three figures side by side every quarter turns this into a habit: whenever ROE moves, you can immediately see which gear moved it. Many brokerage platforms and financial data sites already list net margin, asset turnover, and debt ratios separately, so you often don't even need to calculate them by hand — just look them up side by side.

## Why an ROE Built on Leverage Is Riskier

Debt itself isn't the problem. The problem is that the ROE boost debt produces cuts both ways: it amplifies returns when business is good, and it amplifies losses just as much when business disappoints. As discussed in [ROIC vs. WACC](/en/basics/roic-vs-wacc-value-creation/), adding debt shrinks the equity denominator, so ROE can rise arithmetically even when operating efficiency hasn't actually improved at all. A company like Manufacturer R above, with an unusually high leverage ratio, is especially exposed when interest rates rise or sales slow down — interest expense eats into net income quickly, and ROE can collapse just as fast as it rose. In other words, the same 15% ROE carries very different risk depending on whether it comes from operations (margin and turnover) or from capital structure (leverage). A company with high leverage and weak margin and turnover at the same time is exactly the profile most likely to disappoint investors who judged it on ROE alone.

## Why the Gears Look Different Across Industries

Which of the three pieces dominates often reflects the underlying business model of the industry itself, not a company's individual skill. Industries with strong brand power or high barriers to entry — luxury goods, biotech, software — tend to post high net margins but relatively low asset turnover, since their asset base is small relative to what they sell. Retail chains, convenience stores, and trading companies run the opposite pattern: thin margins offset by high asset turnover from fast-cycling standardized goods. Banks and insurers post structurally high financial leverage because their business model runs on deposits or premiums — liabilities by nature — as raw material; that's normal within the industry but produces a misleading comparison if measured against a manufacturer's leverage on the same scale. This is why DuPont analysis is most useful for tracking how one company's ROE evolves over time, or comparing close competitors within the same industry — comparing the three components directly across unrelated industries rarely produces a meaningful conclusion. Comparing asset turnover between two convenience store chains, for instance, can flag which one is running less efficient stores; comparing the same figure against a bank or a semiconductor maker tells you nothing useful, because the business models themselves are entirely different.

## What to Check as an Investor

When you spot a stock with a high or recently improving ROE, it's worth decomposing it before taking the number at face value. If net margin improved, check whether that came from something durable, like cost discipline or pricing power. If asset turnover improved, check whether it reflects real efficiency gains, like better inventory management or higher facility utilization. But if financial leverage has visibly crept up over the last few quarters while net margin and asset turnover stayed flat, that's worth treating with suspicion — it may mean ROE is being propped up with borrowed money rather than genuine operating improvement. Analyst reports and financial summaries often show only the headline ROE trend, so calculating net margin, asset turnover, and the equity multiplier yourself for the past several years, and lining them up side by side, makes it far clearer which gear is actually driving a company's ROE.

This habit matters most right when a headline announces a "record ROE" after earnings. Every such headline looks like good news on the surface, but a record ROE driven by margin and turnover both improving together is a completely different story from a record ROE built on flat net income and a shrinking equity base from debt-funded buybacks or dividends. As covered in [stock splits and buybacks](/en/basics/stock-splits-and-buybacks/), a shrinking share count or equity base can lift per-share metrics on its own, so it's worth checking whether a capital-structure shift is hiding behind an ROE headline before treating it as pure operating strength.

## Key Takeaways

- DuPont analysis rewrites ROE as Net Margin × Asset Turnover × Financial Leverage, splitting one number into three separate causes.
- Net margin reflects operating efficiency, asset turnover reflects how efficiently assets are converted into sales, and financial leverage (the equity multiplier) reflects reliance on debt.
- The same ROE carries very different downside risk depending on whether it comes from operations (margin and turnover) or from capital structure (leverage).
- Because all three components are shaped heavily by industry structure, DuPont analysis works best for tracking one company over time or comparing close competitors — not for comparing unrelated industries directly.

## FAQ

### How is DuPont analysis different from ROA?
ROA (return on assets) is net income divided by total assets, which equals DuPont's net margin multiplied by asset turnover. In other words, ROA captures pure operating profitability with the leverage effect stripped out; multiplying ROA by financial leverage gets you back to the ROE shareholders actually experience. The bigger the gap between ROA and ROE, the more a company relies on leverage to generate its returns.

### Is high financial leverage always a bad sign?
Not necessarily. Some industries, like banking, are structurally leveraged by nature, and a company with stable cash flow taking on a reasonable amount of debt to improve capital efficiency is a normal financial strategy. The concerning pattern is when net margin and asset turnover stay flat or weak while leverage keeps climbing to prop up ROE.

### Can DuPont analysis be broken down further than three steps?
Yes. A widely used 5-step version further splits net margin into operating margin, interest burden, and tax burden. For most individual investors, though, the 3-step breakdown is usually enough to tell whether an ROE figure is coming from operations or from capital structure.

### Does improving just one of the three DuPont components always raise ROE?
In theory, yes — if the other two stay constant, improving any single component raises ROE arithmetically. In practice, though, the three components are often not independent. Raising prices to boost net margin can reduce volume and lower asset turnover; taking on more debt to raise financial leverage can increase interest expense and squeeze net margin. Using DuPont analysis well means checking whether an improvement in one component came at the expense of another.
