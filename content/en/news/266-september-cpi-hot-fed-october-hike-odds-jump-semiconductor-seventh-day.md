## What Happened

The "pivot point" this site flagged in yesterday's article turned into hard numbers on Wednesday, October 14, at 8:30 a.m. ET. September's Consumer Price Index rose 3.7% year-over-year, hotter than the 3.6% economists polled by Dow Jones had expected, and a clear step up from August's 3.4%. Core CPI, which strips out food and energy, also ran hot at 2.6% versus the 2.5% forecast. On a monthly basis, headline CPI jumped 0.6%, with gasoline prices surging roughly 7-9% for the month alone and accounting for more than half of the overall increase. As covered in yesterday's piece, odds of a hike at the October 27-28 FOMC meeting had collapsed from 70% to around 25% after NY Fed President John Williams said there was "no need for urgency." Within hours of the CPI release, those odds snapped back to roughly 45-51% - more than double where CME FedWatch had them just a day earlier, in the low-20s.

Bond markets reacted instantly. The 10-year Treasury yield, already hovering near its highest levels in roughly 24 years, pushed higher still, with CNBC attributing the move directly to the hotter-than-expected core inflation print. Equities opened lower and stayed there. By early-to-mid afternoon ET, the S&P 500 was trading roughly 0.7-0.9% below Tuesday's close near 7,805, down in the 7,730-7,750 range, while the Nasdaq Composite fell 1-1.7%, slipping below the 27,000 mark. The Dow Jones Industrial Average held up comparatively well, down only modestly, a divergence partly explained by the bank-earnings reactions discussed below (Bank of America and Morgan Stanley). The Nasdaq 100 fell roughly 1% intraday toward the 30,300 area.

Semiconductors sat at the center of the selloff. The iShares Semiconductor ETF (SOXX), which tracks the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index, fell another 2.3% intraday, extending its losing streak to a seventh straight session - a cumulative decline of nearly 10% from its October 6 close. AMD and Micron led the weakness, and even Nvidia slipped roughly 2% intraday. Not every headline was negative: Dutch chip-equipment maker ASML reported record third-quarter sales and raised its full-year guidance on strong AI demand, offering the sector a partial offset. Defensive health-care names provided a cushion elsewhere in the index, helping limit the broader market's decline.

## Why One Inflation Report Can Swing Rate-Hike Odds This Much

To understand why this CPI print carried so much weight, it helps to revisit what happened the two days before it. On Monday, October 12, Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack delivered a hawkish message calling for a restrictive policy stance. On Tuesday, NY Fed President John Williams pushed back, saying there was "no need for urgency." With two sitting FOMC voters sending contradictory signals, markets needed a tiebreaker - and Wednesday's CPI effectively handed one to Hammack's side of the argument. That matters because inflation and employment are the two variables the Fed weighs most heavily in setting policy, and this report sent an unambiguous signal on the inflation side: price pressure hasn't cooled as much as hoped. The fact that both headline and core came in hot simultaneously is harder to wave away as a one-off gas-price spike; it suggests broader price pressure that a single volatile category can't fully explain.

The mechanics behind the overnight doubling of hike odds trace back to how fed funds futures markets work. Traders holding positions that bet on where rates will be at upcoming FOMC meetings continuously reprice those bets as new data arrives, and a reading as directly tied to the Fed's reaction function as CPI gets repriced almost instantly. Traders who had priced in "no hike until December" as of Tuesday flipped a meaningful share of those bets back toward "October is live again" within hours of Wednesday's release - and that repricing is exactly what the headline probability captures. It's worth being precise about what that CME FedWatch number actually represents: it's the market's implied probability, not a Fed decision. The October 27-28 meeting is still two weeks away, and incoming jobs data or further Fed commentary could move the number again before then. Notably, CNBC pegged the odds of at least one hike by year-end at over 95% even after this report - suggesting the real debate in the market isn't "will the Fed hike again" but "at which specific meeting."

The simultaneous reaction in bonds and stocks follows the same underlying logic. With Treasury yields already near multi-decade highs, a higher probability of an additional hike leaves room for the 10-year to climb further still. Rising rates raise the discount rate used to value future cash flows, and that effect hits long-duration growth stocks - tech and semiconductors especially - far harder than value stocks generating profits today. That's the core reason the Nasdaq and the semiconductor ETF fell so much more than the Dow on the same piece of news. Chip stocks were already under pressure from a separate storyline - China's CXMT making progress on DRAM cell-design technology - and Wednesday's rate shock piled a second, unrelated headwind onto a sector already in its seventh consecutive losing session.

## What to Take Away From This

- **Rate-hike odds can more than double on a single data point.** October hike odds fell to roughly 25% after Williams' remarks, then snapped back to 45-51% a day later on the CPI print. Anyone positioning around a specific FOMC meeting should assume this kind of whiplash can continue repeating right up until the actual decision.
- **Longer-duration assets react more violently to rate surprises.** The Dow held up relatively well on the same CPI news that hammered the Nasdaq and semiconductor stocks - a direct illustration of how growth-stock valuations, built on cash flows further out in time, are more exposed to swings in the discount rate than industrial or financial names.
- **Headline and core inflation both matter, for different reasons.** Gasoline accounted for more than half of this month's headline increase, but the fact that core CPI - which strips out that volatility - also ran hot is what worried markets more. Learning to separate a one-off spike from a broader pricing trend is central to reading any inflation print correctly.
- **Stacked headwinds extend losing streaks.** Semiconductors' seventh straight down day wasn't caused by CPI alone; it combined with the pre-existing China memory-chip competitive threat. When analyzing why a sector keeps falling, look for a combination of pressures rather than searching for one single cause.
- **Good company-specific news doesn't always save a sector in a macro-driven selloff.** ASML's record quarter and raised guidance couldn't lift semiconductors broadly. When a rate shock or competitive-threat narrative is dominant, even strong individual earnings have limited power to turn sector sentiment around.

## FAQ

### Did this CPI report lock in a Fed rate hike for October?

No. The 45-51% figure from CME FedWatch is the market's implied probability based on futures pricing, not an actual decision. The real call will be made by FOMC members' votes at the October 27-28 meeting, and additional economic data or Fed commentary between now and then could move that probability again in either direction.

### Why did the Nasdaq fall so much more than the Dow on the same news?

Rising rates discount the value of future profits more heavily for growth and technology companies than for traditional industrial or financial firms, since growth-stock valuations depend more on earnings expected years from now. The Nasdaq is weighted more heavily toward these long-duration growth and semiconductor names, while the Dow skews toward industrials and financials - so the same rate-driven headline hits the two indexes very differently.

### Is the semiconductor sector's seventh straight losing day entirely because of CPI?

CPI was just one of several triggers. Semiconductor stocks were already weak heading into Wednesday because of reports that China's CXMT had made progress on DRAM cell-design technology. The rate shock from CPI added a second, separate headwind on top of that existing pressure, which is why the losing streak extended rather than being caused by a single factor.
