## What Happened

On Thursday, November 5, Wall Street suffered one of its most brutal sessions in years as fears about AI regulation, tied to a projected Democratic sweep of both chambers of Congress in the midterm elections, reignited a sell-off in tech stocks. The Dow Jones Industrial Average plunged 1,833.06 points (3.44%) to close at 51,523.06, the S&P 500 dropped 4.36% to 7,353.73, and the Nasdaq Composite cratered 5.52% to 24,315.00 - more than 10% below its record high, putting it officially in correction territory. The Magnificent Seven lost roughly $1 trillion in combined market value in a single day, the worst one-day loss since the April 2025 tariff shock. The market's shorthand explanation was simple: election results meant tougher regulation was coming for AI data centers. The very next day, a hot October jobs report (165,000 new payrolls, more than double the roughly 60,000 consensus forecast) helped reverse some of that panic, with the Nasdaq rebounding 1.09% to 24,579.42 and the S&P 500 adding 0.31% to close at 7,376.86. On the surface, it looked like a textbook one-day panic followed by a one-day bounce.

But on that same Thursday, something quieter - and arguably more important - happened away from the ticker tape. Dina Powell McCormick, Meta's president of global affairs, took the stage at the Wall Street Journal's Tech Live conference and publicly acknowledged that the midterm election results could make it harder for Meta itself to build new data centers. She said Democratic control of Congress would add friction to new construction, and admitted the industry has done a poor job of explaining the benefits data centers bring to local communities. She also connected the backlash to something broader: a general public unease about AI itself, not just about electricity bills or construction noise. Meta currently operates data centers across 23 states and has five gigawatt-plus projects underway, including one in Texas.

The next day, November 6, CNBC pulled these threads together into an analysis describing the data center fight as having become a "war of attrition" in the wake of the midterm shock. The core argument was straightforward: the real risk investors should be pricing in isn't a single federal bill that either passes or fails, but hundreds of separate, slow-grinding battles with local zoning boards, utilities, and municipal governments playing out simultaneously across the country. Fortune had reported similar friction back in October, noting that Google, Meta, Amazon, and Microsoft were all facing comparable local opposition at their own sites. Polling cited in that coverage found roughly two out of three Americans oppose having a data center built near where they live - evidence this isn't an isolated local dispute but a nationwide pattern.

## A One-Day Panic vs. a Multi-Quarter War of Attrition

Why does this distinction matter? Because stock-price panics and structural political risk operate on completely different timescales. Thursday's crash and Friday's rebound are a textbook example of headline-driven trading: a shift in vote-count narrative spooked futures one night, and a strong jobs report reversed much of that spook the following morning. Reports that oversold names like CoreWeave led Friday's bounce reinforce that this was a bargain-hunting rebound rather than any new fundamental catalyst - no data center construction actually accelerated overnight.

The risk Meta itself flagged runs on an entirely different clock. Local zoning hearings, grid interconnection negotiations, water-rights disputes, and the shifting positions of locally elected officials unfold over months or years, not hours. When an individual project slips by a quarter, that delay rarely shows up in tomorrow's share price. Instead, it tends to surface two or three quarters later, buried inside a hyperscaler's earnings call as softer-than-expected revenue recognition or a trimmed capital-spending guide. In other words, Friday's bounce undoing Thursday's panic didn't undo the structural risk Meta itself admitted to on Thursday - that risk is likely still quietly accumulating well below the surface of the index-level headlines.

One widely circulated figure adds color here, though it needs a caveat. Some financial media, citing Goldman Sachs estimates, reported that the election outcome could affect close to 45% of currently planned U.S. data center expansion. That number hasn't been confirmed directly in a Goldman research note that's publicly available, and it reached wide circulation mainly through secondary aggregators, so it should be treated as directional rather than precise. Still, the fact that it keeps getting repeated across outlets suggests market participants are already treating this as a multi-year capital-planning variable rather than a one-day news blip. Meta, for its part, said the projects that succeed tend to share a few traits: the company covering its own power costs, routing tax surpluses to local teachers and first responders, hiring locally, and securing bipartisan political support - which implicitly means projects lacking those features face a higher risk of delay.

Not everyone reads the situation the same way. The Motley Fool argued on November 6 that tighter regulation could, somewhat paradoxically, help the hyperscalers by capping their capital spending and boosting free cash flow, and drew a sharp distinction between a slowdown in U.S. construction specifically and a slowdown in global AI investment overall. Local backlash might delay specific American sites without meaningfully denting the broader worldwide AI buildout. For investors, the more useful approach isn't picking a side in that debate but tracking both signals over time: individual project timelines on one hand, and hyperscaler capex guidance on the other.

The nearest real test of this theory arrives next week. CoreWeave, the AI cloud infrastructure company that led Friday's rebound, is scheduled to report third-quarter earnings after the market closes on Tuesday, November 10 - and it's also one of the companies most directly exposed to construction and power-delivery timelines. Its backlog figures and forward guidance will be an early concrete data point on whether the "war of attrition" has actually started biting into results, or whether it's still mostly a headline-level fear.

## What to Take Away From This

The first lesson is that a one-day price rebound and the resolution of an underlying risk are not the same thing. Thursday's panic being largely unwound on Friday says nothing about whether the root cause - local-level resistance to data center construction - has gone away. Confusing the speed of price action with the speed at which real-world risk actually resolves is a common and costly mistake; they operate on entirely different clocks.

The second lesson is to weigh the credibility of who is raising the warning. A risk flagged by an outside analyst or pundit carries far less weight than the same risk acknowledged publicly by the company that benefits most from it. Meta is both the largest beneficiary and one of the largest spenders in the AI data center boom, so when its own president stands on a stage and admits the company's own expansion could get harder, that admission should be taken more seriously than ordinary market commentary.

The third lesson is that tracking a structural risk requires looking past the ticker and toward primary sources: local zoning board filings, utility interconnection queues, and the capital-spending language in the next round of hyperscaler earnings calls all carry a more honest signal than the Magnificent Seven's daily swings. Picking a concrete next checkpoint - like CoreWeave's November 10 earnings - and watching it directly is a far more productive habit than reacting to every overnight headline.

## FAQ

### Doesn't Meta's warning contradict Friday's stock rebound?
No. Friday's bounce reflects bargain-hunting in oversold names plus a short-term reaction to a stronger-than-expected jobs report. Meta's warning, by contrast, is about much slower-moving variables - local permitting, grid capacity, and public opinion - that a single day of price action can't undo. The two facts coexist on entirely different timescales.

### What exactly does "war of attrition" mean in this context?
It refers to a situation where no single federal regulation blocks the industry outright. Instead, hundreds of small, separate disputes with local governments, zoning boards, and utilities across the country accumulate over time. No individual fight is large enough to make headlines on its own, but together they can meaningfully slow the overall pace of construction - which is precisely why CNBC reached for that phrase.

### What's the next concrete event investors should watch?
The nearest checkpoint is CoreWeave's third-quarter earnings report, due after market close on Tuesday, November 10. Its order backlog and forward guidance should offer an early signal of whether construction and power-delivery delays tied to local opposition are starting to show up in actual results, rather than just in headlines. Capital-spending guidance from the other major hyperscalers in the following weeks is worth watching as well.
