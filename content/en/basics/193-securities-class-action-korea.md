## Thousands of Victims, but Only 12 Lawsuits in Twenty Years

Picture a listed company that filed a falsified annual report overstating its earnings, and the fraud only comes to light once the stock has already collapsed. The investors holding that stock when the truth came out could number in the thousands, scattered across the market with no way of even knowing who else was hurt. Suing individually rarely makes sense when a single investor's loss is smaller than the legal fees required to pursue it, so most people who could claim damages simply absorb the loss and move on. Korea's **Securities-Related Class Action Act**, passed by the National Assembly in 2003 and effective from January 1, 2005, was built to solve exactly this coordination problem: one victim becomes a "representative party," sues on behalf of everyone similarly affected, and the outcome binds the whole group — including people who never set foot in a courtroom. Yet according to a 2024 news report, only 12 such lawsuits had actually been filed in the law's first two decades on the books. A mechanism exists, and it's barely ever used. This lesson walks through what the law actually covers, the procedural hurdles that stand between a complaint and a verdict, and why Korea's experience looks so different from the US system it was loosely modeled after.

## What the Law Actually Covers

Article 3 of the Act doesn't open the door to any securities dispute a shareholder might want to bring. It's narrowly scoped to three categories: **false disclosure** (misstatements or omissions in a registration statement or business report), **fraudulent trading and price manipulation** (the kind of artificial price distortion covered in our lesson on [stock price manipulation](/en/basics/stock-price-manipulation-wash-trading/)), and **accounting fraud** (financial statements prepared in violation of accounting standards). In other words, this isn't a general-purpose tool for any shareholder grievance — it was built specifically for situations where false or manipulated information causes many investors to suffer the same harm at the same time. Where our lesson on [insider trading and fair disclosure](/en/basics/insider-trading-fair-disclosure-explained/) covers penalizing the party who held an informational edge, this law plays a different role: it's a civil remedy that lets the investors harmed by that distorted information recover damages directly from the company responsible.

## The Permission System: A Gate Before the Real Trial Even Starts

The most distinctive feature of Korea's version is its **permission system (소송허가제)**. Rather than filing suit and moving straight into a trial on the merits — the US pattern — a Korean court must first separately decide whether the case is even allowed to proceed as a class action, and only once that permission is granted does the actual case begin. Article 12 spells out what that requires: at least 50 class members; those members' combined holdings, measured at the time of the alleged wrongdoing, must equal at least one ten-thousandth of the defendant company's total outstanding securities; and the legal or factual issues at the heart of the case must be common to the whole group. The court holds a hearing with both sides before issuing its permission decision.

What stretches this process out further is that the **defendant can immediately appeal that permission decision**, and under Korea's civil procedure rules, filing that appeal automatically suspends enforcement of the lower court's ruling. A single appeal can freeze the entire case for years before the actual substance of the fraud allegation is ever examined. The law was meant to balance two goals at once — efficiently compensating large groups of harmed retail investors, while also guarding against frivolous, opportunistic suits — and in practice, critics argue the balance has tilted heavily toward the second goal at the expense of the first.

## After Permission Is Granted: Representative Plaintiffs and Opt-Out

Once a court grants permission, the real case begins. It's conducted not by every affected investor individually but by a court-appointed "representative party," represented by a licensed attorney. Because attorneys in these cases typically front the litigation costs themselves, few Korean law firms are willing to take on the financial risk of losing — a structural reason the mechanism sees so little use. Of the 12 cases filed to date, a single law firm is reported to have handled a majority of them.

When the court grants permission, it publishes notice of that decision, and this is where the **opt-out procedure** kicks in. Any class member who doesn't want to be bound by the outcome must file an exclusion notice within a set window; anyone who doesn't is automatically included, and the judgment's binding effect applies to them whether they ever appeared in court or not. That might feel unusual at first, but it's exactly this structure that lets a single representative plaintiff's lawsuit deliver compensation to thousands of people who never personally litigated anything.

## Why the US Files Hundreds a Year and Korea Files a Dozen in Twenty

The US equivalent runs on **Rule 10b-5** under the Securities Exchange Act of 1934, with procedure reshaped by the **Private Securities Litigation Reform Act (PSLRA)** of 1995. Cornerstone Research's tracking shows 226 new securities class actions filed in the US in 2024 and 207 in 2025. Set against Korea's cumulative total of 12 cases over twenty years, the gap in actual usage isn't a matter of degree — it's a different order of magnitude.

One structural driver of that gap is the permission system itself. US courts have no equivalent pre-screening gate: a filed case moves straight to the merits. Instead, the PSLRA governs how a **lead plaintiff** gets chosen — the first filer must publish notice inviting other investors to apply, and within 60 days, courts apply a rebuttable presumption that whichever investor (often an institution) suffered the largest financial loss should serve as lead plaintiff. That tends to put well-resourced, litigation-capable parties at the front of the case from day one. Korea, by contrast, requires a separate threshold fight just to determine whether a class action can proceed at all, with an immediate-appeal-plus-automatic-stay mechanism layered on top. Add to that the US system's broad pretrial **discovery**, which lets plaintiffs compel access to a defendant's internal records, versus Korea's comparatively narrow civil evidence-gathering rules — a meaningful disadvantage in cases where the core evidence of fraud or false disclosure sits inside the company being sued.

## A Case Where It Actually Worked: The Deutsche Bank ELS Lawsuit

The clearest example of the mechanism functioning as intended involves a group of [ELS (equity-linked securities)](/en/basics/els-structured-products-explained/) investors who sued Deutsche Bank over how a knock-in barrier was handled. The 2017 first-instance ruling in that case marked the law's first merits verdict, twelve years after it took effect. Deutsche Bank later withdrew its appeal, locking in that outcome — and 464 investors who had never personally argued the case in court ended up sharing roughly ₩12 billion in damages because they hadn't opted out. Separately, in 2015, Korea's Supreme Court reversed a lower court's denial of class-action permission for ELS investors suing over a different product, sending the case back with instructions to grant it — a reminder that even the threshold fight over permission can escalate all the way to the Supreme Court.

## What This Means for an Individual Investor

Twenty years and 12 cases might read as "Korean investors have no real path to collective redress," but that overstates the picture. As covered in our lessons on [unfaithful disclosure designation](/en/basics/unfaithful-disclosure-designation-korea/) and the [Beneish M-Score for detecting earnings manipulation](/en/basics/beneish-m-score-earnings-manipulation/), false disclosure and accounting fraud carry consequences separate from class action litigation — individual investors retain ordinary civil claims for damages, and Korea's Financial Supervisory Service runs a dispute mediation process that sometimes reaches settlement faster than any lawsuit could. The class action law's low usage doesn't make it meaningless: its mere existence sends a deterrence signal to management — commit fraud or manipulate disclosures, and a large group of investors could theoretically come after you at once. But for an investor who's actually been harmed, it's more realistic to look at individual damages claims and regulatory dispute mediation alongside the class action route rather than treating it as the only option.

## Takeaway

- Korea's Securities-Related Class Action Act, effective since January 1, 2005, lets a single representative plaintiff recover damages on behalf of all investors harmed by false disclosure, fraudulent trading/price manipulation, or accounting fraud.
- Before any trial on the merits, a court must grant permission under Article 12 (at least 50 members, combined holdings of at least 1/10,000 of the defendant's outstanding securities, and common issues) — and the defendant's immediate appeal of that decision automatically stays the case.
- Once permission is granted and published, an opt-out window opens; anyone who doesn't file an exclusion notice is automatically bound by the eventual outcome.
- The US runs over 200 securities class actions a year under the PSLRA's lead-plaintiff system with no pre-screening gate, while Korea has accumulated just 12 cases in twenty years as of 2024 reporting.
- The Deutsche Bank ELS case (first merits ruling in 2017, 464 investors, roughly ₩12 billion in damages) is the most cited example of the mechanism actually working.

## FAQ

### Can I use a securities class action for any stock loss?
No. It's limited to the categories the law names — false disclosure, fraudulent trading/price manipulation, and accounting fraud. A stock simply dropping in price doesn't qualify on its own.

### Do I need to personally join the lawsuit to get compensated?
No. Once a court grants permission, it publishes notice, and any affected investor who doesn't file an opt-out notice within the set window is automatically included and shares in whatever the representative plaintiff wins.

### Why is this mechanism used so rarely in Korea?
A combination of factors raises the barrier significantly: the separate permission hearing itself, the automatic stay triggered by an immediate appeal of that permission decision, a cost structure where attorneys typically front litigation expenses, and comparatively narrow evidence-gathering rules. That contrasts with the US, where a filed case proceeds directly to the merits without a pre-screening gate.
