## What Happened

After the close on July 23, Intel (Nasdaq: INTC) reported second-quarter 2026 results. Revenue came in at $16.1 billion, up 25% year over year - the company's fastest quarterly growth rate in roughly 15 years - and about $1.7 billion above the Wall Street consensus of $14.42 billion. Adjusted earnings per share hit $0.42, exactly double the $0.21 analysts had penciled in. The Data Center and AI Group (DCAI) led the way, with revenue jumping 59% year over year and 24% sequentially to $6.3 billion, while Intel Foundry revenue rose 31% year over year (up 6% sequentially) to $5.8 billion. One caveat stood out inside that foundry number: external customers still accounted for only about 5% of foundry revenue, meaning the company's central turnaround bet - convincing outside chipmakers to manufacture on Intel's 18A process - has yet to show up meaningfully in the numbers. Gross margin landed at 41.8%, roughly 280 basis points above guidance, and management raised its 2026 capital expenditure outlook from $18 billion to more than $20 billion, with 2027 spending set to climb further still.

The initial market reaction was mixed. Revenue and profit both blew past estimates, but lingering concerns about foundry's thin external customer base and the scale of the new capex commitment kept the stock volatile in the days right after the report. The real catalyst arrived about ten days later, on Tuesday, August 4, and it had almost nothing to do with Intel directly. That day, data-analytics company Palantir reported what several analysts called an "otherworldly" quarter - revenue up 93% year over year to $1.94 billion versus a $1.8 billion estimate, with commercial revenue up 149% and government revenue up 90% - and its stock rocketed 29.5% in a single session. The shockwave of AI-spending optimism lifted semiconductor stocks broadly, and Intel rode that wave to a 10.84% gain, closing at $100.86 versus Monday's $91.00 close - outperforming the broader chip sector by more than four percentage points and adding roughly $50 billion to Intel's market capitalization in a single day. The S&P 500 itself climbed 1.79% that day to a fresh record of 7,736.52, its first new high since June, as falling oil prices tied to hopes for a US-Iran deal over the Strait of Hormuz added to the risk-on mood across markets.

The strength carried through the rest of the week. Intel closed Friday, August 7 at $101.65, up another 1.84% on the day, bringing its weekly gain to 12.7% - with Tuesday's single-session pop accounting for roughly 86% of that entire weekly move. Zoom out further and the numbers get even more striking: Intel stock is up roughly 190% so far in 2026, on top of an 84% gain last year - a two-year, back-to-back triple-digit run for a company that, as recently as 2024, faced open speculation about its long-term viability.

## Why This Rally Happened - Three Forces Stacking at Once

Understanding this move requires separating out at least three distinct forces that landed on the stock in quick succession.

The first is genuine fundamental improvement. A 59% jump in DCAI revenue signals that the AI infrastructure buildout is not confined to GPU makers like Nvidia and AMD - it is translating into real server-CPU order volume for Intel as well. The gross-margin beat is arguably just as important: it suggests the cost discipline and operational restructuring CEO Lip-Bu Tan has pushed since taking over is starting to show up concretely in the income statement, not just in management commentary.

The second is a "read-through" effect - the phenomenon where one company's earnings surprise moves a completely different company's stock because the market treats the first result as evidence about a shared underlying trend. Palantir is not Intel's customer or supplier. But its blowout quarter was read as fresh proof that corporate AI spending is accelerating faster and more broadly than expected, and traders extended that inference to server and data-center hardware demand across the board - lifting semiconductor names, Intel included, even though Intel itself had released no new information that day. It's a clean illustration of how a stock can move sharply purely on another company's results, if the two sit in the same value chain or theme.

The third is a striking mismatch between analyst price targets and the stock's actual trajectory. The day after earnings, July 24, JPMorgan raised its Intel target from $45 to $85, Wedbush raised its target from $60 to $98, and Rosenblatt raised its target from $65 to $80 - all substantial hikes. What's notable is that all three kept cautious or negative ratings even as they raised targets: JPMorgan stayed at Underweight, Rosenblatt at Sell, and Wedbush at Neutral. In other words, each firm's message was something closer to "the results were impressive, but valuation and foundry execution risk still argue for caution" - not a full-throated buy call. The problem is that the subsequent rally made even those "cautious" targets obsolete within two weeks: Wedbush's $98 target, the highest of the three, was already below Intel's $101.65 close on August 7. Price targets are, by construction, a lagging exercise built on recent results and near-term forecasts. When a third-party catalyst like Palantir's earnings ignites a burst of pure momentum, even freshly revised, deliberately conservative targets can be overtaken by the market before the ink is dry.

There's a policy dimension worth noting too. The US government invested $8.9 billion in Intel in August 2025, acquiring roughly a 9.9% equity stake at $20.47 per share. Thanks to the subsequent rally, that stake is now estimated to be worth well over $30 billion, and President Trump has publicly pointed to the position as a profitable one for the government. Semiconductor manufacturing capacity has become a matter of declared national-security policy in the US, and Intel's ownership structure now reflects that directly - a factor that makes the stock harder to value on earnings fundamentals alone.

## What to Take Away From This

- **A stock can move sharply on another company's earnings.** Intel had no news of its own on August 4, yet it gained nearly 11% because Palantir's results reset expectations for the entire AI-spending theme. When tracking a stock, it pays to also watch the earnings calendar of other companies in the same value chain or narrative.
- **Analyst price targets are a lagging signal, not a leading one.** A raised target doesn't guarantee more upside remains, and a stock trading above its targets doesn't automatically mean it's overvalued. Read the accompanying rating (Underweight, Neutral, Buy) alongside the number - that combination tells you what the analyst actually believes.
- **An earnings beat is not the same thing as a structural problem being solved.** Intel Foundry's external-customer share of revenue is still stuck around 5%. The stock's rally doesn't mean the 18A external-customer challenge has been resolved - that remains a separate metric worth tracking on its own.
- **Non-fundamental variables like government ownership can shape a stock's valuation too.** Because domestic chip manufacturing is treated as a national-security priority, Intel carries an added layer of policy backing that a purely earnings-based model won't capture.
