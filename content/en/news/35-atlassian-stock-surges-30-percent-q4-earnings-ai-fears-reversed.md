## What Happened

After the market closed on August 6, **Atlassian** (NASDAQ: TEAM), the maker of workplace software Jira and Confluence, reported fiscal fourth-quarter 2026 results that beat expectations by a wide margin. Revenue came in at $1.766 billion, up 28% year-over-year and comfortably ahead of the $1.66 billion analysts had modeled. Non-GAAP earnings per share of $1.87 topped the roughly $1.49-1.50 consensus estimate by about 25%.

The stock's reaction was immediate and dramatic. Shares began surging in after-hours and pre-market trading, with the exact size of the move varying by outlet and timestamp: Benzinga reported shares up 30.7% to $143.99 in pre-market trading, while Bloomberg and Capital Brief put the peak gain closer to 33-36%. Whatever the precise figure, a single earnings report re-rated roughly 30% of the company's market value in hours. It's worth noting the rally didn't put the stock anywhere near its 52-week high of $206, and shares had been down about 28.8% year-to-date heading into the print. This wasn't a new-high moment - it was a heavily discounted stock snapping back after results came in far better than the market had priced in.

The details behind the headline numbers point to where the growth is coming from. Cloud revenue reached $1.213 billion, up 31% year-over-year and outpacing the 28% total revenue growth rate, while remaining performance obligations (RPO) - contracted revenue not yet recognized - jumped 44% to $4.8 billion. Profitability improved just as sharply: GAAP diluted EPS came in at $0.55, a swing from a $0.09 per-share loss a year earlier, with GAAP operating margin reaching 12%. Growth and profitability improving together is exactly what markets pricing in a worst-case scenario needed to see reversed.

## Why the 'AI Will Replace Collaboration Software' Fear Broke

To understand the size of this reaction, it helps to understand what had been weighing on Atlassian's stock for the better part of a year. Software investors had grown increasingly worried that AI coding agents and AI assistants would eventually make traditional collaboration tools like Jira and Confluence redundant - the logic being that once AI agents can handle development workflows directly, the need for humans to manually create tickets and organize documentation shrinks. That fear was fully priced in: the stock was down 28.8% year-to-date going into this report.

This quarter's results directly contradicted that narrative, and the evidence was specific. Atlassian's own AI feature, Rovo, is now used by more than 80% of the Fortune 500, and the number of Rovo-assisted actions grew 50% quarter-over-quarter. The more telling data point: customers who have adopted Rovo are growing annual recurring revenue (ARR) at more than double the rate of customers who haven't. That's concrete evidence AI isn't cannibalizing Atlassian's revenue from outside its products - it's driving upsell from inside them, pushing existing customers to spend more rather than replacing what they already pay for.

The quarter wasn't uniformly rosy, though. Atlassian has been migrating customers off its legacy on-premises "Data Center" product line and onto the cloud. This quarter's Data Center revenue of $461.9 million actually beat the $414.6 million analysts expected, but both the company and analysts flagged that this segment's revenue is expected to decline structurally over coming quarters as the migration to cloud continues. That's a deliberate trade-off rather than a warning sign - a lower-margin legacy revenue line shrinking while a faster-growing, higher-margin cloud business more than replaces it. The fact that cloud revenue is already growing faster than total revenue is itself evidence the transition is working as intended.

Alongside the results, Atlassian issued fiscal 2027 guidance targeting 13% revenue growth, 25.5% cloud revenue growth, and a 25% non-GAAP operating margin - signaling confidence that this quarter wasn't a one-off but a trajectory the company expects to sustain. Several brokerages responded with sizable price target increases: Raymond James lifted its target to $330 from $250, and Cantor Fitzgerald raised its own target citing growing confidence in Atlassian's cloud platform and AI monetization. A roughly 32% single-step increase in a price target is unusually large, and it signals that this report reset how analysts were modeling the company's growth, not just adjusted it at the margins.

## How This Compares to Other 'AI Fear Reversal' Stories

Atlassian's rebound fits a pattern that has shown up repeatedly among software companies covered on this site recently. Shopify faced fears that AI would disrupt its core e-commerce SaaS business, then answered them directly with a beat that sent shares up more than 20% ([Shopify Surges on AI Disruption Fears](/en/news/shopify-q2-earnings-ai-disruption-fear-stock-surge/)). Cloudflare turned surging AI-driven automated internet traffic from a perceived threat into a growth driver, sending shares up as much as 18% after hours ([Cloudflare Stock Jumps on Q2 Beat](/en/news/cloudflare-stock-jumps-q2-earnings-ai-demand-guidance-raise/)). All three cases share a structure: the market had priced in a story where AI would erode the company's core business, and a specific, numeric rebuttal in the earnings report - revenue growth, customer adoption metrics, AI feature usage data - unwound that pessimism in a single session.

Atlassian's case stands out mainly in magnitude. Because its stock had fallen further than Shopify's or Cloudflare's heading into earnings, the rebound was correspondingly larger. That relationship - how much pessimism was priced in beforehand shaping how violent the snap-back can be - is worth keeping in mind whenever a heavily discounted stock is approaching a widely-watched earnings date.

## What to Take Away From This

- **How much pessimism is already priced in shapes how big the post-earnings reaction can be.** Atlassian was down roughly 29% year-to-date before this report, so results that were merely "not as bad as feared" were enough to send the stock up more than 30%. When tracking a stock heading into earnings, it's worth asking not just what the numbers might show, but what scenario the current price already assumes.
- **Segment mix tells a fuller story than the headline growth rate.** Atlassian's Data Center revenue is shrinking by design while cloud revenue grows faster than the total. Breaking down which segments are driving growth and which are being deliberately wound down gives a much clearer read on a company's trajectory than the top-line number alone.
- **Verifying an "AI threat" narrative requires looking for concrete adoption and revenue data.** A statistic like "Rovo adopters grow ARR twice as fast as non-adopters" is far more useful for judging whether AI helps or hurts a company than a general narrative in either direction. Look for the specific metric before accepting the story.
- **Large, sudden price target increases are a lagging signal, not a leading one.** Multiple brokerages raised targets sharply after this report, but those moves reflect results that had already been confirmed. It's worth examining the underlying earnings and guidance yourself rather than chasing a stock purely because analysts raised their targets after the fact.
