## What Happened

US stocks opened lower again on Tuesday, November 17, just hours before Nvidia's quarterly earnings release. Monday's session had already been rough: the S&P 500 fell 1.92% to close at 7,416.59, its third straight losing session, while the Dow dropped 1.41% to 51,608.17 and the Nasdaq Composite slid 2.49% to 24,509.76 - its steepest one-day decline since November 5. Tuesday brought no relief. By roughly 11am ET, the Dow was down about 1.3%, the S&P 500 about 1.1%, and the Nasdaq Composite around 1.8%, extending the losing streak to a fourth session. A separate mid-morning tally showed somewhat smaller declines (S&P 500 down 0.67%, Nasdaq down 0.79%), but every account agreed on the broader picture: traders were de-risking ahead of Nvidia's report.

Zoom out past the day-to-day noise, though, and the picture gets more interesting. The Nasdaq 100 closed at a record 31,224.47 on October 6, then fell to 27,989.82 by November 2 - a drop of 10.4% that officially pushed the index into correction territory (commonly defined as a 10%-plus decline from a recent peak). That marked the third time in 2026 the Nasdaq 100 crossed that threshold, following similar episodes on March 27 and July 28-29, both of which were followed by fresh record highs within a matter of weeks. In other words, the index - heavily weighted toward a handful of mega-cap AI and semiconductor names - has now run through the same cycle of sharp rally, double-digit pullback, and recovery to new highs three separate times this year. The broader Nasdaq Composite told a similar story, tumbling more than 5% on Thursday, November 5 alone and landing roughly 11% below the record it had set the previous month.

Nvidia sits at the center of this latest pullback. The stock closed at a record $238.90 on October 5 (intraday trading briefly touched the $240 level), then closed its most recent session, Friday, November 13, at $162.85 - a decline of roughly 32% from that October peak. One market analysis notes that this level is actually below Nvidia's December 31, 2025 closing price of $186.50, meaning the stock has effectively given back its entire 2026 gain. Worth noting, though: AMD has held onto a substantial gain for the year over the same stretch, which suggests this weakness is concentrated in Nvidia specifically rather than spread evenly across the whole chip sector (see our related coverage: [AMD Stock Reverses, Giving Back Most of Last Week's 30% Rally](/en/news/amd-stock-reversal-nvidia-rival-trade-unwind-earnings-eve/)).

All of this converges on a single date for a clear reason. Nvidia reports fiscal Q3 2026 earnings after Tuesday's close - its first full investor conference call since founder and CEO Jensen Huang resigned on November 12 amid a board investigation, handing the interim CEO role to CFO Colette Kress (see: [Jensen Huang Resigns as Nvidia CEO, Colette Kress Named Interim CEO](/en/news/jensen-huang-resigns-colette-kress-interim-ceo-stock-drops-bernstein-cuts-target/)). Options markets had already priced in roughly a 9.6% implied move around the release (see: [Nvidia Reports Earnings November 17: Options Market Prices a 9.6% Swing, Colette Kress's First Call](/en/news/nvidia-earnings-november-17-options-market-9-point-6-percent-move-colette-kress-first-call/)), and Monday's sharp selloff in Taiwanese and South Korean semiconductor stocks was itself a reflection of that same anticipatory anxiety (see: [Asia Chip Stocks Sink, Nasdaq Futures Fall the Day Before Nvidia Earnings](/en/news/asia-chip-selloff-nasdaq-futures-fall-nvidia-earnings-eve/)).

## Why Three Corrections in One Year, and Why This Earnings Call Is a Hinge Point

That the Nasdaq 100 has already logged three separate 10% corrections in 2026 says something about how this market is built right now. Gains - and losses - have become unusually concentrated in a small cluster of mega-cap technology names, Nvidia chief among them, that together account for an outsized share of the index's total weight. When an index is built this way, even a modest wobble in sentiment toward its dominant constituent is enough to push the whole benchmark across the 10% correction line. The fact that the March and July episodes both resolved into fresh record highs within weeks suggests these pullbacks have functioned less like trend reversals and more like periodic releases of overcrowded positioning. What's different about the November episode is the extra variable layered on top: Nvidia's leadership vacuum.

Jensen Huang spent 33 years building Nvidia into a company whose every public statement carried outsized weight with investors. The first earnings call since his abrupt departure isn't just about whether the quarterly numbers hit consensus - it's a live test of whether the company can hold its revenue guidance and customer commitments together without the person markets had come to treat as inseparable from the business itself. The 9.6% implied move options traders had priced in ahead of the print is unusually large by Nvidia's own recent earnings-day standards, which suggests the market isn't just pricing uncertainty about the numbers - it's pricing uncertainty about who delivers them and how. Layer onto that the fact that this report lands with the stock already down 32% from its October high and essentially flat-to-negative for the year, and the stakes multiply: whether tonight's call reads as a buying opportunity at a discounted price or as confirmation that something more structural has shifted depends heavily on tone, not just on the headline revenue and EPS figures.

Mechanically, this is a textbook case of concentration risk working in both directions. The rally that lifted US equities for most of 2026 was driven disproportionately by a single theme - AI data-center buildout - and its most visible proxy. When that proxy wobbles, the broader index wobbles with it in near lockstep, because so much of the index's return over the past several years has come from that same narrow slice of names. The more an index leans on a handful of stocks for its gains, the more a single earnings call from one of those stocks becomes the deciding variable for whether a correction holds or reverses. That's precisely the position the market finds itself in on November 17.

## What to Take Away From This

- **Don't overreact to the word "correction" itself - look at how often it's recurred and in what context.** The Nasdaq 100 has already logged two prior 10% corrections this year, both of which were followed by new record highs. Whether a pullback marks a genuine trend change or a brief unwind of crowded positioning is often only clear in hindsight, based on how quickly and how far the index recovers afterward.
- **When an index leans heavily on a small number of stocks, a single company's event can set the direction for the whole benchmark.** The fact that one earnings call from Nvidia can determine whether the Nasdaq 100's current correction holds or reverses is a reminder that even index investors should track the earnings calendar of the handful of names that actually move their benchmark.
- **Non-financial uncertainty, like a leadership vacuum, shows up directly in options-implied volatility.** Markets can react very differently to similar headline numbers depending on who delivers them and how credible the explanation sounds. When a stock you hold is showing an unusually large implied move ahead of a known catalyst, that's a reasonable moment to double-check position sizing before the event, not after.
- **A stock trading back at - or below - where it started the year isn't automatically "cheap."** Whether a drawdown reflects a temporary unwind of crowded positioning or a genuine re-rating of the underlying business is exactly the question an earnings call like this one is meant to answer. Drawing that conclusion before the numbers are out puts the analysis in the wrong order.

## FAQ

### What exactly does a "technical correction" mean?
It's standard market shorthand for a decline of 10% or more in a stock or index from its most recent peak. A drop of 20% or more is typically labeled a "bear market," a stronger term; a 10%-range decline, by contrast, is common enough during an otherwise upward trend that many market participants treat it as a normal, if uncomfortable, part of the cycle rather than a signal that the trend itself has ended.

### Is it unusual for the Nasdaq 100 to enter correction territory three times in one year?
It's not unprecedented historically, but three separate 10% corrections within a single calendar year does suggest that valuations and investor positioning in the handful of mega-cap technology stocks that drive the index have been running more stretched than usual. The fact that the two earlier 2026 episodes both resolved into new record highs within weeks is a useful data point, but it's no guarantee the same pattern repeats a third time.

### Why does one company's earnings report move the entire Nasdaq 100 this much?
Nvidia ranks among the largest constituents of both the Nasdaq 100 and the S&P 500 by market capitalization. Because most major indices are weighted by market cap, a sharp move in a heavily-weighted stock flows through to the index's overall return in a direct and outsized way - far more than an equally-sized move in a smaller constituent would.
