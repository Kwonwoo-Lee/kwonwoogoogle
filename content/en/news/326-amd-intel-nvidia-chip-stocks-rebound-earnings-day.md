## What Happened

Intel's October 29 after-hours guidance miss sent the chip sector into a tailspin that didn't stop after one day. The stock fell roughly 20-25% intraday on October 30, then kept sliding, dropping another roughly 8-9% on November 2. By the time Tuesday, November 3 rolled around, Intel had shed close to 37% of its value over three trading sessions, according to Yahoo Finance - a staggering decline for a mega-cap chipmaker in under a week.

Then the script flipped. In Tuesday's premarket and regular session, AMD (Nasdaq: AMD) rallied somewhere between 3% and 5% depending on which outlet's snapshot you check, Intel (Nasdaq: INTC) bounced about 4%, and Nvidia (Nasdaq: NVDA) snapped a two-day losing streak to trade higher. 24/7 Wall St. summarized the session as "AMD advances 5%, Intel rises 4%, Marvell gains 2%," while smaller chip-adjacent names like Navitas Semiconductor, Astera Labs, and Credo Technology also moved up in sympathy. Invezz's own tally - Nvidia +1.51%, AMD +2.73%, Intel +2.48% - landed on slightly different numbers, but every outlet agreed on the direction: a battered sector was bouncing hard.

The catalyst behind the bounce was AMD's own third-quarter earnings report, due after Tuesday's close with a 5 p.m. ET call. Wall Street consensus called for revenue near $13.1 billion, up roughly 42% year-over-year, and adjusted EPS around $1.95. AMD's own guidance had pointed to about $13 billion, plus or minus $300 million, with adjusted gross margin near 56%. The number everyone was really watching, though, was data-center revenue - Yahoo Finance's consensus put it at $8.19 billion, up 88% year-over-year, while one analyst model went as high as $8.27 billion, up 90.6%. Prediction markets were pricing roughly 79% odds that AMD's data-center segment would clear the $8 billion mark.

## Why Chip Stocks Rallied Before the Numbers Even Came In

On its face, it seems odd for a stock to rally ahead of the one event that will actually determine whether the rally is justified. But a few concrete mechanics were at work here.

First, the entire three-day, 37% slide had started as an Intel-specific problem - a PC-demand warning that had nothing directly to do with AMD's or Nvidia's core businesses - and a growing number of investors concluded the selloff had over-extended into names that didn't deserve the same punishment. TipRanks noted that both AMD and Nvidia still carried Strong Buy consensus ratings from Wall Street throughout the decline, while only Intel sat at a Hold. Analyst price targets hadn't moved even as the stocks cratered, which is exactly the kind of gap bargain-hunters look for: a sharply lower price next to an unchanged rating is the textbook setup for a dip-buying bounce.

Second, bullish pre-earnings commentary added fuel. According to Tikr's reporting, at least one analyst note framed AMD favorably heading into the print, essentially betting that Intel's PC-specific warning wouldn't bleed into AMD's server-side AI chip business, the Instinct line, which has been running on an entirely different growth trajectory than Intel's PC-heavy client segment. The options market, for its part, was pricing in roughly a 9.11% implied move for AMD in either direction post-earnings - a signal that traders expected a big reaction, just not which way it would break. Betting on volatility and betting on direction are two completely different trades, and Tuesday's price action looked far more like the former.

Third, Marvell's roughly 2% gain had a different root cause entirely. Marvell had been sliding after announcing its $115 billion, all-stock acquisition of Lumentum amid investor concerns about dilution and deal financing, so Tuesday's bounce in Marvell looks more like a partial unwind of that specific, deal-driven selloff than a read-through from the broader Intel story. Not every green ticker in the sector that day was rallying for the same reason, even though the headlines lumped them together under one "chip stocks rebound" banner.

Put together, Tuesday's rebound wasn't evidence that "the problem is solved." It was the market making a pre-emptive bet that the prior three days had been overdone, ahead of the one data point - AMD's actual data-center revenue and fourth-quarter guidance - that would settle the question for real. If data-center revenue came in below the roughly $8.2 billion consensus, or if fourth-quarter guidance turned cautious, Tuesday's gains were fully capable of being handed right back. If the numbers beat expectations instead, the Intel-driven selloff would get re-read as fear that was disconnected from AMD's own fundamentals, and the bounce would have room to extend.

## What to Take Away From This

- **A sharp selloff doesn't automatically mean the new price is "correct."** A single company's guidance shock dragged an entire sector down by double digits over three days, and some of that move reversed simply because analyst ratings never actually changed. The more panic-driven a decline looks, the more worth it is to re-check the underlying fundamentals before assuming the market has it right.
- **A pre-earnings rally is a bet, not a confirmation.** Tuesday's bounce happened before AMD's actual data-center numbers were known. If those numbers disappoint, the rally can get handed right back just as fast as it arrived.
- **Options-implied volatility tells you the size of the uncertainty, not its direction.** AMD's roughly 9% implied move meant the market expected a big swing either way, not that it expected a rally specifically. Sizing your position for volatility, rather than guessing the direction, is the more durable habit heading into any earnings print.
- **Stocks that rise together on the same day don't always rise for the same reason.** Marvell's bounce traced back to its own M&A-related selloff easing, a completely separate story from the Intel-driven AMD and Nvidia rebound. Lumping every green name in a sector into one headline narrative can cause you to miss the specific risk sitting underneath each individual stock.

## FAQ

### Why did chip stocks rally before AMD even reported earnings?
Investors concluded the prior three-day, 37% Intel-led selloff had spread too far into AMD and Nvidia, whose Strong Buy analyst ratings never changed during the decline. That gap between unchanged ratings and sharply lower prices drew bargain-hunters in ahead of AMD's report.

### Does this rally prove the October 30 selloff was an overreaction?
Not yet. The rally happened before AMD's actual data-center revenue and fourth-quarter guidance were known. If those numbers come in below the roughly $13.1 billion revenue and $8.2 billion data-center consensus, the bounce could reverse just as quickly.

### What should I watch for in AMD's earnings report?
The two numbers that matter most are data-center revenue growth, where consensus sits at 88-90% year-over-year, and fourth-quarter guidance. If both come in solid despite Intel's PC-demand warning, it would support the case that Tuesday's rebound was justified.
