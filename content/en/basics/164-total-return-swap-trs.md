## You Can Have the Economics of Owning Stock Without Owning It

You may have run across a headline like "a conglomerate secured a de facto stake in its affiliate through a TRS contract" or "a hedge fund quietly built a position using total return swaps." At first that sounds contradictory — how can you "hold a stake" in something you never bought? Answering that requires understanding exactly what a **total return swap (TRS)** actually trades. A TRS is an over-the-counter derivative that lets one party collect every dollar of economic gain or loss on an asset without ever legally owning it. This lesson covers how a TRS works, why it gets used, and why it has repeatedly resurfaced as a loophole in ownership-disclosure rules — including a landmark Korean case and a 2021 blowup that cost global banks billions.

## What a TRS Is: Splitting Ownership from Economics

A TRS is built around a reference asset — a stock, bond, or index. One side, the **total return payer**, actually holds the asset and passes along everything it generates — price gains or losses, dividends, interest — to the other side. The **total return receiver** pays the payer a periodic fee in return (typically a reference rate plus a spread). The key feature: the receiver never buys a share of the underlying asset, yet gains when its price rises and loses when it falls, exactly as if they owned it. The payer keeps legal title and voting rights but is economically indifferent to the price — those gains and losses have already passed through to the receiver. A TRS cleanly **separates legal ownership from economic exposure**: one party becomes an owner in name only, while the other captures the economics without any formal ownership at all. The payer role is almost always a bank or broker-dealer, which buys and holds the asset and passes its performance to the client for a fee.

## Why Use One: Leverage, Regulatory Workarounds, and Speed

Three motives drive most TRS use. First, **leverage without full funding** — a receiver gains full price exposure while posting only a fraction of the notional as collateral, so the same capital controls a much larger position. Second, **regulatory or disclosure advantage** — because legal title stays with the bank, a TRS can sidestep restrictions tied to direct ownership, as the Korean case below shows. Third, **speed and discretion** — buying a large stake directly pushes up the price and tips off the market, while a bank can quietly build the position behind a TRS contract.

## A Worked Example

Hedge fund B enters a one-year TRS with broker A on $10 million of a stock. A is the payer, actually holding the position; B is the receiver, paying A an annual fee of 4% of the notional. If the stock rises 20% and pays $200,000 in dividends, A owes B $2.2 million; netting against B's $400,000 fee leaves B a net gain of $1.8 million — despite never buying a share. If the stock instead fell 20%, B would owe A $2.4 million. B typically only posts 10–20% of the notional as collateral, which is where the leverage comes from.

## The Korean Case: A Loophole Around the 5% Rule

As covered in [Beneficial Ownership Reporting (the 5% Rule)](/en/basics/beneficial-ownership-report-5-rule/), anyone building a 5%-or-greater stake in a Korean listed company must disclose it. That rule was built around directly holding shares, and cash-settled derivatives like a TRS — where no shares technically change hands — long sat outside its scope. This gap surfaced during the 2015 Samsung C&T–Cheil Industries merger, when Elliott Management combined direct purchases with TRS contracts to build added exposure; regulators considered this a possible disclosure violation, though prosecutors ultimately filed no charges on that point. A more persistent concern is Korean conglomerates spreading TRS contracts across several banks to build an effective stake in an affiliate while keeping each individual contract below any reporting threshold. A October 2026 report from the Federation of Korean Industries flagged this structure and called for extending disclosure rules to cash-settled derivatives. US regulators have faced a similar question: SEC staff have indicated that a TRS receiver who effectively directs how the underlying shares are voted can be treated as the true "beneficial owner" regardless of the contract's legal form — disclosure should follow substance, not label. TRS contracts have also been used to "park" unlisted shares, letting an investor hold the economic value of a stake without it being registered in their own name, which is part of why regulators keep a close eye on the structure.

## The 2021 Archegos Collapse

The clearest illustration of how much risk a TRS book can quietly hide is the March 2021 collapse of Archegos Capital Management. Rather than buying stock directly, the US family office entered TRS contracts with multiple banks — including Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, Credit Suisse, and Nomura — building concentrated, overlapping exposure to a handful of stocks. Because TRS contracts are private and bilateral, no single bank could see that Archegos was running nearly identical exposure with its other counterparties at the same time, while quietly running leverage several multiples of its own capital. When ViacomCBS, one of its concentrated holdings, announced a large share offering in March 2021 and its stock fell sharply, every bank issued margin calls at once; Archegos defaulted, and the banks began dumping the same underlying shares to recover their money, driving prices down further in a self-reinforcing spiral. Banks that sold fastest minimized losses; Credit Suisse and Nomura, slower to react, absorbed losses in the billions (Credit Suisse alone booked roughly $5.5 billion). The lesson wasn't that TRS contracts are inherently dangerous — it was that because they're negotiated privately, no counterparty or regulator could see how much leverage one client had stacked across the whole system, which pushed global regulators toward tighter disclosure and counterparty-concentration rules.

## How a TRS Differs from a CDS or a Stock-Pledge Loan

A [credit default swap (CDS)](/en/basics/credit-default-swap-cds/) only trades a bond issuer's **default risk** — a premium changes hands routinely, with a payout only if a credit event occurs. A TRS instead exchanges the **entire price performance** of an asset continuously, regardless of default. It also differs from a [stock-pledge loan](/en/basics/share-pledge-loan-explained/), where a shareholder borrows against stock they already own and keeps both title and all the price risk. A TRS does the opposite: it keeps title on one side while shifting all the price risk to the other.

## Key Takeaways

- A TRS separates legal ownership from economic performance: the receiver captures all price gains, losses, and income without ever owning the asset.
- The payer (usually a bank) holds the asset and keeps legal rights but passes through all price performance for a fee.
- TRS contracts enable capital-efficient leverage, can work around ownership restrictions or disclosure rules, and leave a lighter market footprint than a direct purchase.
- Cash-settled TRS contracts have historically sat outside Korea's 5% ownership-disclosure rule, letting conglomerates and investors build effective stakes without triggering disclosure.
- The 2021 Archegos collapse showed how dangerous undisclosed, overlapping TRS leverage across multiple banks becomes once a position unwinds.

## FAQ

### Can retail investors enter TRS contracts?
Practically speaking, no. TRS contracts are negotiated bilaterally between banks and institutional clients, require substantial notional amounts, and involve credit review that individuals can't access. Retail investors seeking similar exposure usually go through smaller-scale products like a [CFD](/en/basics/cfd-contract-for-difference-explained/) instead.

### Is a TRS the same as a CFD?
Both separate price exposure from ownership, but at different scale and for different purposes. A CFD is retail-sized and mostly a leveraged trading product; a TRS operates at institutional scale for purposes that often blend hedging, stake-building, and regulatory considerations.

### Is entering a TRS contract illegal?
No — TRS contracts are a widely used, legitimate derivative worldwide. The concern isn't the instrument itself but cases where it's used to substantively defeat investor-protection rules like ownership disclosure, which is why regulators keep revising those rules to close the gap.
