## "It's Only a Few Percent" Is the Most Expensive Mistake in Investing

A bank savings account paying 3% a year versus a stock market historically averaging 8-10% a year. On paper, that looks like a modest 5-7 percentage point gap. Run that gap through 30 years of compounding, though, and "modest" stops being the right word. This article shows exactly how large that gap becomes - and why.

## What Compound Interest Actually Is

Compound interest means interest earns interest. Put 10 million KRW into a 5% simple-interest account and after 10 years you'd have exactly 500,000 KRW added each year - 15 million total. With compound interest, the first year's gain becomes part of the principal the following year, and it starts generating its own returns. The formula:

**Future Value = Principal × (1 + annual return)^years**

The key detail: the return sits in the exponent. A small increase in the rate doesn't add up linearly over time - it compounds exponentially.

## The Real Numbers: 10 Million KRW Over 30 Years

As of 2026, South Korea's five major banks offer promotional 1-year time deposit rates of roughly 2.55-3.30%, while the average across savings banks (a riskier, second-tier category) runs closer to 3.90%. The S&P 500, by contrast, delivered a compound annual growth rate of about 10.2% from 1928 through 2024, dividends reinvested. Plugging these into the compounding formula for 10 million KRW:

| Annual Return | After 10 Years | After 20 Years | After 30 Years |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.5% (low end of major bank deposits) | ₩12.80M | ₩16.39M | ₩20.98M |
| 3.9% (savings bank average) | ₩14.66M | ₩21.49M | ₩31.51M |
| 8% (KOSPI long-run average since 1980) | ₩21.59M | ₩46.61M | ₩100.63M |
| 10.2% (S&P 500 compound return, 1928-2024) | ₩26.42M | ₩69.78M | ₩184.35M |

After 30 years, the bank deposit has roughly doubled your money (2.1x). At the S&P 500's historical rate, it's grown roughly 18.4x. The annual return gap was 7.7 percentage points - the final-result gap is nearly 9x. That's the entire point: in compounding, a "small" rate difference is never actually small once time is involved.

## But Not All "Stock Investing" Is the Same

Here's the crucial caveat. The "KOSPI long-run average of 8%" above covers roughly 40+ years since 1980. **Over just the last 10 years, KOSPI's annualized return has been closer to 2.5%** - essentially tied with, or even below, bank deposit rates over the same stretch. Meanwhile, the S&P 500 delivered over 10% annualized in that same window.

In other words, "invest in stocks and you'll automatically beat a savings account" isn't accurate. **Which market, over what period, and how diversified you are** all determine your actual realized return. The compounding math above shows what happens *if* a given return rate actually materializes - it doesn't guarantee that stocks, as an asset class, will deliver that rate.

## So What Should You Actually Do

- **Don't concentrate in one country or one stock.** The gap between KOSPI and the S&P 500 over the past decade shows that geographic and asset diversification isn't optional - it's close to a prerequisite for actually capturing compounding's benefits.
- **Extend your time horizon.** As the table shows, the difference between 10 and 30 years dwarfs the difference in return rates. Time is compounding's single biggest lever.
- **Only invest money you can afford to see fluctuate.** Unlike a savings account, stocks carry real risk of principal loss. Short-term needs and emergency funds should be fully excluded from this calculation. See [Risk Management Basics](/en/basics/risk-management-basics/) for more on this.
- **Tax treatment differs too.** In Korea, savings interest is taxed at a flat 15.4% withholding rate. Domestic stock gains generally aren't subject to capital gains tax (for non-major shareholders) but dividends are taxed at 15.4%; overseas stock gains above 2.5 million KRW annually face a separate 22% capital gains tax. Always compare returns on an after-tax basis.

## Summary

The math behind compounding is unambiguous: a higher rate held over a longer period produces an exponentially larger gap, not a linear one. But that rate isn't handed to you automatically - as KOSPI's mediocre last-decade performance shows, actually capturing that compounding advantage requires diversification, sufficient time, and risk you can genuinely tolerate.
