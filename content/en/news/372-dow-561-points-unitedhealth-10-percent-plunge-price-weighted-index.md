## What Happened

Wall Street's three major indexes moved in conflicting directions on Wednesday, November 18. The Dow Jones Industrial Average dropped 561.25 points (1.11%) to close at 50,198.17. The S&P 500 fell a far more modest 16.90 points (0.23%) to 7,323.63. The Nasdaq Composite actually rose 101.50 points (0.42%) to 24,429.88. A headline reading "Dow drops over 500 points" sounds like a broad market selloff, but the day was, in fact, unusually split - three indexes, three different directions.

The split traces back almost entirely to one Dow component: health insurer UnitedHealth Group (NYSE: UNH). Shares fell $39.77, or 10.21%, to close at $349.91, after touching an intraday low near $342. With the stock having opened around $387, that's nearly $40 per share erased in a single session. Rival Humana (NYSE: HUM) fell roughly 13% the same day - notable because Humana had already dropped 5.4% in the prior session, meaning the stock absorbed double-digit losses on back-to-back trading days. CVS Health and other Medicare-exposed insurers slid as well.

The catalyst was a new audit proposal from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS), a division of the Department of Health and Human Services, unveiled on Monday, November 16. Dubbed "CRUSH," the proposal would extend risk-adjustment audits of Medicare Advantage insurers - private carriers that administer Medicare plans - back to payment year 2018. Under the plan, if CMS auditors find an overpayment rate in a sample of a plan's medical charts, that same rate would be extrapolated across the insurer's entire contract rather than applied only to the audited sample. CMS estimates the approach could recover roughly $9 billion per payment year industrywide. Insurers with the heaviest Medicare Advantage exposure - UnitedHealth and Humana chief among them - face the largest potential clawback risk. Neither company has yet disclosed a specific dollar estimate of its exposure, and the rule remains a proposal: the public comment period runs through January 19, 2027, leaving room for the final language to change.

A second major event landed the same day. Minutes from the Federal Reserve's October 27-28 FOMC meeting were released, and they reinforced an already hawkish tone. At that meeting, the Fed had held its benchmark rate at 3.75%-4.00% in a 10-2 vote, with Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack both dissenting in favor of a 25-basis-point hike - notably, Cook's first dissent since joining the Board in 2022. The newly released minutes went further, noting that "almost all" participants judged further rate increases would likely be appropriate under their baseline outlooks. Futures markets had already priced roughly 78% odds of a December hike heading into the release; the minutes cemented that pricing rather than shifting it dramatically. Gold, more sensitive to real-rate expectations, fell 2.9% on the day.

## Why the Dow Fell While the Nasdaq Rose

Two distinct stories landed on the same trading day - UnitedHealth's collapse and the Fed's hawkish minutes - yet they showed up in wildly different magnitudes across the three indexes. The explanation lies in how each index is actually built.

The most direct cause is that the Dow Jones Industrial Average is a price-weighted index. It's calculated by summing the share prices of its 30 components and dividing by a fixed divisor - meaning a stock's influence on the index depends on its dollar price per share, not its market capitalization. UnitedHealth trades in the $350-390 range, among the highest-priced stocks in the entire Dow. A 10% drop in a stock at that price level erases nearly $40 per share, roughly eight times the dollar impact of the same 10% move in a $50 stock - regardless of which company is actually larger by market value. This isn't a one-off quirk, either: in April 2025, when UnitedHealth's stock collapsed more than 22% on an earnings shock, that single stock was estimated to have dragged the Dow down by more than 800 points on its own, even as most other Dow components were trading higher that day. The mechanism repeats because it's baked into how the index is calculated, not because of anything unusual about this particular week.

The S&P 500 and Nasdaq Composite, by contrast, are both market-capitalization-weighted. Under that method, a company's total market value - not its per-share price - determines how much it moves the index. UnitedHealth's market cap sits around $320 billion, a sizable company by any normal standard, but tiny next to the multi-trillion-dollar megacap technology names that dominate both the S&P 500 and the Nasdaq. The same 10% decline that devastated the Dow's point count shaved only about 0.2 percentage points off the S&P 500, and had no mechanical pathway into the Nasdaq Composite at all, since UnitedHealth isn't a Nasdaq-listed stock.

Layer on top of that the fact that this particular shock was sector-specific by design. CMS's audit proposal targets the economics of private Medicare Advantage insurers directly - it says nothing about the broader economy, consumer spending, or the fundamentals of any other industry. Shocks like this tend to hit the targeted sector hard without bleeding into the wider market. The Fed minutes, meanwhile, were the kind of news that would normally weigh on equities broadly - but because futures markets had already priced in roughly 78% odds of a December hike before the release, the minutes read more as confirmation than surprise. News that's already substantially priced in tends to produce a muted equity reaction even when the underlying tone is genuinely hawkish, which is part of why the clearest reaction showed up in gold and rate-sensitive assets rather than in the megacap-tech-heavy Nasdaq.

## What to Take Away From This

- **A big point move doesn't automatically mean a broad market move.** The Dow's 561-point drop looked dramatic, but checking the percentage change - and comparing it against the S&P 500 and Nasdaq on the same day - revealed a market that was actually split, not uniformly weak.
- **Price-weighted and cap-weighted indexes encode different information.** The Dow's moves are disproportionately driven by whichever component happens to have the highest per-share price; the S&P 500 and Nasdaq are driven by whichever components are actually the largest companies. The same news story can look completely different depending on which index you're reading it through.
- **Sector-specific regulatory shocks are not the same as market-wide risk.** A CMS rule aimed squarely at Medicare Advantage insurers can devastate that sector's stocks without reflecting anything about the health of other industries or the broader economy.
- **Macro news that's already priced in tends to land as confirmation, not surprise.** With December hike odds already near 78% before the minutes dropped, the hawkish language mostly reinforced existing expectations rather than forcing a fresh repricing - which is why the reaction showed up far more clearly in gold than in equities.
- **Smaller players in a sector can get hit even harder than the market leader.** Humana's roughly 13% decline outpaced UnitedHealth's 10%, a pattern that shows up often when investors judge that a smaller competitor has less financial cushion to absorb the same regulatory risk.

## FAQ

### Why can one stock move the Dow so much?
The Dow Jones Industrial Average is price-weighted, meaning it sums the share prices of its 30 components and divides by a fixed divisor. A stock's dollar price per share - not its total market value - determines its influence. UnitedHealth trades at one of the highest per-share prices in the index, so even a moderate percentage move translates into an outsized point swing for the Dow as a whole.

### What exactly does CMS's "CRUSH" audit proposal do?
It would let CMS apply risk-adjustment audit findings from a sample of a Medicare Advantage insurer's medical charts across that insurer's entire contract, retroactively back to payment year 2018, rather than limiting recoveries to the specific records audited. CMS estimates this extrapolation approach could recover about $9 billion per payment year across the industry. The proposal is still open for public comment through January 19, 2027.

### Does this mean health insurance stocks are now a bad investment?
Not necessarily. CMS's proposal hasn't been finalized - it's still in a public comment period, and the eventual scope and timeline could change materially. That said, insurers with heavy Medicare Advantage revenue exposure appear more sensitive to this kind of regulatory uncertainty, which is worth weighing in any investment decision involving the sector.

### Is the Fed actually going to raise rates in December?
It can't be stated with certainty, but futures markets were already pricing roughly 78% odds of a December hike, and the October minutes confirmed that most participants leaned toward further tightening. The final decision will still depend on employment and inflation data released between now and the December meeting, so the outcome isn't locked in.
