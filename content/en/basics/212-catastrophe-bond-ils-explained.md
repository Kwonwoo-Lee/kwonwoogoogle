## A Bond That Pays Interest If No Earthquake Happens — and Loses Principal If One Does

Industry trackers of the insurance-linked securities (ILS) market reported that outstanding catastrophe bonds worldwide passed $61.3 billion by the end of 2025, up roughly 24% from about $49.5 billion a year earlier and a new record. New issuance for the year also broke records, topping $25 billion. What makes this market unusual is that these bonds move almost independently of the issuer's own credit risk. Investors don't lose money because a company defaults — they lose money if a specific earthquake or hurricane actually happens in a defined region at a defined severity. If no such event occurs, investors earn a yield well above what a comparable corporate bond would pay. This lesson covers how a structure that converts insurance risk into a tradable capital-markets bond actually works, and why the world's largest pension funds and hedge funds have been pouring money into it.

## What a Catastrophe Bond Is: Reinsurance Risk Repackaged as a Bond

A **catastrophe bond (cat bond)** is a bond that insurers or reinsurers issue to transfer large-scale natural disaster risk — earthquakes, hurricanes, typhoons, floods — to capital markets investors. It is the flagship product within the broader category called **insurance-linked securities (ILS)**. To understand the structure, start with ordinary reinsurance: insurers owe policyholders claims payments, and when that exposure gets too large to carry alone, insurers pay a premium to reinsurers to take on part of the risk. The problem is that catastrophic events — a major earthquake or hurricane that can generate tens of billions of dollars in losses in a single event — periodically exceed the combined capital reinsurers worldwide are willing or able to deploy. A cat bond exists to plug that capacity gap by reaching into a far larger pool of capital: the broader capital markets. In effect, a reinsurer slices off a piece of the risk it's carrying, packages it as a bond, and sells it directly to pension funds, hedge funds and dedicated ILS funds. This is why the money cat bonds attract is commonly called "alternative capital" in the industry.

## The Mechanics: An SPV and a Collateral Trust Doing Double Duty

The structure hinges on a **special purpose vehicle (SPV)**, typically domiciled in Bermuda or the Cayman Islands. The insurer or reinsurer transferring the risk (the sponsor) enters into a reinsurance contract with the SPV and pays it a reinsurance premium. The SPV uses that premium to fund interest payments on bonds it issues to investors, while the principal investors contribute gets deposited into a collateral trust and invested in ultra-safe, short-term instruments — typically government bonds or money market funds. The interest investors receive is the return on that collateral (the risk-free rate) plus the reinsurance premium the sponsor pays (the risk premium). If no predefined disaster trigger fires during the bond's term (usually around three years), investors get their principal and interest back in full at maturity. If a trigger does fire, part or all of the collateral gets paid out to the sponsor to cover claims, and investors lose that portion of their principal. Credit ratings on cat bonds typically sit around BB, speculative grade — not because of any issuer default risk, but because that rating reflects the probability that a qualifying disaster actually occurs and wipes out principal. Sponsors range from giant reinsurers like Swiss Re and Munich Re to public risk pools dedicated to a single region's exposure, such as the California Earthquake Authority (CEA) or Florida's state-run insurer, Citizens Property Insurance. Most deals price in U.S. dollars and get arranged by specialist brokers in New York or London, and the buyers on the other side are almost always institutions equipped to underwrite this kind of risk directly — pension funds, insurers and hedge funds.

## Trigger Types: What Determines Whether a Loss Is "Real"

Every cat bond defines its own standard for what counts as a triggering event, and that standard is the single most important variable determining an investor's actual risk. There are four broad categories.

| Trigger type | What it measures | Advantage | Drawback |
|---|---|---|---|
| Indemnity | The sponsor's actual paid claims | Matches the sponsor's real loss exactly | Claims settlement can take months to years; investors depend on the sponsor's reporting |
| Parametric | Physical measurements — earthquake magnitude and epicenter, hurricane central pressure, etc. | Fast (weeks) and transparent | Can diverge from the sponsor's actual loss ("basis risk") |
| Industry loss index | Industry-wide losses tallied by a third party (e.g., PCS) | Avoids delays tied to any one sponsor's claims process | Can still diverge from the specific sponsor's own loss |
| Modeled loss | A loss estimate generated by feeding real weather data into a catastrophe model after the event | Faster than indemnity, more precise than parametric | Outcome depends on the modeling firm's assumptions |

The core tension sits between indemnity and parametric triggers. Indemnity triggers track the sponsor's actual paid claims almost exactly, so basis risk is minimal — but settling claims can take years, leaving investors' capital trapped in limbo. Parametric triggers only need a clear physical reading — an earthquake's epicenter coordinates, a hurricane's central pressure — so settlement is far faster, but that speed comes at the cost of **basis risk**: the gap between what the trigger measures and what the sponsor actually loses. In the most extreme case, a storm that happens to meet the trigger's physical criteria could pay out even if the sponsor's actual losses were modest, while a storm that causes enormous real losses could miss the trigger criteria entirely and pay nothing. Parametric triggers accounted for over 40% of issuance in the early 2000s, but the market has since shifted heavily toward indemnity triggers for most private-sector deals. Parametric structures remain popular, though, in one specific niche: bonds the World Bank issues on behalf of developing-country governments, where speed of payout after a disaster matters more than precision.

## The Trigger in Action: Mexico's 2017 Chiapas Earthquake

A real event illustrates how this plays out. In 2017, Mexico's government issued a $360 million multi-peril cat bond through the World Bank to fund its disaster relief program (FONDEN). The earthquake tranche used a classic parametric structure: if an earthquake's epicenter, depth and magnitude fell within a predefined "box," the payout would be 25%, 50%, 75% or 100% of principal depending on severity. On September 7, 2017, a magnitude-8.1 earthquake struck near Chiapas, and its epicenter landed squarely inside the trigger box. It took the U.S. Geological Survey more than a month to finalize the epicenter, depth and magnitude data, but once confirmed, the tranche met the criteria for a full payout and $150 million was paid to the Mexican government. A separate tranche of the same bond, covering Pacific tropical storms, came close to triggering when Hurricane Willa approached in 2018 — but the storm's central pressure rose above the trigger threshold before landfall, so it narrowly missed, and investors kept their principal intact. Both outcomes were decided purely by whether a predefined physical threshold was crossed, not by how much actual damage occurred — the defining feature of a parametric trigger.

## Why Investors Buy This Risk: Low Correlation

Pensions, specialist reinsurance funds and hedge funds keep allocating to cat bonds for a reason that has less to do with yield than with **correlation**. Stock and bond prices move together with broader economic variables — interest rates, growth, corporate earnings. Whether a major earthquake strikes a specific region in a given year has essentially nothing to do with any of that. During the 2008 financial crisis and the 2020 pandemic, when stocks and bonds sold off together, the cat bond market was comparatively insulated. That's the core reason institutional investors have steadily grown their allocations to the ILS asset class, and it's a big part of why the market set a fresh record in 2025. The appeal comes with two caveats, though. First, climate change is pushing up the frequency and intensity of hurricanes, floods and heat-driven disasters, which can structurally raise the trigger probabilities that historical data was used to estimate. Second, if several major disasters cluster in the same year, the entire ILS asset class can take losses simultaneously — the "low correlation" argument holds in ordinary years but can break down in an unusually severe one. Hurricane Ian's landfall in Florida in September 2022 illustrates that second caveat well. As initial damage estimates climbed, cat bond and ILS funds broadly faced mark-to-market loss projections in the mid-to-high single digits, and some funds running higher-risk strategies were reported to have taken losses in the double digits. Final losses came in well below the earliest fears once claims were actually settled, but industry-wide loss estimates for Ian itself still ranged as high as $40–60 billion, making clear that when a single catastrophe is large enough, cat bond investors can share in losses well outside the "low correlation" comfort the asset class usually offers.

## How Retail Investors Can (and Mostly Can't) Access This Market

Most cat bonds are issued under the U.S. "Rule 144A" exemption, restricting sales to qualified institutional buyers — which makes it nearly impossible for individual investors to purchase a cat bond directly. The realistic path in is indirect: specialist ILS fund managers run funds open to accredited or retail investors, and a small but growing number of ILS-linked exchange-traded products have emerged. Even these funds often carry high minimum investments or limited redemption windows, and performance depends heavily on the manager's skill at modeling reinsurance risk. That makes a manager's track record and the mix of trigger types a fund holds far more important than the headline yield alone.

## Key Takeaways

- A catastrophe bond is an insurance-linked security (ILS) that lets insurers and reinsurers slice off large natural-disaster risk through an SPV and sell it as a bond to capital markets investors.
- Investor principal sits in a collateral trust in safe assets; if no trigger fires during the bond's term, investors get principal and interest back in full, and if one does, they lose principal in proportion to the loss.
- Triggers fall into four types — indemnity, parametric, industry loss index and modeled loss — and the trade-off between settlement speed and basis risk defines an investor's real exposure.
- As Mexico's 2017 Chiapas earthquake bond shows, a parametric trigger pays out based on whether a predefined physical threshold was crossed, not on the actual scale of damage.
- Cat bonds have grown steadily as an institutional diversification tool because of their low correlation to stocks and bonds, but rising disaster frequency from climate change and the risk of several disasters clustering in one year are both factors to weigh.

## FAQ

### Is a catastrophe bond similar to a CoCo bond?
Structurally, both share the idea that a trigger event forces investors to absorb losses automatically, similar to [CoCo bonds (AT1 notes)](/en/basics/coco-bonds-at1-explained/). But a CoCo bond's trigger is a financial metric — a bank's capital ratio — while a cat bond's trigger is a physical or insurance metric tied to an actual natural disaster. The nature of the underlying risk is completely different even though the mechanical idea of an automatic, forced trigger is similar.

### How is a catastrophe bond different from a credit default swap (CDS)?
Both pay out when a defined risk event occurs, but a [CDS](/en/basics/credit-default-swap-cds/) is an over-the-counter derivative contract that trades an issuer's credit (default) risk, while a cat bond is an actual bond backed by real collateral that trades natural disaster risk. A CDS is a derivative contract with no principal changing hands upfront; a cat bond is a real security with principal actually at risk.

### Why does it matter that the cat bond market keeps growing?
A growing cat bond market means insurers and reinsurers have access to disaster-response funding beyond what traditional reinsurance capital alone can provide, by tapping the much larger pool of global capital markets. That cuts both ways: it strengthens the industry's capacity to absorb catastrophic losses, while giving institutional investors like pensions and hedge funds a new asset class with low correlation to traditional stocks and bonds.
