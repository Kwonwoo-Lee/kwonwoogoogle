## What Happened

After the market closed on Thursday, October 22, Deckers Outdoor Corporation (NYSE: DECK) - the parent company of HOKA running shoes and UGG boots - reported fiscal second-quarter 2027 results covering the July-to-September period. On the surface, the headline EPS number looked like a blowout. Diluted earnings per share came in at $2.31, nearly 29% above the Wall Street consensus of $1.79. Under normal circumstances, a beat of that size should send a stock higher. Instead, Deckers shares moved the opposite way. In after-hours trading right after the release, the stock fell roughly 8%, sliding into the high-$70s, and by premarket trading the next morning, Friday, October 23, the decline had widened to nearly 10%, pushing shares down toward the $77 level. Measured against Wednesday's close near $85, that is close to a tenth of the company's market value erased in about a day.

The first clue to this contradiction is revenue. Second-quarter sales came in at $1.48 billion, up 3.5% year-over-year but short of the roughly $1.51 billion analysts had modeled. According to the company, this was the first revenue miss in more than five years. A business that beats decisively on earnings but misses on revenue is an unusual combination, and the explanation lies in where that earnings beat actually came from. Deckers disclosed that approximately $0.28 of the quarter's $2.31 EPS was a one-time benefit tied to tariff refunds issued under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA). Strip that one-time item out, and most of the headline beat disappears.

The bigger problem showed up in full-year guidance. Deckers cut its fiscal 2027 revenue outlook to $5.700-$5.725 billion, down from a prior range of $5.86-$5.91 billion. HOKA's full-year revenue growth guidance was lowered from "up low double digits" to "up high single digits," while UGG's was cut from "up mid-single digits" to "up low single digits." Full-year adjusted EPS guidance, by contrast, was nominally raised, from $7.35-$7.50 to $7.80-$7.95 - but that increase included $0.52 per share of already-booked tariff refund benefit. Strip that out, and the real underlying EPS guidance is $7.28-$7.43, which is actually lower than the prior range. In other words: revenue guidance was unambiguously cut, and EPS guidance only looked higher because a one-time item was propping it up.

The broader market was weak the same day. The Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 652.82 points (1.23%) to close at 52,215.16, the S&P 500 slipped 0.52% to 7,623.68, and the Nasdaq Composite eased 0.21% to 25,955.34. Much of that broad-market weakness traced back to oil prices spiking to a five-month high after reports that the US and Israel had drawn up plans for renewed strikes on Iran. Even against that backdrop of general market softness, Deckers stood out as one of the hardest-hit individual names of the week.

## Why an EPS Beat Didn't Protect the Stock

The first thing to understand here is the quality of the earnings beat, not just its size. Investors typically reward an EPS beat because it signals the core business is generating more profit than expected. But in this case, $0.28 of the $2.31 EPS - a meaningful share of the entire beat - came from a tariff refund, which is an administrative repayment of taxes already paid, not revenue earned by selling more shoes or managing margins better. It took analysts and investors several hours after the release to work through the earnings call and press release and isolate this fact, and the stock kept drifting lower through after-hours trading and the next morning's premarket session as that realization spread.

The second key is the direction and composition of guidance, not just the headline number. It would be easy to read "adjusted EPS guidance raised to $7.80-$7.95" as unambiguously good news. But the entire $0.52 increase came from an already-recognized, one-time tax refund. Strip it out, and the real guidance for the underlying business actually went down, to $7.28-$7.43. The message buried inside those numbers is that the core business outlook got worse, and a one-time tax refund happened to be large enough to mask that deterioration in the headline figure. The market saw through the mask and priced the stock accordingly.

The third key is the qualitative difference between what's happening at HOKA versus UGG. HOKA revenue grew 6.8% year-over-year to $677.2 million - still growing, but well below the double-digit pace the prior guidance had assumed. UGG was the more worrying signal: direct-to-consumer (DTC) revenue fell 8% year-over-year to $146 million, pointing to an outright decline in demand for the brand's signature boots, not just slower growth. HOKA's story is deceleration; UGG's is contraction. The parallel cuts to both brands' full-year growth guidance simply reflect that two-speed weakness showing up at the same time.

Timing compounded the damage. The same day that Deckers' numbers were being digested, oil prices were spiking and the broader market was selling off on renewed Iran-related tension, leaving investors with less appetite to look past a "beat with an asterisk." A result that might have been shrugged off as "a decent quarter with some quality concerns" in a calm market got a harsher discount inside a day that was already risk-off.

## What to Take Away From This

- **Always check where an EPS beat actually came from.** When a meaningful share of a beat traces back to a one-time item - a tax refund, an asset sale, a legal settlement - that is not evidence the core business is improving. Listen for words like "one-time" or "non-recurring" on the earnings call and in the release before trusting the headline number.
- **A nominal guidance "raise" can still be a cut in disguise.** What matters more than the raw direction of a guidance number is what's driving the change. Try to back out one-time items yourself to see the real underlying trend - doing so can put you ahead of the market's own slower realization.
- **For multi-brand companies, look at segment-level numbers, not just the consolidated total.** Deckers' overall 3.5% revenue growth sounds merely soft; UGG's 8% DTC revenue decline is a much sharper, more specific warning sign buried one level down.
- **Macro conditions can amplify how the market reacts to a flawed earnings print.** A "low-quality beat" landing on a day when oil is spiking and major indexes are falling tends to get sold harder than the same news would on a calm trading day. Check the day's broader market context alongside any single earnings reaction.

## FAQ

### Deckers beat EPS estimates by almost 29%. Why is this being treated as bad news?

The headline $2.31 EPS did beat the $1.79 consensus, but about $0.28 of that came from a one-time tariff refund rather than core operations. More importantly, revenue missed estimates for the first time in over five years, full-year revenue guidance was clearly cut, and full-year EPS guidance - once the tariff refund is excluded - was effectively cut as well. The market weighted these underlying signals of a weakening business far more heavily than the inflated headline EPS number.

### Which brand is the bigger concern, HOKA or UGG?

HOKA is still growing, with revenue up 6.8% year-over-year, but that pace fell short of the double-digit growth the prior guidance had assumed. UGG is the more worrying case: its direct-to-consumer revenue declined 8% year-over-year, an outright contraction rather than just slower growth. Both brands had their full-year growth guidance cut, but UGG's underlying demand signal looks weaker than HOKA's.

### What exactly is this "tariff refund," and why did it boost EPS?

Certain US tariffs had been imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA), and companies have been receiving refunds of tariffs already paid after questions were raised about the legal basis for those duties. Deckers included refunds received through October 8 in both its quarterly results and its updated full-year guidance. Because this money is a repayment of taxes already paid rather than operating profit from selling more product, it is a one-time benefit that won't repeat every quarter - and the company explicitly said it did not assume any further refunds in its forward guidance.
