## When 95% Ownership Lets a Controlling Shareholder Force Out the Rest

[Stock Appraisal Rights](/en/basics/stock-appraisal-rights-explained/) covered a right that runs in one direction: a shareholder who opposes a merger demands the company buy back their shares. Korean law also contains a right running the exact opposite way. A controlling shareholder who owns 95% or more of a company can demand the remaining minority shareholders sell their stock to them — whether those holders want to or not. This is the **controlling shareholder's sell-demand right**, under Article 360-24 of Korea's Commercial Act, and it belongs to the family of mechanisms known internationally as a **squeeze-out** or **freeze-out**. Once the legal requirements are met, the minority shareholder is obligated to sell. Here's why this exists, what has to happen before it triggers, and how it relates to the appraisal right covered earlier.

## Why Bother Buying Out the Last Sliver

A shareholder already above 95% can single-handedly pass any special resolution — control isn't in question. So why acquire the remainder? Mostly administrative cost: even a tiny minority stake obligates the company to keep sending meeting notices, paying dividends, and maintaining a shareholder registry for every holder, and running that machinery for hundreds of near-zero stakes can cost more than simply buying them out. A second reason is structural efficiency — a wholly owned subsidiary can be restructured, merged, or folded into a [holding-company structure](/en/basics/holding-company-discount/) without needing minority consent. A third, common pattern pairs this with a [tender offer](/en/basics/mandatory-tender-offer-korea/): ownership gets pushed to 95% through tendering, and the sell-demand right mops up whoever didn't tender, clearing the way to delist.

## The Mechanism: 95%, Shareholder Approval, and a Two-Month Deadline

The requirements run in sequence. First, the controlling shareholder must hold **95% or more** of total issued shares "for its own account" — shares held by affiliated parent or subsidiary companies within the same group can be combined to reach that figure. Second, hitting 95% doesn't trigger anything automatically: the shareholder must state a legitimate business purpose and win approval at a shareholders' meeting, with the meeting notice disclosing current ownership, the stated purpose, and a certified appraiser's price assessment. Third, at least one month before issuing the actual demand, the shareholder must publicly announce it and notify each minority holder individually. Only then can the demand go out — and once it does, the minority shareholder must transfer their shares within **two months**. Price is set by agreement in principle, but if the two sides can't agree within 30 days, either side may petition a court to set it. A Korean Supreme Court ruling (2018Da224699) adds that the demand must cover a shareholder's *entire* stake — no picking some shares and leaving others.

## Why 95%, Not the 90% Used for Short-Form Mergers

Korean law already lets a 90%-plus shareholder push through a merger by board resolution alone, without a full vote. So why does this right sit at a higher 95%? Lowering it to match 90% was debated and rejected, because this right forcibly extinguishes property rights regardless of consent — a far more intrusive power than approving a restructuring. The higher bar restricts it to cases where control is truly overwhelming, limiting abuse. The same 95% threshold also runs in reverse: minority shareholders can themselves demand the controlling shareholder buy *their* shares. Either direction converges on the same outcome once ownership is this concentrated.

## Two Rights, Opposite Directions

The appraisal right and this sell-demand right sound similar and both end in compulsory transactions, but who initiates — and why — is reversed.

| | Minority appraisal right | Controlling shareholder's sell-demand right |
|---|---|---|
| Who initiates | A dissenting minority shareholder | A shareholder owning 95%+ |
| Triggered by | A merger or similar special resolution | A separately approved business purpose |
| Who must transact | The company (must buy) | The minority shareholder (must sell) |
| Minority's position | Optional — exercise it or keep holding | Mandatory once demanded |

The key row is "minority's position": appraisal rights are **optional and defensive**; the sell-demand right is a **one-sided obligation**.

## A Simplified Example

Company A was listed; controlling shareholder B built ownership to 96% via tender offers and open-market buying. The remaining 4% is spread across roughly 1,000 holders. B states a restructuring purpose, wins shareholder approval, and issues a sell-demand at ₩50,000 per share, backed by an appraiser's valuation. Holders must transfer within two months. If a holder disputes ₩50,000 and no deal is reached within 30 days, they can petition the court instead. A court-set ₩55,000 means B owes the difference; a lower court price means B pays less. The real fight here isn't over whether shares must be sold — it's over the price.

## What Investors Should Know

A shareholder receiving a sell-demand should first confirm every procedural step was met — the 95% threshold, shareholder approval, advance notice — since a defect can be grounds to contest it. If procedure checks out, the dispute shifts to price: whether the appraiser's number reasonably reflects recent trading or the company's asset and earnings value, weighed against the time and uncertainty of a court petition, much like the tradeoff discussed under [merger swap ratios](/en/basics/merger-swap-ratio-explained/). This area keeps evolving — a 2024 amendment bill before Korea's National Assembly would exclude treasury shares from the 95% calculation and require pricing records to stay available for inspection, both aimed at curbing abuse. Whether these changes take effect depends on the legislative process, so confirm current law with a professional before relying on any of this.

## Takeaway

- The controlling shareholder's sell-demand right (Commercial Act Article 360-24) lets a 95%+ owner, after shareholder approval, force remaining minority holders to sell — the mechanism known abroad as a squeeze-out or freeze-out.
- Controlling shareholders use it to cut administrative costs, complete a wholly owned subsidiary ahead of restructuring, or finish off the last stake after a tender offer before delisting.
- The 95% bar sits above the 90% used for short-form mergers specifically to limit abuse of a right that overrides minority property rights; the same threshold gives minority holders a mirror-image buyout right.
- Unlike the optional, defensive appraisal right, the sell-demand right is a one-sided obligation triggered by the controlling shareholder.
- A shareholder disputing the price can petition a court if talks fail within 30 days, though that takes time and doesn't guarantee a better outcome.

## FAQ

### Does crossing 95% ownership automatically transfer minority shares?
No. Owning 95% is only a precondition — the controlling shareholder must also state a business purpose, win shareholder approval, and complete advance notice before any demand can be issued.

### Can a minority shareholder just refuse and keep holding?
If the demand meets every legal requirement, they're obligated to sell their entire stake, though they can still dispute the price through negotiation or a court petition, or challenge the demand if the procedure was flawed.

### Is this the same as a tender offer used to delist a company?
No. A [tender offer](/en/basics/mandatory-tender-offer-korea/) is voluntary — shareholders choose whether to tender. The sell-demand right is compulsory and only available once ownership already reaches 95%. In practice they're often sequential: a tender offer pushes ownership toward 95%, and the sell-demand right mops up whatever didn't tender.
