## What Happened

This site reported on November 15 that Fed Governor Christopher Waller had floated a conditional 50-basis-point "jumbo" hike for December, pushing the implied odds of any December hike to roughly 99% (see our [earlier coverage](/en/news/fed-waller-half-point-december-hike-odds-99-percent/)). Three days later, on Wednesday, November 18, the Fed released the official minutes of its October 28-29 FOMC meeting, and the debate picked up two important new layers.

The first is what the minutes themselves confirmed about the nature of the internal split. The committee had held its benchmark rate at 3.75%-4.00%, and it was already known that Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack and Fed Governor Lisa Cook had dissented, arguing for an immediate quarter-point hike. What the newly released minutes add is that a broader group of officials - not just the two dissenters - flagged AI-related capital spending on chips, data centers and power as a source of inflation pressure that could persist even as the labor market cools. That's the same "AI-driven inflation" argument that first surfaced in the Fed's July minutes (see our [prior coverage](/en/news/fed-minutes-three-dissents-since-2016-september-hike-odds-32-percent/)), and its reappearance in October shows it wasn't a one-off talking point.

The second new layer is St. Louis Fed President Alberto Musalem's warning, delivered the same day the minutes were released: as many as four additional rate increases could be necessary by the spring of 2027. That's a materially longer tightening path than the binary Waller had framed - go big once in December, or go small while clearly pre-committing to January. It's worth noting Musalem doesn't hold an FOMC vote this year.

Hammack herself, despite having pushed for an immediate hike in October, said she now favors smaller, steadier increases over a sudden 50bp jump heading into December. Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan, who does vote in December, holds a similar gradualist view, saying at least two more quarter-point hikes are still needed. The result is a committee where almost no one disputes that more tightening is coming - the argument has moved entirely to how big and how fast.

Markets' initial reaction was comparatively muted. Futures are pricing a quarter-point hike at the December 9-10 meeting as close to a certainty, while largely dismissing the 50bp scenario as a minority view rather than a real possibility. That reading lines up with a Reuters poll of 101 economists conducted November 6-10: every single respondent expected a 25bp hike to 4.00%-4.25% on December 9. The real split shows up one layer deeper - 56 of the 101 expect December to be the final hike of this cycle, while the other 45 (including more than half of surveyed primary dealers) expect at least one more increase after that.

## Why This Round of Minutes Reads Differently From July's

Back in August, this site covered the July FOMC minutes, where a hawkish three-way dissent had already been overtaken by events: weak jobs and retail-sales data released in the weeks after the meeting dragged September hike odds from 82% down to 32% by the time the minutes were even published. That was a case of stale news arriving late. This time, the pattern runs the opposite direction. The hawkish case in the minutes wasn't undercut by what came after - it was reinforced. A hotter-than-expected October CPI print was followed, on the very day the minutes were released, by sitting officials publicly pushing an even more aggressive line. In other words, this isn't a month-old argument catching up to reality; it's an argument still actively building in real time.

That distinction matters for investors because the uncertainty has shifted, not disappeared. In a typical tightening cycle, the central question is usually "hike or hold." Here, all 101 surveyed economists already agree on "hike" - the live question is whether December closes the chapter or opens three or four more months of it. For equity markets already sitting in correction territory, that means the December FOMC decision may not be a single resolved event; it could be the first of several moments where the market has to re-price the entire tightening path, not just one meeting's outcome.

Waller's specific logic for going big is worth dwelling on, because it isn't simply "inflation is bad, therefore 50bp." His argument was conditional and strategic: if the Fed can credibly commit to a gradual path and have markets believe it, a smaller move suffices; if it can't establish that credibility, front-loading a bigger hike removes the ambiguity in one shot. Hammack and Logan's gradualist counterargument is essentially the opposite bet - that abrupt policy swings risk shocking financial markets more than the inflation problem itself warrants. The result is an unusual alignment: this isn't hawks against doves, it's a split between two flavors of hawk, one betting on shock-and-awe and the other on steady, telegraphed pressure.

## What to Take Away From This

- **The Fed debate has moved from "whether" to "how much and how fast."** With all 101 surveyed economists agreeing a December hike is coming, the variable worth tracking has shifted from hike-versus-hold to 25bp-versus-50bp, and whether December is the last word or the first of several.
- **The same official can hold different positions at different meetings.** Hammack argued for an immediate hike in October but prefers a gradual path into December - a reminder that labeling an official simply "hawkish" or "dovish" misses how their view can shift meeting to meeting.
- **A non-voting official's comments still move markets.** Musalem has no FOMC vote this year, but his four-hikes warning can still push markets to re-price the tightening path out to 2027. Voting power and market influence aren't the same thing.
- **Look past the headline consensus to the dispersion underneath it.** A 101-for-101 agreement on a December hike sounds like certainty, but the near 56-45 split on what happens after it shows the real uncertainty sits one layer deeper than the top-line number suggests.

## FAQ

### How does a 50bp "jumbo" hike affect markets differently than a standard 25bp move?
A quarter-point hike is the size markets typically expect and have already priced in, so it tends to produce a smaller shock. A half-point move is unusual enough that it reads as a signal about how seriously the Fed views the inflation problem, and it tends to hit bond yields, the dollar, and growth-stock valuations more abruptly. Futures markets currently treat the 50bp scenario as unlikely rather than the base case.

### Who actually votes at the December FOMC meeting?
FOMC votes belong to all seven Fed governors (fewer if there are vacancies), the New York Fed president, and four of the other eleven regional Fed presidents on a rotating annual basis. Of the officials named here, Dallas Fed's Lorie Logan votes this year, while St. Louis Fed's Alberto Musalem does not. Non-voters can still move market expectations through public remarks, which is exactly what happened here.

### Why is the market treating 25bp, not 50bp, as the base case despite Waller's comments?
Several officials who do vote in December, including Logan and Hammack, have said they favor a gradual approach, and the Fed has historically been reluctant to spring an unsignaled jumbo hike on markets without clear advance guidance. That said, if the October personal consumption expenditures (PCE) inflation data due November 25 comes in well above expectations, this base case could shift quickly.
