## Two Banks, Same Profit, Opposite Dividend Decisions

[Why Bank Stocks Trade on P/B](/en/basics/bank-stock-pbr-nim-valuation/) covered how a bank's ROE and net interest margin (NIM) drive its valuation. But scan recent earnings headlines from Korean (or any) financial holding companies, and one number tends to get mentioned before net income or ROE: "CET1 ratio hits 13%, buyback and cancellation expanded." It's common to see two banks post similar profit growth in the same quarter, yet one raises its dividend and buyback while the other holds back, citing the need to "build capital first." That gap can't be explained by profit alone. Understanding it means understanding a regulatory ledger that applies only to banks: capital adequacy.

## Why Banks Get Their Own Rulebook

When an ordinary manufacturer fails, the loss lands on its shareholders and creditors. When a bank fails, the damage can spread fast to depositors and the whole financial system, because banking is structurally a high-leverage business — a bank runs a balance sheet many times larger than its own equity. That's why regulators worldwide follow a common standard set by the Basel Committee on Banking Supervision, housed at the Bank for International Settlements (BIS) — the framework commonly called "Basel III." It requires banks to hold a minimum cushion of capital at all times. The name "BIS ratio" comes directly from that institution.

## The Denominator: Risk-Weighted Assets — Not All Loans Weigh the Same

The core formula is simple: **capital ÷ assets**. The twist is that the denominator isn't total assets — it's **risk-weighted assets (RWA)**. A government bond, carrying almost no default risk, gets a risk weight near 0%. An unsecured consumer loan to a low-credit borrower can carry a risk weight well over 100%. Lend the same $100 million to a blue-chip company versus a subprime borrower, and the two loans add very different amounts of "weight" to the denominator. This means a bank can't just chase loan growth for its own sake — every new loan also has to be weighed against how much capital-ratio room it eats up.

## The Numerator: Not All Capital Is Equal — BIS Ratio, Tier 1, CET1

The numerator, "capital," also comes in different grades under Basel III, ranked by how well each layer absorbs losses:

- **Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1)**: capital raised from common shares plus retained earnings — the purest, highest-quality layer. It's permanent and dividends can be adjusted, so it absorbs losses first and most flexibly.
- **Tier 1 capital**: CET1 plus Additional Tier 1 instruments such as [contingent convertible bonds, or CoCo bonds](/en/basics/coco-bonds-at1-explained/).
- **Total capital** (the numerator of the headline BIS ratio): Tier 1 plus Tier 2 supplementary capital, such as subordinated debt.

All three ratios share the same denominator (RWA), so by definition **BIS ratio ≥ Tier 1 ratio ≥ CET1 ratio**, always. Regulators and markets care most about CET1 specifically, because AT1 and Tier 2 instruments mostly behave like capital in calm times but only convert or get written down in a genuine crisis — they're "contingent." CET1, by contrast, is capital that absorbs losses right now, which is why it's treated as the strictest and most scrutinized of the three.

## Why CET1 Works Like a Switch for Shareholder Returns

Here's the link that matters to an investor. Korean financial holding companies have increasingly tied their shareholder-return policy directly to CET1: a public commitment like "once CET1 hits 13%, 50% of the capital above that target goes to dividends and buybacks." That replaces ad-hoc dividend decisions with a rule investors can actually forecast. The mechanism: any profit a bank doesn't pay out as dividends or use for buybacks gets added to retained earnings, which directly enlarges the CET1 numerator. Simply retaining earnings pushes the ratio up over time. Once the ratio clears its target, the excess is reclassified from "crisis buffer" to "capital that can safely go back to shareholders." Run it in reverse: if bad loans rise in a downturn, or a regulator revises how risk weights are calculated and RWA jumps, CET1 can fall below target even while profit is rising — and in that case, policy can swing toward retaining more capital and pulling back on payouts, profit growth notwithstanding.

## A Numerical Example

Say Bank A has CET1 capital of $10 billion against risk-weighted assets of $80 billion:

```
CET1 ratio = $10B ÷ $80B = 12.5%
```

If Bank A has publicly targeted a 13% CET1 ratio, it's still 0.5 points short. To close that gap with RWA unchanged, capital would need to reach $10.4 billion (13% of $80B) — so the bank likely keeps retaining a larger share of profit for now to grow the numerator. Bank B, by contrast, already sits at 13.5% CET1. The 0.5-point excess above its own target gets classified as capital with no further reason to sit idle, freeing it up for bigger dividends or buybacks and cancellations. Two banks with identical profit growth can land on very different payout decisions purely because of where each one sits relative to its own target.

Now flip the scenario: if the economy slows and the mix shifts toward lower-rated corporate loans, or a regulator raises the risk weight on some asset class, RWA might rise from $80 billion to $85 billion with capital unchanged. CET1 then falls from 12.5% to roughly 11.8% ($10B ÷ $85B) — with no new lending and no drop in profit, purely because the denominator grew. In response, a bank might pull back on originating higher-risk-weight loans, or issue more AT1 instruments to rebuild the numerator. The denominator alone can reshape both lending strategy and the pace of shareholder returns.

## Minimum Requirement vs. Management Target Are Different Numbers

One distinction worth keeping straight: Basel III's legal CET1 minimum (typically around 4.5%) is not the same as the "required ratio" regulators actually enforce, which stacks several buffers on top — a capital conservation buffer, an extra buffer for systemically important banks, a countercyclical buffer — nor is it the same as the "management target" a bank voluntarily discloses to investors. Falling below the regulatory requirement lets authorities force restrictions on dividends and bonus payouts; missing a voluntary management target carries no legal force, but breaks a promise to the market, which banks have strong incentive to keep. When a headline says "CET1 hits 13%," that's almost always the management target, not the legal floor — worth not confusing the two.

Regulators also run periodic **stress tests** — simulating a severe recession or asset-price crash — to check whether CET1 would still clear the minimum even under that scenario. A bank can look healthy on today's numbers and still have its dividend or buyback plan blocked if it fails a stress test. CET1 is both a snapshot of today and a pre-cleared margin of safety against tomorrow's shock.

## Do Insurers and Brokers Have Something Similar?

Yes — each type of financial institution runs its own version of this logic. Insurers are measured under risk-based capital frameworks (Korea's K-ICS, for example), and brokerages under net capital ratio rules. The specific risk factors differ by sector — insurers weigh underwriting and market risk, brokers weigh market and counterparty risk — but the underlying logic is identical: divide capital by a measure of risk exposure, use that cushion to judge safety, and tie it to how much can flow back to shareholders. For financial-sector stocks, that capital-adequacy ratio is the equivalent of the debt-to-equity ratio you'd check for an industrial company — the first thing to look up before anything else.

## What to Check in Earnings Reports

Alongside net income and ROE, check three things. First, whether CET1 moved up or down from the prior quarter or year — that tells you whether RWA growth or earnings retention won the race. Second, how far the bank's actual CET1 sits above or below its own stated target — the gap is roughly the ceiling on near-term extra shareholder returns. Third, watch for regulatory shifts like the finalized Basel III rules (sometimes called "Basel III Endgame"), where a change in how risk weights are calculated can mechanically push CET1 down even with flat profit. One number — the BIS ratio — ends up telling you not just how safe a bank is, but how much more it's likely to hand back to you.

## Takeaways

- The **BIS ratio** divides capital by risk-weighted assets to measure a bank's capital adequacy, with CET1 < Tier 1 < Total Capital (BIS ratio) forming progressively broader definitions of capital.
- **Risk-weighted assets (RWA)** apply different weights by default risk, so the same loan volume can strain the ratio differently depending on asset quality.
- **CET1** isolates only the highest-quality capital and is the strictest benchmark regulators and markets use to judge bank soundness.
- Korean financial holding companies publicly tie dividends and buybacks to CET1 targets, effectively making the ratio a switch that governs shareholder-return policy.
- When reading earnings, track CET1's direction and its gap to target alongside net income and ROE — that combination tells you where shareholder-return policy is headed before the headline number does.

## FAQ

### Which matters more, the BIS ratio or the CET1 ratio?
Both share the same denominator, but CET1 isolates the capital that can absorb losses immediately, so regulators and markets treat it as the stricter benchmark. A bank can post a healthy BIS ratio while its CET1 ratio is comparatively weaker if a large share of its capital sits in AT1 or Tier 2 instruments rather than common equity.

### Is a higher CET1 ratio always better?
It's safer, yes, but holding an unusually high ratio for a long stretch can also mean capital isn't being deployed efficiently into lending or investment, which drags on ROE. That's why banks typically set a target range they consider "safe enough," and return anything above it to shareholders rather than let it sit idle.

### How would the finalized Basel III rules affect bank stocks?
Jurisdictions have been discussing more conservative ways of calculating risk-weighted assets under the finalized Basel III framework. Holding the same assets, a bank facing higher RWA under the new rules sees its CET1 ratio fall mechanically, which could force some banks to slow shareholder returns while they rebuild toward target. Timelines and exact requirements are still being finalized and vary by jurisdiction, so check the latest official guidance from your market's regulator before drawing conclusions for any specific stock.
