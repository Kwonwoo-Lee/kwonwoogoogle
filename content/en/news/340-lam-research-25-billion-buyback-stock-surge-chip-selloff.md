## What Happened

Thursday, November 5 was a historic down day on Wall Street. Fears of incoming "AI data center regulation" in the wake of the midterm elections sent the Nasdaq Composite plunging more than 5.5%, and the entire semiconductor value chain - anything tied to AI infrastructure spending - got swept up in the selloff. Chip equipment maker Lam Research (NASDAQ: LRCX) was no exception, falling by a double-digit percentage along with its peers that day. But on that same Thursday, Lam's board of directors made a decision that pointed in the exact opposite direction of the market's panic: it authorized a new $25 billion share repurchase program and declared a quarterly dividend of $0.33 per share, payable January 6, 2027. The new authorization stacks on top of whatever balance remained from Lam's prior buyback program, carries no expiration date, and - as the company's own filing notes - can be suspended or discontinued at any time, with no obligation to actually spend the full amount.

The market's reaction was immediate. In premarket trading the following morning, Friday, November 6, Lam's shares spiked as much as 11.45%, touching $206.31. Gains held up through much of the regular session too, though the exact closing move varied by source and by what time of day it was measured - 24/7 Wall St., for instance, reported a 7.43% gain to $198.96. Whichever figure you use, the conclusion is the same: Lam's bounce that day was noticeably bigger than those of fellow chip names that had been hammered in the same Thursday rout, including Nvidia (+5.6%), AMD, Intel and Micron. In other words, on top of the sector-wide bargain-hunting rally that lifted essentially every chip stock that Friday, Lam had its own, company-specific catalyst layered on - and that extra catalyst is what set its move apart.

The size of the authorization itself is worth putting in context. Lam's market capitalization was roughly $287-297 billion as of late October, which puts the new $25 billion program at somewhere around 8-9% of the entire company's market value. The timing is notable for another reason too: just two weeks earlier, Lam had reported record free cash flow of $3.24 billion for its fiscal Q1 2027 (the quarter ended in September) - up roughly 103% year over year. That same quarter, the company returned about 41% of its free cash flow to shareholders through roughly $1 billion in buybacks and $325 million in dividends, continuing the long-term capital-return policy that CFO Doug Bettinger first outlined back in May 2024: returning 75-100% of free cash flow to shareholders via dividends and buybacks.

## Why a Buyback Announcement Can Work So Well in the Middle of a Selloff

Buybacks move stock prices through two distinct channels. The first is purely mechanical: when a company buys back its own shares and retires them, the number of shares outstanding shrinks, which automatically raises earnings per share even if net income doesn't grow at all. A higher EPS, applied to the same price-to-earnings multiple, mathematically implies a higher "fair" share price. The second channel is signaling. When management authorizes a massive buyback the day after its own stock craters by double digits, that timing itself sends an unusually strong message: "we believe the current price badly undershoots our own view of intrinsic value." That signal carries even more weight when it's backed by a concrete financial data point - in this case, a record quarterly free cash flow print - because it tells investors the buyback is funded by actual cash generation, not debt taken on in a moment of overreaction.

That said, there's a crucial distinction investors need to keep in mind: authorization is not the same thing as execution. As Lam itself disclosed, the $25 billion program has no end date and carries no legal obligation to actually spend that amount. Last quarter, the company's actual repurchase pace was about $1 billion. At that rate, fully using the new authorization would take years, not months. What the market cheered on November 6 wasn't the idea that $25 billion was about to flood into the stock overnight - it was the reassurance that Lam has enough cash-generating power to keep supporting EPS for years to come, and the reaffirmed intent to actually deploy that cash toward shareholders rather than, say, an acquisition.

It's also worth separating two forces that happened to land on the same day. Nvidia, AMD, Intel and Micron all rose that Friday too, reflecting broad, sector-wide bargain hunting after Thursday's oversold conditions - a technical bounce that had nothing to do with any one company's news. Lam's outsized move, by contrast, came specifically from its own capital-return announcement. Conflating the two risks the kind of sweeping conclusion that's easy to reach but hard to support - "chip stocks have bottomed" - when what actually happened is that a sector-wide technical rebound happened to coincide with one company's unrelated capital allocation decision. Neither one, on its own, resolves the underlying political and regulatory uncertainty around AI data center spending that triggered Thursday's crash in the first place.

## What to Take Away From This

The first lesson is not to be swayed by the headline dollar figure of a buyback authorization alone - check the company's actual track record of executing past programs. Lam's $25 billion authorization carries more credibility than an identical headline number from a company announcing its first-ever buyback, precisely because Lam has consistently returned 75-100% of free cash flow to shareholders for years running.

The second lesson is to separate the causes behind an outsized move. When one stock rises noticeably more than its sector peers on the same day, ask whether that extra gain reflects a sector-wide factor or genuinely company-specific news. Lam's case was relatively easy to untangle because the buyback catalyst was clearly disclosed, but in murkier cases, it's easy to mistake a stock simply riding a sector rally for one with real improving fundamentals.

The third lesson is that a buyback is not a cure-all. If the root cause of a selloff is political or regulatory uncertainty around an entire industry's spending plans, a company repurchasing its own shares does nothing to resolve that uncertainty directly. A buyback shrinks share count and signals management confidence, but it can't reverse a structural shift in industry demand or policy risk - and investors should keep those two effects mentally separate.

## FAQ

### Why does it matter whether a buyback is "authorized" versus actually "executed"?
An authorization is simply the maximum amount a company's board has permitted it to spend - it carries no legal requirement that the company actually spend that much, or spend it quickly. Lam's $25 billion program has no end date and can be paused or canceled at any time. Based on last quarter's actual pace of roughly $1 billion, fully using the authorization could take years, which is why the historical execution rate matters more than the headline number.

### Why announce a buyback the day right after the stock crashed?
This isn't a coincidence - it's a classic pattern. When management believes a stock has been sold off in a panic disconnected from the underlying business, authorizing a large buyback at that exact moment sends the strongest possible signal that the current price undershoots intrinsic value. It helps that Lam had just reported record quarterly free cash flow, which gave the market concrete evidence that the buyback is financially sustainable rather than a stretch.

### How big is $25 billion relative to Lam's overall size?
Based on a market capitalization of roughly $287-297 billion in late October, $25 billion works out to about 8-9% of the company's total market value. Because actual repurchases are likely to be spread out over several years rather than executed all at once, it's worth avoiding the misreading that 8-9% of shares will be pulled from the market in one shot.
