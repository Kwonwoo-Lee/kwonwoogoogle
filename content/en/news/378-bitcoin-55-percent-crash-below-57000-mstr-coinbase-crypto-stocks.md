## What Happened

Bitcoin's slide deepened again this week, with the largest cryptocurrency breaking below $57,000 - a level that puts it roughly 55% below the all-time high of approximately $126,200 it set in October 2025. The drop capped a brutal stretch that saw bitcoin fall below $60,000 on November 20, sliding 5.6% that session alone to around $59,326, after an earlier liquidation cascade had already pushed it down to roughly $62,000 on November 16. Each leg down has come faster than the last, and the cumulative decline now ranks among the deepest drawdowns in bitcoin's history, even if traders disagree about whether it is also the longest.

The selloff has not stayed confined to crypto exchanges. U.S.-listed companies with direct bitcoin exposure have taken some of the hardest hits of the entire stock market this month. Strategy (Nasdaq: MSTR), the business-intelligence company that transformed itself into the largest corporate holder of bitcoin with roughly 840,000 BTC on its balance sheet, has fallen for six of its last seven trading sessions. Coinbase (Nasdaq: COIN) touched its lowest level since November 2023 during the same stretch, compounding a stock that was already under pressure after its Q3 2026 revenue came in down 35% year-over-year. Bitcoin mining stocks and other crypto-adjacent names have moved in sympathy, extending losses alongside the underlying asset.

Sentiment gauges confirm just how one-sided the selling has become. The Crypto Fear & Greed Index - a widely cited composite that blends volatility, momentum, social media sentiment, and derivatives positioning into a single 0-to-100 reading - fell as low as 10 in mid-November, deep in "Extreme Fear" territory. For context, that same index sat at 80, solidly in "Greed," as recently as late August. Few sentiment shifts of that magnitude happen within a single quarter, and the speed of the reversal is itself a signal that something changed in how the market is pricing risk, not just in bitcoin's spot price.

## Why a Crypto Drop Is a U.S. Stock Market Story

It's tempting to treat bitcoin's price as a crypto-only matter, disconnected from the Nasdaq and S&P 500. But that view misses how deeply intertwined bitcoin has become with U.S. equity markets since spot bitcoin ETFs launched and companies like Strategy built their entire corporate identity around holding the asset. When bitcoin falls this hard, it doesn't just hurt people who hold the coin directly - it mechanically drags down a basket of publicly traded, U.S.-listed stocks that institutional and retail investors hold inside ordinary brokerage accounts and 401(k)s.

Strategy's situation illustrates the mechanism most clearly. The company has spent years raising debt and issuing new shares specifically to buy more bitcoin, effectively turning its stock into a leveraged bet on the coin's price. When bitcoin trades below the average price Strategy paid for its holdings - reportedly around $75,452 per coin - the market has to confront the possibility that the company's balance sheet, once a selling point, has become a liability. Making matters worse, MSCI announced it will remove Strategy from its MSCI USA Index, and by extension the broader MSCI World and ACWI indexes, effective after the close on November 30. MSCI's new screening rule treats companies whose operating assets fall short of 50% of total assets as non-operating issuers - a definition that was clearly written with treasury companies like Strategy in mind, even though it never mentions bitcoin by name. Because index funds are contractually obligated to track their benchmark, that removal forces automatic selling regardless of what anyone thinks bitcoin is actually worth. JPMorgan analysts have estimated the MSCI exclusion alone could trigger roughly $2.8 billion in outflows, with additional billions possible if other index providers follow suit.

Coinbase represents a different but related dynamic. As the largest U.S.-listed cryptocurrency exchange, its revenue is directly tied to how much trading volume moves through its platform - and trading volume tends to collapse, not grow, during a deep and demoralizing selloff, because retail traders who would normally buy dips become reluctant to add new capital. That's part of why Coinbase's own Q3 revenue already showed a steep year-over-year decline even before this latest leg of the crash. A falling bitcoin price doesn't just erase paper gains on a balance sheet - for an exchange, it can shrink the underlying business itself.

More broadly, this crash is unfolding at the same time that AI-related megacap tech stocks have been wobbling on their own worries - questions about valuations, spending commitments, and (as seen in recent Nvidia-related headlines) regulatory scrutiny. Bitcoin and high-growth tech stocks have increasingly traded as parts of the same "risk-on" basket in recent years: when investors feel comfortable taking risk, both tend to rally together, and when sentiment sours, both tend to sell off together. A bitcoin crash of this size adds one more data point suggesting that broader risk appetite - not any single company's fundamentals - is driving a meaningful share of what's moving U.S. markets right now.

## What to Take Away From This

- **A "Fear & Greed" reading near 10 is itself informative.** Sentiment indexes don't predict the future, but a reading that extreme tells you the market has become heavily one-sided. Historically, extended stretches of "Extreme Fear" have sometimes preceded local bottoms - but they've also sometimes preceded further declines, which is exactly why relying on sentiment alone as a buy signal is risky.
- **A leveraged balance sheet amplifies both gains and losses.** Strategy's stock has historically moved by a larger percentage than bitcoin itself on both the way up and the way down, because the company's bitcoin holdings are financed partly with debt. Any time you see a stock marketed as a way to get "amplified" exposure to an underlying asset, assume the downside is amplified in exactly the same proportion as the upside.
- **Forced index-fund selling is a real, mechanical risk - separate from a company's fundamentals.** MSCI's decision to drop Strategy means index funds must sell regardless of their own view on bitcoin's prospects. This kind of structural, calendar-driven selling pressure can push a stock lower even if nothing about the underlying business has actually changed.
- **An exchange's revenue is a bet on trading activity, not just asset prices.** Coinbase's business model means it can suffer even when it holds no bitcoin on its own balance sheet, simply because its customers trade less during a demoralizing bear market. When evaluating any trading-platform stock, separate the health of the platform's business from the price of the asset it trades.
- **Correlated "risk-on" assets can fall together without a shared cause.** Bitcoin and growth tech stocks don't always move together for company-specific reasons; sometimes it's simply that both are classified by large institutional investors as risk assets to be trimmed in the same moment. Recognizing that correlation helps you avoid assuming there's always a direct causal story linking two assets that happen to be falling on the same day.

## FAQ

### Why is bitcoin falling so much right now?
Reporting points to several overlapping causes: a cascade of forced liquidations among leveraged traders once bitcoin broke below key technical levels, sustained outflows from U.S. spot bitcoin ETFs, rising Treasury yields and a more hawkish Federal Reserve rate outlook that makes non-yielding assets like bitcoin less attractive, and broader nervousness about risk assets that has also weighed on AI-related tech stocks. No single catalyst fully explains the move; it appears to be a combination of technical selling pressure and a genuine shift in risk appetite.

### How does bitcoin's current drop compare to past crypto bear markets?
Bitcoin's prior major bear markets - 2018-2019 and 2022-2023 - each saw the price fall more than 75% from its peak and lasted well over a year from top to bottom. The current decline, at roughly 55% from the October 2025 high, is not yet as deep as those historical troughs, though analysts are divided on whether it has further to fall or whether a bottom is closer than the sentiment data suggests.

### Why would a stock exchange removal (MSCI) matter if a company's business hasn't changed?
Large institutional funds that track an index are often required by their own mandates to hold exactly what's in that index, in the same proportions. When MSCI removes a stock from an index, every fund benchmarked to that index must sell its shares by the effective date, regardless of their own opinion of the company. That selling pressure is mechanical and forced, not a reflection of a fresh judgment about the company's prospects - but it can still move the share price meaningfully simply because of the volume involved.

### Does a falling bitcoin price directly hurt the stock market as a whole?
Not uniformly. The direct impact falls hardest on companies with explicit bitcoin exposure, like Strategy, Coinbase, and bitcoin mining companies, along with bitcoin ETF holders. The broader S&P 500 and Nasdaq are affected more indirectly, through shared "risk-on" sentiment and because a handful of crypto-exposed names carry meaningful weight in certain sector indexes. It's a connected but not identical story to the swings in megacap AI stocks.
