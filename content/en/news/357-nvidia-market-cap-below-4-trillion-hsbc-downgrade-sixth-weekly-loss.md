## What Happened

On Friday, November 13, Nvidia (NASDAQ: NVDA) closed at $162.85, down about 2% on the day, pushing its market capitalization to roughly $3.92 trillion. That matters because it's the first time Nvidia's market cap has closed below the $4 trillion mark since July 2025 - a threshold the company spent more than a year building up to, and one investors had come to treat as a kind of psychological floor for the world's most valuable public company. The move capped a brutal week: Nvidia shares fell 12.75% over the five sessions, their sixth consecutive weekly decline, a losing streak long enough that some market data providers flagged it as the stock's worst stretch in well over a decade.

The proximate trigger on Friday was a ratings cut. HSBC lowered its rating on Nvidia to Hold from a more bullish stance and slashed its price target from $375 to $170 - a reduction of more than 50% in a single call, and one of the more dramatic target cuts any major bank has issued on a mega-cap stock this year. HSBC's note pointed to the unresolved leadership vacuum at the top of the company rather than any new concern about chip demand or AI spending itself. That distinction matters: this wasn't a case of a bank souring on Nvidia's underlying business, but one souring on the uncertainty now surrounding who runs it and how that uncertainty could ripple into customer and supplier relationships in the near term.

The leadership vacuum in question dates back to the week's opening story. On November 8, Nvidia's board opened an independent investigation into CEO Jensen Huang's personal relationships, including whether he had been the target of a foreign intelligence effort. By November 11, the Wall Street Journal reported the board was preparing to remove him, and by November 12, Nvidia confirmed Huang had resigned as President, CEO, and board member effective immediately, with CFO Colette Kress named interim CEO and Harvey C. Jones installed as the company's first independent board chair. By Friday, Yahoo Finance was reporting that a third affair allegation involving Huang had surfaced, extending a story that had already dominated the market's attention for most of the week. Since the scandal first broke, Nvidia has shed roughly $490 billion in market value from the prior week's close near $4.5 trillion - a sum larger than the entire market capitalization of all but a small handful of U.S. companies.

Despite the single-stock carnage, the broader market held up far better. The Dow Jones Industrial Average actually closed higher on Friday, up 50.46 points (0.1%) to 52,347.55. The S&P 500 slipped 30.73 points (0.4%) to 7,561.67, and the Nasdaq Composite, more exposed to the mega-cap tech names at the center of the storm, fell 229.82 points (0.91%) to 25,136.27. All three indexes still finished the week higher than where they started, which tells you this was a concentrated, company-specific unwind rather than a broad risk-off move across U.S. equities.

## Why a Single Stock Can Move Like This Without Dragging the Whole Market Down

The mechanics here are worth walking through, because they explain both why Nvidia alone can lose nearly half a trillion dollars in value in a matter of days and why that loss didn't translate into a market-wide crash. Nvidia's weight in the S&P 500 and Nasdaq 100 is large enough that its moves are always felt at the index level, but a company-specific governance crisis - as opposed to, say, a disappointing demand signal from a major customer or a surprise in chip export policy - tends to stay relatively contained to the stock and its closest peers. Investors can distinguish between "the story everyone was buying has gotten structurally worse" and "a specific, resolvable leadership problem has created short-term uncertainty," and they priced this episode closer to the second category. That's precisely why HSBC's note framed the downgrade around governance risk rather than around any change to Nvidia's revenue outlook, GPU roadmap, or data-center order book.

The $4 trillion threshold itself deserves attention as a psychological and technical marker, separate from the fundamentals. Round-number market-cap milestones function the way round-number price levels do for individual traders: they attract attention precisely because so many people are watching them, which can create self-reinforcing selling (or buying) around the level. Nvidia first closed above $4 trillion only in July 2025, meaning the stock gave back more than a year of market-cap gains relative to that marker in the space of about two and a half weeks. Algorithmic strategies that track index weightings, as well as discretionary funds with mandates tied to market-cap thresholds, can accelerate moves through such levels in either direction, which is part of why Friday's close below the line drew outsized attention relative to the 2% daily move that caused it.

The sixth-straight-weekly-loss framing is the other number worth sitting with. A single bad week is market noise; six in a row for a stock this large, this closely tracked, and this central to a trillion-dollar investment thesis around AI infrastructure spending is a genuinely rare occurrence, and it signals that sentiment has shifted from "buying every dip" to something closer to "waiting for clarity." The timing compounds the pressure: Nvidia reports fiscal third-quarter earnings on Tuesday, November 17, just days after this leadership crisis and ratings cut. That call will be the first time an interim CEO has to answer not just for the quarter's numbers but for whether the company's customer relationships, supply agreements, and employee retention have held up through a month of public turmoil at the top.

HSBC's price-target math is also instructive for anyone trying to parse what a bank-wide price target actually represents. Cutting a target from $375 to $170 isn't simply "the stock is worth less because the CEO left" - it reflects a bank re-rating the appropriate valuation multiple investors should pay for the same future earnings stream, given a wider range of possible outcomes (a smooth transition, a messy one, customer defections, or a quick resolution with a strong permanent CEO hire). When uncertainty about management and governance widens, analysts often apply a larger discount to forward earnings even if their underlying revenue and margin forecasts for the business haven't changed much at all - which is a different mechanism from a straightforward earnings miss or demand slowdown, and one investors frequently conflate with the latter when they see a headline target cut of this size.

## What to Take Away From This

A leadership crisis at a single company, however dramatic, does not automatically become a market-wide event. The Dow's gain and the broader market's weekly advance alongside Nvidia's rout is a useful reminder to separate single-stock, governance-driven selloffs from macro-driven ones - they call for different responses and carry different implications for a diversified portfolio.

Round-number thresholds like a $4 trillion market cap matter more as attention magnets and potential inflection points for algorithmic and mandate-driven flows than as any kind of fundamental valuation signal on their own. Don't read too much economic meaning into a stock crossing a psychological line; read the news driving the move instead.

A sharp analyst price-target cut is not always a verdict on the underlying business. When a cut is explicitly tied to governance or leadership uncertainty rather than a change in revenue or margin assumptions, it reflects a wider range of possible outcomes being priced in - and that range can narrow quickly once there's more clarity, which is exactly the kind of event earnings calls and permanent-CEO announcements can provide.

Multi-week losing streaks in a single mega-cap stock are rare enough to be genuinely informative about sentiment. They suggest the market has shifted from reflexively buying dips to waiting for a specific catalyst - in this case, very plausibly the November 17 earnings call - before committing fresh capital either way.

## FAQ

### Why did Nvidia's stock keep falling even after the CEO transition was announced?
The initial resignation announcement on November 12 answered one question (who runs the company now) but left several others open, including whether a permanent CEO search would surface new risks, how customers and suppliers would react, and whether further personal allegations against Jensen Huang might surface - which is exactly what happened on Friday with reports of a third affair allegation. Markets often continue to discount a stock as long as material uncertainty remains unresolved, even after the single biggest headline has already been digested.

### Is HSBC's $170 price target unusually low compared to other banks?
It's a sharp cut relative to HSBC's own prior target of $375, and it's lower than many other banks' current targets on Nvidia, which signals HSBC is pricing in a more cautious outlook on governance risk specifically than some of its peers. Price targets across Wall Street can vary widely during periods of active uncertainty like this one, so it's worth checking a range of current analyst targets rather than anchoring on any single bank's number.

### Does falling below $4 trillion in market cap mean Nvidia is now overvalued?
Not on its own. A market-cap threshold is a round number, not a valuation judgment - it only tells you the share price times shares outstanding crossed a level, not whether that level is justified by the company's earnings power. Whether Nvidia is overvalued or undervalued depends on comparing its price to expected future earnings and growth, which is a separate question from where its market cap sits relative to a round number.

### What should investors watch for on November 17?
The earnings call will be Colette Kress's first as interim CEO, and investors are likely to focus as much on her comments about customer relationships, employee retention, and the permanent-CEO search timeline as on the quarter's actual revenue and margin figures. Any sign that the leadership turmoil has started affecting real business metrics - order backlogs, customer commitments, or executive departures - would likely matter more to the stock than the historical numbers themselves.
