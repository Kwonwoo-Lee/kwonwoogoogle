## What Does 'Hedge Fund' Actually Mean When It Shows Up in the News

"Hedge funds are betting on a yen carry trade unwind." "An activist hedge fund has built a stake." "Hedge funds have piled into a large short position on this stock." The phrase shows up constantly in financial headlines, yet it's rarely explained what actually separates a hedge fund from an index fund or an ordinary mutual fund — or why a vehicle named after "hedging" routinely uses tools like short selling and leverage that sound riskier, not safer. This lesson breaks down the structural features that define a hedge fund, then walks through the mechanics behind four of its most common strategy types: long/short equity, event-driven, global macro, and relative value. This isn't a lesson about specific entry or exit signals — it's a structural explanation of how this category of capital actually moves through markets.

## How a Hedge Fund Differs From an Index Fund or Active Fund

The index funds covered in [what is an ETF](/en/basics/etf-basics/) simply replicate a benchmark and aim for the market's average return, while an ordinary active fund tries to beat a specific benchmark (say, the S&P 500). Both are structurally long-only — when the market falls, the fund's return generally falls with it. A hedge fund sets a different goal entirely. Rather than chasing **relative return** against a benchmark, it targets **absolute return** — a positive number regardless of whether the broader market goes up or down. To chase that goal, hedge funds are permitted to use tools that most public funds either can't or rarely do: short selling, derivatives, and leverage from borrowed capital. In exchange, they're regulated differently and sold differently — access is restricted to institutions and wealthy individuals who meet "accredited" or "qualified" investor thresholds, with minimum investments far above what a retail mutual fund requires (Korea's equivalent regulatory category is called a "professional investment private fund," open only to qualified investors who meet specific eligibility and minimum-commitment requirements). The fee structure is different too. Where a public fund typically charges around 1% of assets under management, hedge funds have traditionally charged roughly 2% of assets plus about 20% of any profit generated — the well-known **"2 and 20"** structure. Because a big chunk of the manager's pay only shows up when the fund makes money, this structure also creates an incentive to chase higher, more volatile returns rather than settle for something steady and modest.

## Strategy 1 — Long/Short Equity: Stripping Out Beta to Isolate Alpha

The most widely used hedge fund strategy is **long/short equity**. The logic is simple: buy (go long) stocks judged undervalued, and simultaneously short stocks judged overvalued. Borrowing the concept from [beta and volatility](/en/basics/beta-and-volatility/), a long position makes money when the market rises and loses money when it falls, while a short position does the exact opposite. Size the long and short books roughly equal in dollar terms — what's called a **market-neutral** position — and the market-wide moves in both legs largely cancel out, leaving only the pure effect of stock selection: did the longs outperform the shorts, independent of what the overall market did? That's the process of separating [factor investing's](/en/basics/factor-investing-explained/) **alpha** (excess return from skill) from **beta** (the market's own movement). Not every long/short fund aims for full market neutrality — many run net long, keeping a larger long book and layering in shorts more as insurance against a downturn. A structure with 100% long exposure and 30% short exposure is commonly called a "130/30" fund in the industry — still a variant of the same long/short family.

## Strategy 2 — Event-Driven: Betting on Corporate 'Events' Like Mergers and Restructurings

**Event-driven** strategies target the price impact of a specific corporate event — a merger, a restructuring, a bankruptcy, a spin-off. The best-known subtype is **merger arbitrage**. Once a merger between two public companies is announced, the target company's stock typically trades below the offer price. That gap is the **deal spread**, and it exists because of the months it usually takes for a deal to actually close — shareholder votes, regulatory approval, financing — and because there's a real chance the deal falls apart before any of that happens; the market is pricing exactly that risk into the spread. A merger arbitrage fund buys the target's stock, and — as covered in [merger swap ratios](/en/basics/merger-swap-ratio-explained/) — if the deal is a stock-for-stock exchange, shorts the acquirer's stock too, isolating the position down to just the relative movement between the two companies' shares. If the deal closes as planned, the target's price converges toward the offer price and the spread becomes profit; if the deal breaks, the target's stock usually falls back close to where it traded before the announcement, turning into a loss. The strategy's return, in other words, depends not on whether the market goes up or down, but on whether this one specific transaction actually closes. The activist campaigns covered in [shareholder activism](/en/basics/shareholder-activism-explained/) — where a fund buys a stake and pushes for a spin-off, sale, or bigger dividend — fit under the same broad event-driven umbrella, since the fund is actively trying to engineer the corporate event itself rather than just betting on one that's already announced.

## Strategy 3 — Global Macro: Betting on Country-Level Economic Trends

**Global macro** strategies look past individual companies entirely, analyzing country- and region-level variables — interest rates, currencies, inflation, political shifts — and trading whatever asset class best expresses that view: equities, bonds, currencies, commodities, or derivatives. The macro variables covered in [interest rates and stock valuations](/en/basics/interest-rates-and-stock-valuations/) and [yield curve inversions](/en/basics/yield-curve-inversion/) are exactly the kind of raw material this strategy analyzes. The most famous example is George Soros's Quantum Fund shorting the British pound in 1992. At the time, the UK was bound by the European Exchange Rate Mechanism (ERM) to keep the pound within a set trading band, and Soros judged that the UK's underlying economic conditions made that band unsustainable to defend — so Quantum built a massive short position against the pound. On September 16, 1992 (now remembered as "Black Wednesday"), the UK was forced to exit the ERM and devalue the pound, and Quantum is widely reported to have made roughly $1 billion on the trade, earning Soros the nickname "the man who broke the Bank of England." The borrow-low, invest-high trade covered in [the yen carry trade](/en/basics/yen-carry-trade-explained/) — borrowing in a low-rate currency to invest in higher-yielding assets elsewhere — is a textbook structure that global macro funds favor as well.

## Strategy 4 — Relative Value: Capturing a Thin Gap Between Theoretical and Market Price

**Relative value** strategies hunt for temporary price gaps between two securities that should, in theory, move together. The classic example is convertible bond arbitrage. As covered in [convertible bonds](/en/basics/convertible-bonds-cb-bw/), a convertible bond is a bond bundled with an embedded stock call option — and sometimes the market prices that embedded option below what a pricing model says it's worth. A fund can buy the convertible bond while simultaneously shorting a calculated ratio of the underlying stock, capturing the gap as the two securities' prices eventually converge toward their theoretical relationship. The problem is that this gap is usually tiny — a matter of a few percent — so extracting a meaningful return from it requires heavy leverage, and that leverage is exactly where relative value's biggest vulnerability lives.

| Strategy | What it bets on | Source of return | Key risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Long/short equity | Relative strength between individual stocks | Stock-picking skill (alpha) on both legs | Unlimited loss potential on the short leg |
| Event-driven | A specific corporate event (M&A, restructuring) | The spread tied to whether the event closes | The deal falling through or being delayed |
| Global macro | Country- or region-level macro variables | Correctly calling rate, currency, or commodity direction | Large losses when the macro call is wrong |
| Relative value | Price gap between two linked securities | Convergence arbitrage | Losses amplified by leverage |

## How Leverage Turns Into a Catastrophe — The LTCM Collapse of 1998

The clearest real-world example of how leverage can amplify a small price gap into a disaster is the 1998 collapse of Long-Term Capital Management (LTCM). Founded by a roster of star traders that included two Nobel laureate economists, LTCM ran relative value strategies chasing tiny price discrepancies across bond and swap markets, reportedly running leverage upward of 25 times its equity capital to build positions with a notional value exceeding $1 trillion. When Russia defaulted on its government debt and declared a moratorium in 1998, a global flight to safety swept through bond markets — and the spreads LTCM's models assumed would eventually narrow instead widened sharply in the wrong direction. With leverage running tens of times equity, even a relatively modest adverse move in those spreads was enough to wipe out a large share of the fund's capital within weeks, eventually forcing the Federal Reserve Bank of New York to organize a roughly $3.6 billion bailout consortium among 14 major banks. The case is still cited constantly because each of LTCM's individual positions was, in a narrow sense, theoretically sound. The problem was that "the theory is correct" and "I can survive on leverage long enough for the theory to play out" turned out to be two entirely different propositions — a structural risk that applies to every leveraged strategy, not just relative value.

## Why This Structure Matters Even If You'll Never Invest in a Hedge Fund

Most retail investors will never invest directly in a hedge fund, but understanding this structure still changes how you read market news. A headline reporting that "hedge funds have built up short positions" in a stock might mean the fundamentals genuinely look weak — or it might just mean a long/short fund used that stock as the short leg of a relative-value trade within its sector. A headline about a hedge fund "loading up" on a stock after a merger announcement is more likely a merger-arbitrage spread bet than a conviction call on that company's long-term growth. Once you can tell these apart, you can separate "this trade reflects a genuine judgment about the company's future" from "this trade is the mechanical output of a specific strategy's position construction" — a distinction that changes how much weight that headline deserves.

## Key Takeaways

- A hedge fund targets **absolute return** independent of market direction rather than relative return against a benchmark, using short selling, leverage, and derivatives freely, and typically charges a "2 and 20" fee structure.
- **Long/short equity** holds long and short positions simultaneously to cancel out market-wide movement (beta), leaving only the return from stock selection (alpha).
- **Event-driven** strategies, most notably merger arbitrage, capture a spread tied to whether a specific corporate event closes, carrying real risk if the deal is delayed or falls through.
- **Global macro** bets on country-level variables like rates, currencies, and commodities — Soros's 1992 pound short is the textbook example.
- **Relative value** amplifies a thin price gap between linked securities using leverage, and LTCM's 1998 collapse is the defining case study in what happens when that leverage moves against you.
- Korea's regulatory equivalent is the "professional investment private fund" category, open only to qualified investors who meet eligibility and minimum-investment requirements.

## FAQ

### Do all hedge funds use short selling?
It depends on the strategy. Short selling is structurally central to long/short equity and relative value, but global macro and some event-driven strategies can run net long for extended periods. The word "hedge" doesn't mean every strategy shorts something — it traces back to the original idea of constructing a position that offsets, or hedges, a specific risk.

### Can retail investors access these strategies?
Directly joining a professional investment private fund requires meeting qualified-investor eligibility and a high minimum commitment, which limits direct access for most retail investors. Some publicly offered multi-strategy products, REITs, or alternative-investment vehicles offer indirect, partial exposure to similar strategies, though these differ meaningfully from the hedge funds described here.

### Does a large hedge fund always mean systemic risk for the market?
Not necessarily. The danger LTCM illustrated comes from excessive leverage combined with many funds crowding into similar positions, so that a single triggering event cascades into widespread losses — not from the mere existence of hedge fund strategies. That said, regulators do continuously monitor leverage levels and position concentration across the industry precisely because of that risk.
