## What Happened

Netflix (Nasdaq: NFLX) reported third-quarter 2026 results after the closing bell on Tuesday, October 20. On paper, it was a clean beat. Revenue came in at $12.87 billion, up 12% year-over-year and landing almost exactly on the $12.86 billion the company itself had guided to back in July. Earnings per share were the bigger standout: $0.87, topping the $0.82 Wall Street consensus and up 49% from $0.59 a year earlier. Net income rose 44% year-over-year to $3.66 billion, and operating margin expanded to 34.6% from 28.2% in the same quarter last year - a 6.4-percentage-point improvement. Revenue growth, an EPS beat, and expanding margins: on the surface, this was about as clean a quarter as a company can report.

And yet the stock fell more than 5% in the session that followed. A company growing revenue, beating on profit, and expanding margin saw its shares sell off anyway - a reaction that looks contradictory until you look past the headline numbers. The timing made the drop sting more than it otherwise might have. Going into the print, Netflix shares had already slid from a 52-week high near $125 earlier this year down into the $65-$70 range, sitting near a 52-week low. This also marked the third time in 2026 that Netflix delivered a quarter with respectable headline numbers only to watch the stock drop anyway, following similar patterns after both its Q1 and Q2 reports this year.

One more detail from the week leading into earnings complicated the picture further. Roughly a week before reporting, Netflix announced it was cutting about 1,000 jobs - roughly 6% of its workforce, the deepest round of layoffs the company has made since 2022. At the time, the move raised questions about why Netflix, a company with growing revenue and improving margins, needed to cut staff so aggressively. Once the earnings report showed that same operating margin jumping 6.4 percentage points year-over-year, the two events got tied together in investors' minds - not as cause and effect exactly, but as a reason to question how much of that margin gain reflects durable efficiency versus a one-time cost cut.

## Why the Numbers Looked Good But the Stock Still Fell

Two specific items in the report drove the sell-off, and both point forward rather than backward. The first is Netflix's advertising business. The company had spent most of 2026 reaffirming a roughly $3 billion full-year ad revenue target. In this report, it cut that target to $2.7 billion - a roughly 10% reduction - and attributed the shortfall to its ad-tech product rollout moving slower than planned. That is a meaningful signal, because advertising has been the growth story investors have been asked to believe in as subscription price increases slow: it's supposed to be the engine that drives both revenue growth and margin expansion going forward. Cutting the target doesn't just lower a number for this year - it discounts the credibility of the broader pitch that advertising will carry Netflix's next leg of growth.

The second is fourth-quarter guidance. Netflix guided to Q4 revenue of roughly $13.4 billion, which still represents 12% year-over-year growth but came in below the roughly $13.5 billion Wall Street had modeled. Full-year revenue guidance ticked up slightly to about $51.1 billion, but the quarter that actually matters most to investors right now - the next one - came in light. Combined, that meant the backward-looking numbers (revenue, EPS, margin) simply couldn't carry the stock, because none of them are what the market was actually pricing.

The layoffs add a layer of interpretation on top of that. A 6.4-point jump in operating margin is, on its face, a clearly positive number. But sitting a week after a 6% workforce reduction, it raises an unavoidable question: how much of that margin gain came from running the business more efficiently, and how much came from cutting costs that will eventually need to be replaced - in content spending, product development, or both? If margin improvement is substituting for investment rather than reflecting genuine efficiency, that tends to show up later as weaker content competitiveness, not as a one-time gain investors can simply bank.

Context matters here too. This is now the third consecutive quarter in which Netflix's reported numbers and the stock's reaction have diverged. Q1 results were inflated by a one-time $2.8 billion termination fee tied to Netflix's abandoned bid for Warner Bros. Discovery, masking weaker underlying profitability. Q2 results came with Q3 guidance that fell short of expectations, sending shares down 9%-12% the next session. Investors walked into this report already primed to look past the headline and hunt for the catch - and when the ad revenue cut surfaced, they didn't wait around. With Wall Street's average price target still sitting near $90, well above the current stock price, the next question is whether analysts follow this report with another round of target cuts, as they did after both previous disappointments this year.

## What to Take Away From This

- **Check the quality of forward guidance before reacting to a headline beat.** Netflix beat on both revenue and EPS, but the stock moved on the ad revenue target cut and soft Q4 guidance - numbers about what's coming, not what already happened. A beat on trailing results means little if the forward outlook disappoints.
- **Separate where margin improvement actually comes from.** A 6.4-percentage-point jump in operating margin sounds unambiguously good, but whether it comes from genuine efficiency gains or from a round of layoffs made days earlier changes how durable that improvement really is.
- **Treat a pre-earnings layoff announcement as a piece of context, not a standalone event.** News that lands in the run-up to an earnings report rarely stands alone - it's worth holding onto until the earnings report itself gives you the fuller picture.
- **A repeating pattern changes how the market reacts the next time around.** This is Netflix's third 2026 quarter where solid headline numbers failed to support the stock. A single disappointing reaction can be explained away; a third one in a row starts getting priced in as a standing discount on the stock, independent of any single quarter's results.

## FAQ

### Why did Netflix cut its advertising revenue target from $3 billion to $2.7 billion?

The company said its ad-tech product rollout - the technical infrastructure needed to sell and serve ads at scale - is moving slower than originally planned. Advertiser demand itself hasn't necessarily weakened; the bottleneck is on Netflix's own build-out timeline, which pushed the achievable revenue for this year down by roughly 10%.

### Is there a confirmed link between the ~1,000 job cuts and this quarter's margin improvement?

Netflix hasn't officially tied the two together, but the timing - layoffs announced about a week before an earnings report showing a 6.4-percentage-point margin jump - is close enough that the market is drawing its own connection. Whether the margin gain holds up as durable or fades once the cost base normalizes should become clearer over the next couple of quarters.

### When does Netflix report next, and what should investors watch for?

Netflix typically reports roughly three weeks after each quarter closes, which would put fourth-quarter 2026 results around mid-to-late January 2027. The key thing to track between now and then is whether the company's roughly $13.4 billion Q4 revenue guidance holds up, and whether the advertising business shows signs of catching up to its reduced $2.7 billion target.
