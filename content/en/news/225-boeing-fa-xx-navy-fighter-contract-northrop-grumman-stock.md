## What Happened

On Tuesday, September 29, the Pentagon announced that Boeing (NYSE: BA) had won the U.S. Navy's next-generation carrier-based fighter program, F/A-XX, beating out rival Northrop Grumman (NYSE: NOC) for a full-scale development contract worth more than $20 billion. Boeing shares jumped roughly 2.5% in after-hours trading on the news and carried that momentum into the following session, closing up around 2%. Northrop Grumman moved the opposite way - down about 5% after-hours, then tumbling 4% in pre-market trading the next day to roughly $482.94.

F/A-XX is the sixth-generation fighter program set to replace the Navy's current carrier-based workhorses - the F/A-18E/F Super Hornet and the EA-18G Growler electronic-warfare jet - starting sometime in the 2030s. This particular award covers the full-scale development phase, which includes building multiple test aircraft for ground testing, airworthiness evaluation, systems integration, and weapons integration. It does not cover actual series production, and the Navy has not yet disclosed how many F/A-XX aircraft it ultimately intends to buy. On the competitive side, Lockheed Martin was eliminated from the running back in March 2025, narrowing the contest to a two-way fight between Boeing and Northrop Grumman that Boeing has now won.

## Why Boeing Won - The Mechanism Behind the Decision

The key to understanding this outcome is that Boeing already holds the Air Force's equivalent sixth-generation program, the F-47, which it won over Lockheed Martin back in 2025 as part of the Next Generation Air Dominance (NGAD) initiative. With this F/A-XX win, Boeing now holds both of the U.S. military's manned sixth-generation fighter programs outright. Much of the market had actually priced in this possibility ahead of time. BNP Paribas aerospace and defense analyst Matthew Akers noted that Boeing's F-47 track record made it the favored candidate for F/A-XX as well - a pattern that reflects a real incentive for the Pentagon to concentrate related programs with a contractor that has already demonstrated a compatible design philosophy and production system, which can reduce both development risk and cost.

The Navy also emphasized that the new aircraft will be built around an "open mission systems architecture," designed to let sensors, weapons, and software be swapped and upgraded far more flexibly than on legacy fighters. This directly addresses a long-standing problem in fighter design, where hardware locked in at the outset of a program often became difficult to meaningfully upgrade for decades afterward; the open-architecture approach is meant to keep the jet's internal technology current throughout its operational life. That said, some market observers had actually expected the opposite outcome - that the Pentagon would deliberately split its two sixth-generation programs between different contractors to diversify supply-chain risk. The fact that both ended up concentrated at Boeing is part of what made this result notable rather than a foregone conclusion.

## What It Means for Northrop Grumman - Shut Out of Both Programs

With this loss, Northrop Grumman has now missed out on both of the U.S. military's manned sixth-generation fighter competitions - the Air Force's F-47 and now the Navy's F/A-XX. That's a real setback to the company's broader strategy of securing next-generation fighter production capacity, and it means Northrop's growth narrative going forward will lean more heavily on the B-21 Raider stealth bomber, the Sentinel intercontinental ballistic missile modernization program, and its satellite and space businesses. All three remain meaningful long-term revenue sources, but none carries quite the same large-scale, decades-long production cadence that a fighter program typically provides. Wall Street's reaction reflected this split clearly. On Boeing, JPMorgan reiterated an Overweight rating with a $290 price target. Susquehanna's Charles Minervino kept a Buy rating and $285 target. UBS also maintained a Buy rating with a $275 target, arguing explicitly that Boeing's recent stock weakness had "gone too far." RBC Capital Markets went further still, calling the award "a near-complete reversal of Boeing's defense trajectory." Morgan Stanley, by contrast, stayed comparatively cautious with an Equal-weight rating and a $250 price target - a reminder that even among bulls on this news, there's real disagreement about how much the win actually changes.

## The Context Behind Boeing's Rebound - A Sharp Contrast to the 737 MAX 10 Setback Days Earlier

What makes this win especially striking is the timing. Just two days earlier, on September 28, Boeing shares had fallen roughly 7% in a single session after the FAA paused certification of the 737 MAX 10 over a software issue tied to automated landing guidance (see our September 29 coverage). That episode reignited long-standing concerns about Boeing's recurring quality and certification problems. Then, within 24 to 48 hours, an entirely unrelated piece of good news from a completely different business segment drove shares right back up. This is a clean illustration of how a large, diversified industrial company's stock doesn't move on any single business unit's news in isolation. Boeing effectively operates on two separate pillars - commercial aircraft (the 737 MAX family and beyond) and defense programs (F-47, F/A-XX, and others) - and a serious setback in one pillar can be offset, or even outweighed, by a major catalyst in the other. Analysts were careful to note, however, that this single defense win doesn't by itself resolve Boeing's broader financial picture. The variables that will actually determine Boeing's medium-term trajectory remain commercial production normalization and free cash flow recovery; the F/A-XX contract is best understood as a positive catalyst layered on top of that larger story, not a replacement for it.

## What to Take Away From This

- **A diversified company's stock price reflects multiple business segments at once.** For a conglomerate like Boeing that straddles commercial aviation and defense, bad news in one segment can be offset or even reversed by good news in another. Avoid drawing conclusions about the whole stock from a single headline.
- **Check the spread of analyst price targets right after a major contract win.** A gap from JPMorgan's $290 down to Morgan Stanley's $250 on the very same news tells you the market hasn't fully converged on how durable or significant the catalyst really is.
- **Look at the loser's reaction too, not just the winner's.** A company like Northrop Grumman that loses a major competitive bid can effectively see that growth avenue close off, so it's worth checking how solid its remaining portfolio (B-21, Sentinel, and similar programs) really is.
- **"Expected" good news can still move a stock meaningfully.** Much of the market had already priced in Boeing as the likely F/A-XX winner, yet shares still reacted notably on the official announcement - a reminder that formal confirmation of an anticipated outcome can still carry real information value.

## FAQ

### When will the F/A-XX fighter actually enter service?
This contract covers the full-scale development phase, including building prototype aircraft for ground, systems, and weapons integration testing. The Navy has said it plans to begin replacing the F/A-18E/F Super Hornet and EA-18G Growler sometime in the 2030s, but a specific deployment date and total production quantity haven't been finalized publicly yet.

### Does this contract immediately fix Boeing's financial situation?
Not in the short term. This is a development-phase contract, and meaningful production revenue is still years away. Analysts broadly agree that Boeing's real path to financial recovery runs through normalizing commercial aircraft production (737 MAX and others) and improving free cash flow - this contract is viewed as a positive supporting factor rather than the main driver.

### Will Northrop Grumman stock stay weak going forward?
The fighter-jet growth story has clearly taken a hit, but Northrop still holds a sizable defense portfolio, including the B-21 Raider stealth bomber, the Sentinel ICBM modernization program, and its space and satellite businesses. Sentiment may stay soft near-term, but the stock's longer-run trajectory will likely hinge on order flow and revenue trends across those other segments.
