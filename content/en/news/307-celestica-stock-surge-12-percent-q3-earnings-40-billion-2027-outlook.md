## What Happened

After the close on Monday, October 26, Canadian electronics manufacturing services (EMS) company Celestica (NYSE/TSX: CLS) reported third-quarter 2026 results that blew past expectations on nearly every line. Revenue came in at $5.83 billion, up 83% year over year and above the high end of the company's own guidance range of $5.3-$5.6 billion. Adjusted EPS hit $3.30, more than double the $1.58 posted a year earlier and comfortably ahead of the $2.99 Wall Street consensus. Adjusted operating margin rose to 8.5% from 7.6% a year ago.

That alone would have been a strong quarter. But the bigger story was guidance. Celestica raised its full-year 2026 outlook to $21.0 billion in revenue and $11.55 in adjusted EPS - growth of 69% and 91%, respectively, over 2025. More striking still was a brand-new disclosure: first-ever 2027 guidance calling for roughly $40 billion in revenue and an operating margin near 9%, implying close to 90% year-over-year growth. The market's reaction was immediate. Shares opened sharply higher in Tuesday's premarket session, and by the close on October 27 the stock had jumped from Monday's $246.65 close to $276.54 - a gain of roughly 12.1% in a single session, with an intraday high touching $283.

## Why "Rebound" Is the Right Word - What Happened in October First

To understand why this move mattered as much as it did, you have to look at where Celestica's stock stood going into earnings. The company had spent most of 2026 as one of the stronger beneficiaries of AI data center buildout spending, but October told a very different story. Around October 13, the stock got caught up in a broad, sector-wide selloff in AI-exposed names. By October 16, it had logged a 16.1% decline over the prior week alone. The slide continued from there: shares fell another 11.7% in a single session on October 22, dropping to $269.01, and kept sliding into the earnings print itself, closing at $246.65 on October 26 - the day results came out after the bell.

The character of that selloff matters here. This wasn't a decline driven by company-specific bad news - a profit warning, a lost customer, an accounting issue. It was a sector-wide repricing driven by valuation anxiety around AI-related names broadly, some profit-taking after a long run higher, and investors sitting on their hands ahead of Celestica's own earnings and investor day. In other words, the stock fell because the market was pricing in fear about what AI capex might look like going forward, not because the underlying business had actually deteriorated. That distinction explains why the reversal was so sharp: when a stock drops on sentiment rather than fundamentals, a clean beat-and-raise quarter has nothing standing in its way, and short covering plus a scramble to rebuild positions can produce an outsized bounce in a single session - which is exactly what happened here.

## What Celestica Actually Does, and What's Behind the $40 Billion Number

Understanding this story requires understanding Celestica's business, which has transformed considerably over the past several years. The company got its start making PCs and telecom gear on contract, but today roughly 80% of its revenue comes from its Connectivity & Cloud Solutions (CCS) segment - and the core product there is high-speed networking switches sold to hyperscalers (the handful of giant cloud and AI companies building out data centers). The current workhorse is the 800G (800 gigabits per second) Ethernet switch, with mass production of next-generation 1.6T switches now approaching. On top of that, Celestica has won a co-packaged optics Ethernet switch program with a hyperscale customer and a scale-up switch program with AMD, both of which management says will ramp meaningfully starting in 2027.

That context matters because it means the $40 billion 2027 target isn't a vague aspiration - management is framing it as a number grounded in already-won programs with known production ramps, not hoped-for future orders. To support that growth, the company is guiding to roughly $1 billion of capital expenditure in 2026, potentially rising to as much as $1.5 billion in 2027. That capacity investment has to happen ahead of the revenue it's meant to capture, which is a pattern showing up across the AI data center supply chain right now, echoing similar capex buildouts from chip equipment makers and the broader financing structures backing AI infrastructure spending.

That said, the quarter also surfaced risks worth flagging. Celestica's top three customers account for 66% of total revenue - a concentration level that means a shift in ordering patterns from just one or two hyperscalers could move results materially. And the rapid growth has a cash cost: rising inventory and working capital needed to support that expansion caused free cash flow to decline even as revenue and earnings surged - a familiar tradeoff for fast-growing hardware manufacturers scaling to meet demand.

## What to Take Away From This

- **Distinguish sector-wide selling from company-specific deterioration.** Celestica's October decline wasn't evidence the business was getting worse - it reflected broader anxiety about AI spending. That kind of "sector beta" selloff tends to reverse fastest once hard evidence, like an earnings report, arrives. Before assuming a falling stock signals a real problem, check whether the move tracks the whole sector or is specific to that company.
- **A multi-year guide can move a stock more than the quarter itself.** This reversal wasn't really about the Q3 numbers - it was about the first-ever 2027 target. For capital-intensive hardware and manufacturing businesses in particular, a long-term guide tied to a visible order backlog can unwind an uncertainty discount in a single session.
- **Check the quality of growth - customer concentration and cash flow matter.** Behind the 83% revenue growth headline sits a less flattering detail: three customers make up 66% of sales, and free cash flow fell as inventory built up. A flashy growth rate paired with concentration risk and deteriorating cash conversion deserves a second look, not just a celebration of the top line.
- **In the AI supply chain, track who's actually capturing the economics.** Contract manufacturers like Celestica sit downstream of the chip designers (Nvidia, AMD) that get most of the attention - they assemble and build, rather than design. Checking whether a company's growth is backed by confirmed, named programs rather than vague AI-boom optimism is a useful filter for separating real demand from sentiment across AI-adjacent stocks generally.

## FAQ

### Did Celestica's 12% jump erase its October losses?

Not entirely. Celestica had fallen more than 16% over one week in mid-October and dropped another 11.7% in a single session on October 22, so the cumulative decline from its October highs was considerably steeper than the 12% it recovered on October 27. The bounce reversed a meaningful chunk of that damage but didn't fully restore the stock to its early-October levels. It's more accurate to call this the start of a recovery than a complete round trip.

### How is Celestica different from a chip company like Nvidia or AMD?

Celestica doesn't design or fabricate semiconductors itself. It's a contract manufacturer that takes chips designed by companies like Nvidia and AMD, along with networking components from other suppliers, and assembles them into finished switches and server systems that hyperscalers can deploy directly in their data centers. That puts it in the manufacturing and assembly layer of the AI supply chain rather than the design layer, which means its margin structure and valuation logic differ meaningfully from pure chip designers.

### Why is customer concentration flagged as a risk for a company like Celestica?

With its top three customers accounting for 66% of revenue, any single customer scaling back orders or shifting business to a competing supplier could move Celestica's results significantly. Hyperscalers are known to adjust their own data center capex plans based on changing conditions, so a supplier this reliant on a handful of large customers carries structural exposure to those customers' own spending decisions.
