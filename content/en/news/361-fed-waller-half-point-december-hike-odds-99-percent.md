## What Happened

On Tuesday, November 10, Federal Reserve Governor Christopher Waller told reporters he's leaning toward backing a standard quarter-point interest rate hike at the Fed's December 8-9 meeting - but then went a step further than almost any sitting Fed governor has gone this cycle. Waller explicitly put a larger, half-point increase on the table, conditioned on the data: if core CPI, which strips out food and energy, comes in at 0.3% month-over-month or higher for a third consecutive month, he said a 50-basis-point hike "should be considered." He framed the stakes bluntly: "Hiking only 25 basis points would put our credibility at risk, a risk I am not eager to take."

Waller wasn't alone that day. Kansas City Fed President Jeffrey Schmid, a well-known hawk, said the Fed's benchmark rate is "fairly accommodative" and argued tighter policy is still needed given inflation running persistently above target. Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan added hawkish commentary in the same session, rounding out a three-person chorus that moved markets within hours.

The reaction was immediate. According to CME Group's FedWatch tool, the probability of some size of rate hike at the December meeting jumped to roughly 99% in the wake of the comments - up sharply from where odds stood just days earlier, when fed funds futures implied closer to an 85% chance of a move. Within that near-certainty, the market now assigns a small but real probability - under 10% - specifically to the larger half-point move Waller described, with the rest concentrated on a conventional quarter-point increase.

The timing is what makes this notable. At the Fed's previous meeting on October 28, the committee voted 10-2 to hold its benchmark rate steady in the 3.75%-4.00% range. The two dissenters, Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack, wanted to hike then rather than wait. Waller, by most accounts, sat in the majority that chose to hold in October. His shift toward actively floating a bigger move barely two weeks later is a signal that the committee's center of gravity - not just its most hawkish fringe - has moved.

## Why a Few Basis Points of Difference Matters This Much

It's worth asking why markets reacted so sharply to a comment about hike *size*, when a December hike itself was already close to priced in following the hot October CPI report (see our earlier coverage: [October CPI Comes In Hot, December Fed Hike Odds Jump to 90%](/en/news/october-cpi-hot-fed-hike-odds-90-percent-nvidia-selloff/)). The mechanism here is about rate-*path* uncertainty, not just rate-level certainty. A market that's 90% confident the Fed hikes a standard quarter-point can price that into bond yields and equity valuations with relative precision - the math of discounting future cash flows barely changes at the margin. A market facing even a one-in-ten chance of a half-point move has to price in a fatter tail risk: the chance that borrowing costs rise twice as fast as the base case, which compresses the present value of future corporate earnings by more than most models currently assume, particularly for longer-duration growth stocks whose valuations lean heavily on cash flows many years out.

This lands at a particularly sensitive moment for equities. The market has just closed its first winning week since early October (see [S&P 500 Closes Down 0.4% Friday, Yet Logs First Winning Week Since Early October](/en/news/sp500-weekly-gain-nvidia-13-percent-week-fed-watch/)), even as mega-cap tech names, Nvidia chief among them, have been sliding for six straight weeks. Nvidia itself reports earnings this Tuesday, November 17 (see our [earnings preview](/en/news/nvidia-earnings-november-17-options-market-9-point-6-percent-move-colette-kress-first-call/)), with options markets already pricing a 9.6% implied move on the print alone. Layer Waller's half-point threat on top of that setup and you get two separate sources of tail risk compounding in the same week: a company-specific earnings-and-leadership risk, and a macro rate-path risk, both resolving within days of each other.

There's also a credibility dimension worth flagging. Waller explicitly invoked the word "credibility" - a loaded term for a central banker to use in public. When a Fed official worries aloud about credibility, they're usually signaling a belief that markets might otherwise doubt the committee's resolve to bring inflation down, which could in turn let inflation expectations drift upward and become self-fulfilling. A hawkish stance adopted partly to defend institutional credibility tends to be sticky and harder to walk back than a purely data-dependent forecast would be, precisely because reversing it risks spending the same credibility it was meant to protect.

## What to Take Away From This

- **Rate-hike odds and rate-hike-*size* odds are two different numbers, and both move markets.** A binary "will they hike" probability near 99% can coexist with real uncertainty about magnitude. Fed funds futures increasingly price a distribution of outcomes, not a single one - treating a near-certain hike as fully "priced in" ignores the size question entirely, and that question is where a lot of the remaining risk sits.
- **When several Fed officials shift tone in the same news cycle, treat it as a signal, not noise.** One hawkish regional president making headlines is background chatter. Three officials - including a sitting governor who votes on every decision - using similar language within hours of each other is a far more coordinated signal than any single data point, and tends to be a better predictor of the eventual vote than polling individual past statements.
- **Watch specifically for language about "credibility."** When a central banker frames a policy choice in terms of institutional credibility rather than pure data analysis, it often means they're trying to anchor market expectations ahead of time. That framing is itself informative: it suggests the eventual decision may lean more hawkish than a simple read of the most recent data would otherwise imply.
- **Compounding risk events landing in the same week deserve extra caution, not extra conviction.** With Nvidia's earnings, the Fed's rate-path uncertainty, and several other catalysts arriving within days of one another, position sizing into any single one of them should account for the fact that these risks can overlap and amplify each other rather than simply canceling out.

## FAQ

### What's the difference between a 25 basis point and a 50 basis point hike?
A basis point is one-hundredth of a percentage point, so 25 basis points equals 0.25% and 50 basis points equals 0.50%. A quarter-point move is the Fed's standard-sized adjustment; a half-point hike is twice as large and historically signals the committee sees a more urgent need to cool the economy than a standard-sized move would address on its own.

### When is the Fed's December meeting, and when will markets know the outcome?
The Federal Open Market Committee meets December 8-9, 2026. The rate decision and the Fed Chair's press conference are typically released on the second day of the meeting, in the afternoon Eastern time.

### Does a near-99% hike probability mean the decision is already final?
No. Tools like CME's FedWatch reflect market-implied probabilities derived from current futures pricing, not a guarantee of the outcome. Those odds can and do shift quickly with new economic data - including the next jobs report or CPI print - before the committee actually meets and votes.
