## What Happened

Oil prices jumped across the board on Thursday, August 6. West Texas Intermediate crude futures rose 2.8% to settle at $77.29 a barrel, while international benchmark Brent crude gained 3.8% to close at $82.48. The reversal was sharp and sudden, triggered by a draft document Iran's state news agency Fars published outlining new conditions for shipping traffic through the Strait of Hormuz.

According to Fars, the draft is currently under review by an Iranian parliamentary committee, and its contents ran directly counter to what markets had been pricing in. The plan would ban US and Israeli vessels from transiting the strait entirely, bar ships from any nation deemed to have "harmed Iran" until compensation is paid, and impose penalties equal to 20% of a cargo's value on violators. For a chokepoint that carries a substantial share of the world's seaborne crude, that reads less like a negotiating position and more like a blueprint for a more restrictive, not more open, Hormuz.

What makes the timing notable is how abruptly it reversed the prevailing narrative. Just two days earlier, on August 4, US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent told CNBC that a deal to reopen Hormuz with free navigation could arrive "as soon as Wednesday" (August 5). That comment helped drive oil prices down roughly 8% over the course of the week. It also fed directly into South Korea's stock market: the Kospi jumped 3.85% on August 5 to reclaim the 6,600 level, with chip heavyweights SK Hynix and Samsung Electronics rallying on hopes that a resolved Hormuz standoff would ease pressure on risk assets broadly. Iran's draft plan undid a meaningful chunk of that optimism in a single session, sending oil back up and erasing part of the week's earlier decline.

US equities felt the pressure too. The Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 464.02 points, or 0.85%, to close near 53,885, snapping a six-session winning streak that had carried the index above 54,000 for the first time ever just two days earlier, on August 4. The S&P 500 slipped 0.18%, and the Nasdaq Composite edged down 0.06%. The 10-year Treasury yield rose 0.07 percentage points to 4.68%, a sign that bond markets were also pricing in some inflationary pressure from the oil move. Among individual names, Salesforce fell about 3% on news of a leadership shuffle, accounting for a meaningful share of the Dow's point decline on its own.

## Why One Draft Document Moved Both Oil and Stocks

To understand why a single unconfirmed document could swing both commodity and equity markets, it helps to trace the narrative arc of the past week. Growing optimism about a US-Iran resolution over Hormuz had been steadily stripping what traders call a "geopolitical risk premium" out of oil prices - the extra cushion baked into crude when supply through a critical chokepoint looks uncertain. Bessent's comment on Tuesday accelerated that process, and by Thursday morning oil was down nearly 8% for the week. Kospi's 3.85% single-day surge reflected the same logic in reverse: if a resolved Hormuz standoff removes a major supply-disruption risk, that's good news not just for shipping- and fuel-cost-sensitive sectors like airlines, but for risk assets broadly, semiconductors included.

Iran's draft plan flipped that script. Rather than moving toward an open, freely navigable strait, the document codified a more restrictive framework - one that singles out specific nations' vessels for exclusion and backs it with financial penalties. Markets treated this as a signal that a deal, while not necessarily dead, is considerably less likely to arrive on the timeline investors had started pricing in. It's worth noting the draft is still just that - a draft, under committee review, not enacted policy - but commodity markets tend to react to the direction of risk rather than wait for confirmation. That asymmetry is a defining feature of how oil trades around geopolitical chokepoints: supply-disruption fears get priced in well before any actual disruption occurs, and the risk premium rarely fully unwinds until there's real confidence the threat has passed - not just hope that it might.

The Dow's pullback fits the same mechanism, one step removed. Rising oil raises fuel costs for airlines and shippers and feeds into broader inflation expectations, which in turn complicates the Federal Reserve's path toward further rate cuts. The 10-year Treasury yield ticking up alongside the oil move is consistent with bond markets pricing in at least some of that inflationary pressure. That said, the magnitude of Thursday's Dow decline - 0.85% - was modest relative to the record-setting run that preceded it, suggesting markets are treating this as a near-term risk reassessment rather than a full trend reversal for now. It's also worth flagging that Salesforce's leadership-shuffle-driven decline was a company-specific factor layered on top of the macro story, meaning the Dow's drop wasn't purely a geopolitical-risk read-through.

## What to Take Away From This

- **Geopolitical risk can swing between "deal imminent" and "deal at risk" in days, not weeks.** Oil fell nearly 8% on deal optimism, then reversed sharply on a single draft document. When a rally or selloff is built on an unresolved negotiation, weigh how fragile the underlying assumption actually is before treating the move as durable.
- **Draft and preliminary documents move markets just as much as final agreements.** Iran's plan hadn't cleared parliamentary review, yet oil and equities reacted immediately. For commodity and geopolitical stories, the question that matters for near-term price action is often "has the direction of risk changed?" rather than "has anything been finalized?"
- **Oil, bond yields, and equities are linked through a single transmission mechanism.** This episode traced a clean path from rising crude to inflation concern to higher Treasury yields to equity pressure - a reminder that commodity moves ripple well beyond the energy sector into broad market valuations.
- **Don't attribute an index move to a single headline cause without checking the components.** Some of the Dow's decline traced back to Salesforce's company-specific leadership news, not just the macro oil story. When a major index moves, breaking down which constituents actually drove it helps avoid drawing the wrong conclusion from the headline number alone.
