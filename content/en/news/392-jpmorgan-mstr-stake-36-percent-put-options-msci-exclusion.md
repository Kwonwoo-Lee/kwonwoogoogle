## What Happened

A 13F filing JPMorgan Chase disclosed in mid-November, covering its holdings as of the September 30 quarter-end, contained a number that made headlines on its own: the bank's stake in Bitcoin treasury company Strategy (Nasdaq: MSTR) had risen to 848,932 shares, up 36% from 624,387 shares at the end of the second quarter. Valued at the September 30 closing price, that position was worth roughly $131.3 million. The increase looked even larger in dollar terms once you factor in that Strategy's share price itself had jumped from $92.68 to $154.67 over the same quarter. On the surface, this read like a straightforwardly bullish headline: a major investment bank adding to its position in the bellwether Bitcoin treasury stock.

But the same filing contained another figure that complicates that story considerably. JPMorgan also disclosed put options covering 1,148,500 shares of MSTR. Put options are derivatives that gain value when a stock falls - essentially a form of downside insurance. The key detail is that this put position was larger than JPMorgan's actual long holding of 848,932 shares. In other words, while the "36% increase" headline implied the bank was doubling down bullishly on Strategy, the full picture of its position suggests greater exposure to the downside than to the upside. A 13F filing shows share counts and the notional size of option positions, but not strike prices or expiration dates, so it's impossible to say from the filing alone exactly how conservative or aggressive that hedge really was. Directionally, though, this was a long way from a simple "net buy."

Not long after that filing became public, a much bigger catalyst hit the stock. On November 11, index provider MSCI announced its November index review and confirmed that Strategy would be removed from the MSCI USA Index. Because the MSCI USA Index feeds into broader global benchmarks, that removal automatically pulls the stock out of the MSCI World and MSCI ACWI indexes as well. The basis for the decision was a new "non-operating company" classification rule MSCI adopted on October 15: companies that hold the bulk of their assets in things like Bitcoin, without substantial operating assets to back them, no longer qualify for inclusion in MSCI's global indexes. The same review also flagged other Bitcoin treasury companies for removal, including Japan's Metaplanet and the US-listed Upexi - underscoring that this wasn't a one-off decision about Strategy specifically, but a rule change targeting the entire "public company as Bitcoin balance sheet" business model. The removal is scheduled to take effect at the close of trading on November 30.

Strategy's stock reacted immediately to the confirmation. Shares slipped 2.6% to $119.06 in after-hours trading the day the news broke, then fell a further 6.49% to close at $114.41 the next regular session, with a broader crypto market selloff compounding the move. The decline didn't stop there. By November 19, Strategy had fallen for six consecutive sessions, closing at $86.13 - down roughly 29% from the $121.07 close just before MSCI's confirmation on November 10. In a little over a month, the stock had shed close to a third of its value.

Running that price path through JPMorgan's disclosed position tells its own story. The long stake, valued at roughly $131.3 million on September 30, would have been worth only about $97.1 million using the November 12 closing price of $114.41 - a mark-to-market decline of roughly 26% in about six weeks. Using the lower November 19 close of $86.13, the paper loss would have been larger still. It's worth stressing that a 13F is only a quarter-end snapshot; whether JPMorgan has since trimmed the position or adjusted its hedges won't be visible until the next 13F is filed, expected around mid-February 2027.

## Why This Matters: The 13F Trap and the Mechanics of an Index Exit

Understanding this case starts with a structural limitation of 13F filings themselves: they're disclosed with a 45-day lag after quarter-end. That means the "JPMorgan raised its stake 36%" story that surfaced in mid-November was actually describing a snapshot from six weeks earlier. In the time between that snapshot and its public disclosure, the stock had fallen from the $150s to the $80s, and MSCI's exclusion had become a confirmed, dated event. Reading a stale "increased position" headline and treating it as current information is a trap that applies well beyond this one stock.

The second point worth internalizing is that what looks like a bullish accumulation can, in fact, be a hedge. Holding more shares and holding even more puts against those shares are not contradictory positions - banks frequently build stock-plus-options combinations to support client structured products, convertible-bond-related trades, or their own trading desks' arbitrage books. Collapsing a disclosure down to a single number like "stake up 36%" risks badly mischaracterizing what the position actually represents. That gap between headline and filing detail matters most precisely when institutional positioning gets cited as a bullish or bearish signal.

Third, it's important to separate mechanical, rules-driven selling from fundamentals-driven selling. When an MSCI exclusion takes effect, passive index funds and ETFs tracking MSCI USA, World, or ACWI must sell the affected stock by the effective date regardless of their own view on the company - the selling is a function of index rules, not analysis. JPMorgan's initial estimate put this forced selling at roughly $2.8 billion, with a larger conditional scenario of up to $8.8 billion if other major index providers, such as FTSE Russell or S&P Dow Jones Indices, adopted similar rules. Later analysis scaled that estimate down considerably, to roughly $1 billion, or about 13 million shares - around 3.1% of shares outstanding, and less than a single day's average trading volume for MSTR, a framing that casts the impact as manageable rather than disruptive. The wide range between these estimates mostly comes down to differing assumptions about which indexes are affected and whether other providers follow MSCI's lead.

Fourth, the MSCI exclusion does not force Strategy itself to sell any Bitcoin. Passive fund selling simply moves shares between investors; it carries no mechanism requiring the company to liquidate its treasury holdings. The more fundamental risk lies elsewhere: Strategy's Bitcoin-buying strategy has relied on issuing new shares at a premium to the value of its Bitcoin holdings (an "at-the-market" program) and using the proceeds to buy more Bitcoin. If sustained index-driven selling pressure compresses or eliminates that premium, the financing engine behind the entire strategy weakens - a far bigger structural concern than any one-time forced-selling event.

Through all of this, the company's public stance hasn't shifted. Founder Michael Saylor pushed back publicly on the MSCI threat, saying his "conviction in Bitcoin is unwavering." Bitcoin's own price action, however, has worked against Strategy during this same window. In early November, Bitcoin fell below Strategy's average purchase price of roughly $75,451 per coin, and by mid-November it had slid into the low-$60,000s. That means the company's roughly 850,000 BTC treasury sat in unrealized-loss territory in aggregate - the first time that's happened since the company began aggressively scaling its Bitcoin accumulation strategy.

## What to Take Away From This

A 13F filing is a snapshot of the past, not a picture of the present. Because of the 45-day disclosure lag, the "increased" or "decreased" headline you read on the day it's published may already be describing a market environment that has since changed substantially. Always check the filing's reference date, not its publication date.

A headline about rising share counts and an options position need to be read together to get the full picture. Owning more shares while simultaneously holding an even larger put position is entirely possible, and in that case the real directional exposure can differ sharply from what the headline implies. When a news story cites institutional positioning, look for whether it also discloses the options side of that position.

Forced selling from an index exclusion and fundamental deterioration at a company are distinct risks, even if they're related. Mechanical selling by passive funds stems from a change in index eligibility rules, not from anything about the company's earnings or its Bitcoin holdings. That said, the two aren't entirely separable either - sustained share-price pressure from index-driven selling can, in turn, damage a company's ability to raise capital, which matters a great deal for a company whose entire growth strategy depends on issuing shares at a premium.

A well-telegraphed event is often substantially priced in before it happens. MSCI's non-operating company rule had been flagged as a risk since its October 15 adoption. As the November 30 effective date approaches, it's worth watching whether the market's reaction to the actual event differs meaningfully from the reaction that already played out in anticipation of it.

Falling below a treasury company's average cost basis is a psychological inflection point. Bitcoin trading below Strategy's average purchase price doesn't instantly damage the company's balance sheet, but it often marks the moment the market starts reassessing the risk of that business model more broadly. For any company running a treasury strategy like this, tracking the gap between its average acquisition cost and the current market price of the underlying asset is a useful ongoing habit.

## FAQ

### Does MSCI's index removal force Strategy to sell its Bitcoin?
No. The selling triggered by MSCI's removal comes from passive funds tracking the MSCI USA, World, and ACWI indexes offloading their MSTR shares - that's a transfer of shares between investors, not a mechanism that forces Strategy as a company to sell any of the Bitcoin it holds. That said, if the stock stays under pressure, the appeal of Strategy's usual playbook - issuing new shares to fund further Bitcoin purchases - could diminish.

### Did JPMorgan actually lose money on this position?
Based on the filing, it's an unrealized, paper loss calculated by applying the subsequent price decline to the September 30 valuation. Whether JPMorgan has since added to or trimmed the position, or exercised any of its puts, won't be clear until the next 13F filing. It's also worth noting that the put position - which was larger than the long position - may have gained value during this same decline, so the net profit or loss on the combined position can't be determined from public filings alone.

### What happens after November 30?
MSCI's index removal officially takes effect at the close of trading on November 30. Index funds and ETFs tracking MSCI USA, World, and ACWI are expected to concentrate their rebalancing-related selling around that date. Because the event has been publicly known since the November 11 confirmation, though, some analysts argue that much of the anticipated impact may already be reflected in the stock's price, meaning the reaction on the effective date itself could turn out to be more muted than the decline that preceded it.
