## What Happened

Semiconductor stocks sold off again on Monday, November 16, extending a reversal that began just two trading days earlier. As of 11:30 a.m. Eastern time, the Dow Jones Industrial Average was down about 0.1%, the S&P 500 had slipped 0.8%, and the Nasdaq Composite had fallen 1.4%. Those headline index numbers understate what was actually happening underneath: the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index (SOX) dropped as much as 3.93% earlier in the session, far steeper than the broader market, and AMD (NASDAQ: AMD) extended its slide with another drop of more than 8% on the day. Nvidia (NASDAQ: NVDA) fell over 2%, and Broadcom (NASDAQ: AVGO) also declined. Notably, the selling wasn't confined to Nvidia's direct rivals - "neocloud" names CoreWeave and Nebius, which rent out Nvidia GPUs to AI customers rather than compete with Nvidia's chips, fell right alongside them, a signal that something broader than a simple AMD-versus-Nvidia trade was unwinding.

To understand Monday's drop, you have to rewind to the previous week. After Nvidia's board announced an independent investigation into CEO Jensen Huang on November 8, the situation escalated fast: Huang resigned as president, CEO, and board member effective immediately on November 12, with CFO Colette Kress stepping in as interim chief executive. During that stretch, investors rotated money out of Nvidia and into its chip rivals, and AMD's stock, riding that rotation, surged more than 30% at one point over the course of the week. But the rally cracked almost as fast as it built. On Friday, November 13, AMD tumbled 8.75% to close at $534.06, while Nvidia fell 2.01% and Broadcom dropped 4.76% - the whole trade reversing in a single session. Monday's action shows that reversal wasn't a one-day blip: AMD kept sliding, meaning the stock has now given back a large share of the gains it built up over barely a week.

The timing isn't a coincidence. Nvidia reports fiscal third-quarter earnings after the close on Tuesday, November 17 - its first full investor conference call since Huang's resignation, and Colette Kress's first time addressing the market as interim CEO. Options traders are already pricing in roughly a 9.6% move for Nvidia shares after the report, the largest expected swing since 2024. With that much uncertainty concentrated into a single, imminent event for the whole sector, holding onto a rotation trade built around last week's narrative - that Nvidia's rivals deserved a premium because Nvidia carried company-specific risk - has become a lot less comfortable.

## Why the 'Nvidia Alternative' Trade Turned Against Itself

Last week's shift into AMD and Broadcom made sense at the time. With a leadership vacuum creating company-specific risk at Nvidia, money rotating into same-theme peers that weren't exposed to that particular risk is a textbook relative-value trade. The problem is how fast that trade got crowded. A stock gaining more than 30% in a matter of days builds up just as much pressure to take profits as it does conviction in the underlying thesis - regardless of how sound that thesis is. And once Nvidia itself is about to address the very uncertainty that drove investors away from it in the first place, the original rationale for favoring AMD and Broadcom gets diluted. That gives traders a strong incentive to lock in gains before the event, rather than hold the position through it.

The fact that CoreWeave and Nebius fell too is the more interesting detail here. Unlike AMD and Broadcom, these aren't Nvidia's competitors - they're cloud operators that lease out Nvidia's own GPUs. Their shares declining in tandem with AMD and Broadcom suggests this wasn't just a relative-value trade unwinding between two sides of a bet; it looks more like sector-wide de-risking, where investors are trimming exposure across the entire chip and AI-infrastructure complex rather than fine-tuning which name within it deserves to win. Ahead of the single most consequential data point for the whole group, the simpler move for many traders is just to reduce exposure broadly until the uncertainty clears, rather than keep betting on which stock benefits most.

The gap between the SOX's 3.93% intraday drop and the Nasdaq's 1.4% decline tells the same story from a different angle: the headline indexes looked only mildly shaky, but one specific sub-sector was absorbing a disproportionate share of the selling. This isn't a new pattern. Back in August, Nvidia, AMD, Broadcom, and Meta all sold off sharply on the same day that the equal-weighted S&P 500 actually rose - a reminder that a falling benchmark doesn't always mean broad market stress; sometimes it just means a handful of mega-cap, thematically concentrated names are getting sold while most of the market holds up fine.

## What to Take Away From This

- **A relative-value trade built on one company's bad news gets riskier to hold as the event that caused that news approaches resolution.** The AMD/Broadcom rotation was anchored to Nvidia's leadership crisis; as Tuesday's earnings call approaches - the next chapter in that very story - the incentive to take profits grew sharply. Rallies anchored to a single headline are especially vulnerable once the market knows exactly when the next chapter in that headline arrives.
- **When a competitor's suppliers or customers sell off alongside it, that's usually a sign of sector-wide de-risking, not a company-specific story.** CoreWeave and Nebius aren't Nvidia rivals, so their declines point to something broader than an "AMD versus Nvidia" framing alone would explain.
- **Compare the headline index move to the move in the specific sub-sector driving it.** The Nasdaq's 1.4% drop looked moderate, but the semiconductor index fell nearly three times as much. Watching only the benchmark can hide exactly where the real selling pressure is concentrated.
- **Ahead of a major, scheduled catalyst, stocks only loosely connected to that catalyst can still get caught in the volatility.** When enough traders decide to de-risk at once, correlated selling can override individual fundamentals for a session or two, which is a good reminder to prioritize position sizing and risk management over picking relative winners in moments like this.

## FAQ

### Did something new go wrong at AMD specifically to cause this drop?
No specific new negative headline about AMD has emerged. The more likely explanation is that AMD's stock had surged more than 30% in just over a week on rotation out of Nvidia, and that run attracted profit-taking combined with broader de-risking ahead of Nvidia's earnings report.

### Does this reversal mean the "AMD and Broadcom as Nvidia alternatives" trade is over for good?
Not necessarily. Rotation within the chip sector tends to recur around specific news events rather than disappear permanently. What this episode does confirm is that a trade that builds up extremely fast can unwind just as fast. Whether the broader rotation theme continues will likely depend on how money flows once Nvidia's earnings uncertainty is resolved.

### What should investors watch for in Tuesday's Nvidia earnings call?
Beyond the revenue and margin figures themselves, the key focus is how interim CEO Colette Kress addresses whether customer relationships, supply agreements, and executive retention held steady through the leadership transition. With options markets pricing in a 9.6% move, the reaction to this report is likely to ripple back through chip-sector positioning more broadly, not just Nvidia's own stock.
