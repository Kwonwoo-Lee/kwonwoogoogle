## What Happened

The Nasdaq Composite just closed out October down 5.3% for the month, its worst since March 2025, and Wall Street barely gets a breather before the next stretch of risk. The week of November 2-6 packs four separate catalysts into five trading days: Apple's (Nasdaq: AAPL) fiscal fourth-quarter report, AMD's (Nasdaq: AMD) earnings - widely seen as the real test for semiconductors after Intel's guidance shock - the U.S. midterm elections on Tuesday, November 3, and the October jobs report on Friday, November 6. CNBC's Jim Cramer summed up the mood in his October 30 week-ahead column, warning that concerns about AI spending are still weighing on tech stocks and that "the rout may not be over."

Walking through the calendar day by day: Apple reports after the closing bell on Monday, November 2. Cramer says he still likes the stock, but flagged that its valuation - close to 40 times earnings - leaves "almost no room for disappointment." His own position, in his words, is that he'd "trade it, but I am going to own it." Bank of America has already warned that Apple's guidance for the December quarter, the critical holiday-season period, could come in weaker than the Street expects. The very next day, Tuesday, November 3, happens to be both Election Day and AMD's earnings date. Cramer called AMD's report "another important test for the semiconductor sector" following Intel's disappointing guidance, and said he expects AMD to beat Intel's numbers. Then, to close out the week, Friday's October jobs report is where Cramer expects "strong hiring with limited wage growth" - a combination that could ease inflation worries rather than add to them.

Of the four catalysts, the midterms are drawing the most scrutiny from institutional investors. According to an analysis from Man Group, the world's largest publicly traded hedge fund and alternatives manager, S&P 500 options are pricing in roughly 1.2 times a typical day's volatility for November 4, the day after the election - about 20% above normal, but broadly in line with past midterm cycles. In other words, the options market is betting on a fairly ordinary outcome: a result that's known by the morning of November 4. Prediction-market odds on Polymarket, cited in Man's research, put the probability of a Democratic sweep of both chambers at 66% and a Republican sweep at just 8%. The remaining roughly 26% covers some form of divided government, where neither party controls both chambers outright.

## Why Betting on a "Clean Result" Carries Hidden Risk

That's exactly the assumption Man Group's quant researcher, Adam Keogh, is pushing back on. "The market is pricing the midterms as a largely ordinary event, on the assumption that the result is known on November 4," he said. "That assumption carries a risk, though." History backs him up: when control of a chamber hinges on a handful of close races, certification and recounts have dragged on for weeks in past cycles. If that happens again this time, positions built on the premise of a clean, fast outcome could unwind all at once, hitting specific sectors and stocks that had been priced for a known result.

And which sectors move depends heavily on who wins. Man Group's analysis finds that a Democratic sweep would likely lift healthcare and retail stocks, while weighing on capital-markets names like investment banks and brokers, and to a smaller degree on aerospace and defense. A Republican sweep would likely flip that pattern in the other direction. JPMorgan takes a somewhat different view, arguing that gridlock - divided government - is actually the most favorable outcome for markets, with healthcare, defense, civil infrastructure, and select technology names as the beneficiaries. Its "red wall" basket, built around a full Republican sweep, includes names like Devon Energy, Lockheed Martin, L3Harris, Bloom Energy, Veeva Systems and Bank of America. Alpine Macro, for its part, expects the midterms to play out as a classic "sell the rumor, buy the news" event - meaning stocks beaten down by pre-election anxiety could actually rally once the outcome is confirmed.

All of this is also landing on top of a market that's already running hotter than usual on fear. The VIX, Wall Street's so-called fear gauge, broke above 22 intraday on October 16 for the first time in four months, and closed that week up 34.7% at 19.99 - its biggest weekly jump since early June. It climbed again on October 22, rising 6% to close at 20.24 after a 14% jump to 19.03 the day before, on a session also driven by Iran-related geopolitical tension and crude oil topping $100 a barrel. In short, Wall Street is heading into the midterms, two major tech earnings reports and a jobs number all at once, from a starting point of elevated anxiety rather than calm.

## What to Take Away From This

- **What the market is pricing in and what could actually happen are not the same thing.** Options markets are betting on a clean, fast midterm result on November 4 - a fairly ordinary assumption - but the tail risk of a delayed count isn't fully reflected in that pricing, according to multiple analysts.
- **When several major catalysts land in the same week, isolating any single cause after the fact gets much harder.** If stocks move sharply that week, untangling how much came from Apple's guidance, AMD's results, election uncertainty, or the jobs report may not be straightforward even in hindsight. Position sizing should account for that ambiguity up front.
- **Election-driven sector bets are safer approached as scenario diversification than as an all-in bet on one winner.** As both Man Group's and JPMorgan's analyses show, the winning sectors differ across a blue sweep, a red sweep, and gridlock - and gridlock is arguably the scenario Wall Street actually prefers, a detail many investors overlook.
- **"Sell the rumor, buy the news" is a real pattern in event-driven trading, but a delayed result can distort or postpone it entirely.** Alpine Macro's constructive scenario still assumes the outcome gets confirmed in a reasonably short window.
- **Entering a cluster of catalysts with the VIX already elevated is itself a setup for outsized moves.** In a market that's already on edge, the same piece of news can trigger a larger-than-usual price reaction than it would in a calmer environment.

## FAQ

### Is it a coincidence that AMD reports earnings on Election Day?

Yes, purely a scheduling coincidence. But for investors, having two major catalysts - the election and a major semiconductor earnings report - land on the same day makes it harder to attribute that day's market reaction to either one individually.

### If Democrats sweep both chambers, is that automatically bad for the overall stock market?

Not necessarily. According to Man Group's and JPMorgan's analyses, a Democratic sweep could actually lift healthcare and retail stocks, with the pressure concentrated more narrowly on capital-markets names like investment banks and brokers, and to a lesser extent on defense. The right lens is sector-by-sector, not "good or bad for the market" as a whole.

### What specifically happens if the vote count drags on?

Based on past cycles, when control of a chamber comes down to a small number of close races, official certification can take weeks. In that scenario, sector bets built on the assumption of a clean result - buying the blue-sweep winners, buying the red-sweep winners, and so on - can unwind or reverse under the uncertainty, producing additional price swings in those sectors.

### Which matters more for markets this week: Apple's earnings or the midterms?

Comparing the two head-to-head isn't especially useful. Apple's guidance is a single-company catalyst that can shake confidence in megacap valuations broadly, while the midterms reset how the market prices policy risk across entire sectors. The fact that two structurally different risks are landing in the same week is exactly what makes this stretch unusually volatile.
