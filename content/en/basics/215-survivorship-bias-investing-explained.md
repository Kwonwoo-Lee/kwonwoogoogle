## Why Historical Returns Always Look Better Than They Should

"This strategy returned 15% a year over the last 20 years." "This fund beat its benchmark five years running." "If you'd put money into the Nasdaq 100 two decades ago, you'd be sitting on a fortune by now." Numbers like these are easy to take at face value. But they all tend to leave out the same thing: the funds that went under, the companies that got delisted, the strategies that quietly stopped being tracked because they failed. If you only look at the funds that are still open today, or the companies still sitting in an index today, every failure that happened along the way simply never shows up in the data. This pattern — where only the survivors remain in the dataset, while the failures silently drop out, making the overall statistic look better than reality — is called **survivorship bias**.

The idea is often introduced through a story from World War II involving statistician Abraham Wald. The U.S. military studied the location of bullet holes on bombers that made it back from missions, planning to reinforce the areas that were hit most often. Wald spotted the flaw: those were only the planes that survived. The real information was in the planes that never came back — the spots with few bullet holes on the surviving planes were exactly the spots that, if hit, meant the plane didn't make it home. The armor needed to go where the surviving planes showed the fewest hits, not the most. The same trap shows up in investment data in exactly the same shape.

## The Mechanism: How Disappearing Cases Push the Average Up

In investing, survivorship bias creeps in through three main channels.

The first is **fund databases**. A fund that performs badly for long enough tends to see investors pull their money out, and it eventually gets liquidated or merged into another fund. Underperformance is, in fact, the single most common reason a fund closes. If a database or ranking site only counts funds that are "currently operating," every closed fund simply vanishes from the sample — and because almost all of the funds that vanish are the ones that performed poorly, the average return calculated from the survivors alone is mechanically biased upward from the start.

The second is **the index itself**. Benchmarks like the S&P 500, KOSPI 200, or Nasdaq 100 periodically reconstitute their membership: companies that deteriorate or get delisted drop out, and growing newcomers take their place. That process keeps the index healthy and relevant, but it causes a specific problem when someone tries to trace an index's "historical return" using only the companies that happen to be members of it today. Any losses suffered by a company that later went bankrupt or got kicked out are erased from the calculation simply because that company is no longer on today's list.

The third is **look-ahead bias in backtesting**. When testing an investment strategy against historical data, it's easy to accidentally build the test around "the list of companies that exist today" and run it backward 10 or 20 years. That implicitly assumes an investor back then somehow knew in advance which companies would survive to the present. A real investor living through that period would have held some of the companies that later went bankrupt or got delisted — but those cases have since disappeared and never make it into the test.

## How Large Is the Effect, in Practice?

A body of research gives some sense of the scale. Early academic work by Elton, Gruber, and Blake (1996) and Carhart (1997) on the U.S. mutual fund industry found that the size of the bias grows with the length of the measurement window. Carhart's estimates ranged from roughly 0.07 percentage points a year over a one-year sample up to nearly 1 percentage point a year over samples longer than 15 years. More recently, an analysis by Dimensional Fund Advisors covering U.S. active equity funds from 1991 to 2020 found that the median alpha was -0.84% when looking only at surviving funds, but dropped to -1.44% once closed funds were added back in — implying survivorship bias had been inflating reported fund performance by roughly 0.6 percentage points a year. That's a specific estimate from one study's methodology and time period, not a universal constant, but the direction is consistent across the literature: survivorship bias always pushes measured returns up, never down.

A widely cited statistic shows the bias in action: claims that "62% of large-cap value funds beat their benchmark over the last five years" typically count only funds that survived the entire five-year window. A fund that got liquidated mid-period — likely because it was underperforming — simply isn't counted as a loss at all. Had it been counted, the real win rate would likely look considerably worse. On the index side, a 2026 study of India's Nifty Smallcap 250 found that a backtest built only on today's index members excluded 82.5% of every stock that had ever belonged to the index at some point, and that doing so overstated the index's Sharpe ratio by roughly 9%. On the backtesting side, one data vendor illustrated the effect using the Nasdaq 100: a 22-year backtest using only companies that still exist today showed about 12.75% annualized returns, while a version that properly included the losses from delisted companies instead showed roughly -17% annualized over the same period. That's one vendor's illustration rather than a general finding, but it makes the point vividly: including or excluding delisted names can completely flip the conclusion. An even starker case comes from quant research firm Alpha Architect, which found that properly including delisted stocks in a backtest of a "52-week-low reversal" strategy flipped the sign of its returns entirely — turning an apparent winning strategy into a losing one. Survivorship bias doesn't just inflate the size of a return; it can overturn the basic conclusion of whether a strategy works at all.

The same trap shows up outside of raw numbers, too, in the success stories investors read all the time. Articles and books built around "what the people who made a fortune on this stock had in common" or "the investing principles behind people who turned a small stake into a windfall" draw their sample only from the people who succeeded — the far larger number of people who followed a similar approach and quietly lost money and left the market never make it into the story. Success gets interviewed, written about, and put on YouTube; failure mostly disappears without a trace. That leaves readers with a far stronger impression that "follow these principles and you'll succeed" than the real odds would support. It's structurally the same bias as in fund and index data — it just shows up as narrative instead of as a number.

## Why This Is a Different Problem Than Behavioral Biases

The biases covered in [efficient markets vs. behavioral finance](/en/basics/efficient-market-hypothesis-behavioral-finance/) — loss aversion, overconfidence, herding — describe psychological distortions that happen while an individual investor is processing information and making a decision. Survivorship bias works differently: the data sitting in front of the investor is already skewed before any decision gets made. Statisticians classify it as a type of **selection bias** — the sample being analyzed fails to fairly represent the full population, because it's been implicitly filtered down to cases that meet one specific condition: having survived to today. Wald's bomber story captures exactly this distinction. The issue wasn't that individual pilots or analysts reasoned poorly — it was that the only data available for analysis had already been filtered down to planes that made it home. The same structural problem applies to investment data. Even a perfectly rational, unbiased investor will reach a distorted conclusion if the dataset they're working from has already had its failures quietly removed. That's why this problem isn't solved by "thinking more carefully" — it's solved by checking whether the underlying sample is fair in the first place.

## What an Investor Can Actually Check

Survivorship bias can't be eliminated entirely, but the odds of being misled by it can be reduced substantially. The most basic habit is to ask, whenever you look at a fund performance table or a backtest result, whether liquidated or merged funds and delisted companies were included in the data. Reputable financial data providers are aware of this problem and often explicitly label datasets as "survivorship-bias-free" or "point-in-time," meaning they reflect only the securities that actually existed and traded at each historical moment. A backtest or performance claim built on data without that kind of labeling deserves extra skepticism. It also helps to remember that the bias compounds with time: a claim about "the last 20 years" has had far more opportunity to accumulate hidden failures than a claim about "the last year." As covered in [the index rebalancing effect](/en/basics/index-rebalancing-effect/), an index is a living, constantly-reshuffled thing — and once you internalize that an index's membership list is always changing, it becomes clear why judging an index's past purely by who's in it today will structurally skew toward an overly optimistic picture.

## Key Takeaways

- Survivorship bias is the pattern where funds that closed, companies that got delisted, or strategies that failed quietly drop out of a dataset, making statistics based only on the survivors look better than reality.
- The effect shows up the same way in fund performance databases, index historical returns, and backtested strategies, and academic research shows it grows larger the longer the measurement period.
- Unlike behavioral-finance biases such as loss aversion or herding, survivorship bias is a selection-bias problem baked into the data itself, not a psychological distortion in how an investor reasons.
- The most practical defense is checking whether long-term performance claims or backtest results account for the failures that disappeared from the record — longer time horizons deserve more scrutiny, not less.

## FAQ

### Is survivorship bias the same thing as selection bias?
Not exactly. Selection bias is the broader category — any case where a sample fails to fairly represent the full population. Survivorship bias is one specific form of it, caused by the sample being implicitly filtered down to cases that happened to survive to the present.

### Does this mean I shouldn't trust historical fund or index return data at all?
No — it means you should check whether the data accounts for the failures that disappeared. Reputable data providers often offer a survivorship-bias-adjusted version of their datasets; it's worth confirming whether that adjustment has been made before taking a long-term performance claim at face value.

### What should I watch out for if I run my own backtests?
The key mistake to avoid is testing a strategy using only the list of companies that exist today. Make sure the dataset includes securities that were actually trading at each historical point in time, even if they were later delisted or dropped from an index — and if you can't confirm that, treat the backtest's results with caution rather than taking them at face value.
