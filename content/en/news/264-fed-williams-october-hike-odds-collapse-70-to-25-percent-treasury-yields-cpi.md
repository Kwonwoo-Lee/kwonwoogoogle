## What Happened

Tuesday, October 13 was supposed to be a day dominated by bank earnings, and for most of the morning it was. But the more consequential headline for the broader market came from New York Fed President John Williams, a permanent voting member of the Federal Open Market Committee and one of its most closely watched voices. Speaking publicly that day, Williams said "there is no need for urgency" in adjusting the Fed's current policy stance, adding that "one further upward adjustment" to the benchmark rate "may be appropriate" before year-end - but notably declining to commit to the Fed's next meeting, October 27-28, as the moment it happens.

The market's reaction was immediate and sizable. Odds of a hike at that October 27-28 meeting, which had sat near 70% just days earlier (a level this site covered in its own "week ahead" preview), collapsed to roughly 25% within hours of Williams' remarks. Fed funds futures and swaps traders effectively pushed their expected timing for the next quarter-point increase from this month to December. Goldman Sachs' economics team moved its own forecast for the next hike from October to December in response. Treasury yields eased in sympathy: the 10-year yield, which had been pressing up against its highest levels in roughly 24 years, slipped a couple of basis points to around 5.22%, while the 2-year yield - more sensitive to near-term Fed expectations - held in the high-4.80% area, essentially flat to modestly lower on the session. Fed Vice Chair Philip Jefferson added a similarly unhurried tone the same week, saying "my colleagues and I will need to come to our own judgment, which may take more time." Against that backdrop, the S&P 500 closed essentially flat near 7,805, the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell about 180 points to roughly 51,670 as bank earnings produced a mixed picture (Goldman Sachs beat big, JPMorgan missed), and the Nasdaq Composite slipped modestly as memory-chip stocks extended a two-day slide.

## Why One Sentence From One Fed Official Moved So Much

To understand why "no need for urgency" carried so much weight, it helps to see what it was responding to. Just one trading day earlier, on Monday, October 12, Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack - another sitting voter on the 2026 FOMC - had argued the opposite case, telling a conference that "a restrictive monetary policy stance is appropriate to ensure a timely return to 2% inflation" and that she generally prefers to tighten "relatively quickly" rather than wait and have to correct with a larger move later. Hammack dissented in favor of a hike back in July and has kept pushing that hawkish line since. Williams' comments the very next day landed as a direct counterweight from an arguably more influential voice: as president of the New York Fed, Williams sits on the FOMC permanently (he never rotates off, unlike most regional presidents), gets to set policy implementation through the open market desk, and is traditionally viewed as one of the committee's closest allies to the Fed chair's own thinking. When the market has to weigh a hawkish regional president against a more measured New York Fed president in the same 24-hour window, it tends to lean toward whichever official looks more likely to actually swing the committee's vote - and that is usually Williams.

The current setup makes every one of these data points matter more than usual. The Fed raised its target range to 3.75%-4.00% in September, its first hike since 2023 and a reversal of the cutting cycle that had been underway as recently as last year. That September move came on a 12-0 vote, suggesting the committee was more unified going into the hike than it has been in the weeks since. The September "dot plot" - the anonymous survey of individual officials' rate projections - showed most members expecting the funds rate to land between 4.1% and 4.4% by year-end, which only pencils out if one more quarter-point move happens in 2026. The open question has never been whether another hike is coming this year; it's been exactly when, and Williams' remarks are the clearest signal yet that "when" has shifted from this month to December. Not every official agrees on the pace beyond that, either - Minneapolis Fed President Neel Kashkari said just two weeks earlier that he expects one hike before year-end and potentially another extending into 2027, a reminder that even among officials who agree hikes aren't finished, there's real disagreement about how many more are needed and how fast.

That disagreement is exactly why Wednesday's September CPI report, due at 8:30 a.m. Eastern, carries outsized weight. Economists expect headline CPI to accelerate to roughly 3.6%-3.7% year-over-year, up from August's 3.4%, with core CPI (excluding food and energy) holding closer to 2.4%. A hotter-than-expected print would hand Hammack's camp fresh ammunition and could push October hike odds back up from their newly depressed 25% level. A softer print would tend to validate Williams' "no urgency" framing and could pull Treasury yields down further from their already-elevated, multi-decade-high range. Either way, a single data release tomorrow morning is positioned to resolve - at least temporarily - a debate that two sitting Fed voters couldn't settle in the same week.

## What to Take Away From This

- **Not all Fed speakers carry equal market-moving weight.** Hammack is an FOMC voter, but Williams' role as New York Fed president and the committee's closest link to policy execution historically gives his comments outsized influence over futures pricing. When officials disagree, markets tend to react more to whichever one is viewed as more likely to swing the committee.
- **"No urgency" is not the same as "no hike."** Williams explicitly left the door open to one more increase before year-end - he simply pushed back on the idea that it needs to happen at the very next meeting. Reading his comments as fully dovish would be a mistake; the more accurate read is a shift in timing, not direction.
- **Rate-hike odds can swing by huge margins on a single data point or speech.** October's odds moved from roughly 70% to 25% within about a week, driven by a combination of softer data and Williams' remarks. Anyone positioning around a specific meeting date should expect that kind of volatility to continue right up until the decision itself.
- **A single CPI report rarely settles a genuine policy debate on its own, but it can move the probabilities a lot.** With officials publicly split between Hammack's hawkish urgency and Williams' more patient stance, Wednesday's inflation data is the next concrete input both sides will have to reckon with - traders should expect odds to move again once it's out, in whichever direction the number points.
- **Treasury yields and Fed-speak move together, but not always proportionately.** A two basis-point move on the 10-year sounds small, but it came right at a level close to a multi-decade high, meaning even modest shifts in the rate outlook are happening against an unusually sensitive backdrop for bond prices.

## FAQ

### Did the Fed actually decide anything on October 13?

No. October 13 wasn't a scheduled FOMC meeting day - it was a single Fed official's public remarks. The next actual policy decision comes at the October 27-28 meeting. What changed on October 13 was market-implied probability (how traders are pricing the odds of a hike at that meeting), not Fed policy itself.

### Why does John Williams' opinion matter more than other regional Fed presidents'?

The New York Fed president holds a permanent vote on the FOMC every year, unlike the other eleven regional presidents, who rotate onto the voting roster. The New York Fed also executes the committee's market operations, giving Williams a day-to-day view of financial conditions that other officials don't have in the same way. Markets have historically treated his public comments as a reasonably reliable signal of where Fed leadership's thinking is heading.

### What happens if Wednesday's CPI report comes in hotter than expected?

A hotter print (headline meaningfully above the roughly 3.6%-3.7% consensus, or core above 2.4%) would likely push October hike odds back up from the 25% level markets settled at after Williams' comments, and could send Treasury yields back toward their recent highs. It wouldn't guarantee a hike at the October 27-28 meeting on its own, but it would materially shift the probability the market assigns to one.
