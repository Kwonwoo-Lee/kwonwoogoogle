## The Question Splitting Chip Investors: Is the Cycle Over, or Just Finding a Bottom?

Every time memory chip stocks sell off hard, the same argument plays out on trading desks. One side argues that a sharp pullback alone doesn't prove the cycle has ended — it just means the floor got slipperier. The other side points to memory's long-standing pattern: demand surges, supply follows, and prices eventually buckle under their own weight, a pattern they expect to repeat again. Both camps, it turns out, are staring at the same variable: **inventory**. How far customer inventory has fallen, whether buyers have started restocking yet, and when the next new fab comes online — these are the specific facts analysts argue over. This lesson unpacks why a single variable, inventory, moves stocks in cyclical sectors like semiconductors, chemicals, and shipping months ahead of the earnings reports that eventually confirm the story.

## What the Inventory Cycle Actually Is: The Gap Between Production and Sales

The inventory cycle starts from a simple fact: production and sales rarely move in perfect lockstep. If a company makes 100 units and sells all 100, inventory stays flat. Sell only 90, and 10 units pile up as inventory. Sell 110 by drawing down stock, and inventory shrinks by 10. National accounts call this gap "inventory investment," and as it builds up and gets worked off repeatedly, it creates a rhythm in the broader economy — a cycle. The complication is that this gap isn't just a matter of selling more or less in any given month. Companies set production and order volumes based on **forecasts of future sales**, not realized sales, and those forecasts get distorted more and more severely the further they travel back up the supply chain.

## The Bullwhip Effect: Why a Small Consumer Shift Grows Larger With Each Step Upstream

That distortion has a name: the **bullwhip effect**, first formalized by MIT's Jay Forrester in 1961 (hence its alternate name, the Forrester effect). The name comes from how a small flick at the handle end of a whip produces a much larger swing at the tip. Picture a simple chain: retailer → distributor → manufacturer → component/raw-material supplier. If a retailer normally sells 20 units a day and suddenly sells 70 one day, it reads that as a demand surge and bumps its next order up to 100. The distributor sees the retailer's order jump roughly fivefold and interprets that as an even bigger demand spike, so it places a larger order with the manufacturer. The manufacturer, seeing that order, ramps production and orders even more raw material from its suppliers. The end consumer's actual demand spiked for a single day and then returned to 20 — but each step back up the chain amplifies the swing. Procter & Gamble famously noticed this with diapers, a product with remarkably stable end-consumer demand, after observing wildly swinging orders from its upstream distributors, which is where the "bullwhip" label stuck. Batch ordering (saving up orders and placing them in bulk), overordering after a stockout scare, and long lead times (the gap between placing an order and actually receiving goods) are the usual suspects that make the distortion worse.

## Destocking and Restocking: Why the Cycle Swings Between Two Phases

The buildup and unwinding of this amplified swing typically plays out in two phases. When demand first softens, customers burn through inventory they're already holding before cutting new orders — so the orders they place fall far faster and further than actual end demand did. This is **destocking**, and during this phase, chipmakers and component suppliers see bookings fall harder and sooner than the real underlying demand decline would suggest. Once inventory gets worked down close to the bone, the dynamic flips. Even a modest pickup in real demand pushes customers — who are now holding almost no buffer — to order not just what they need immediately but enough to rebuild safety stock on top of it, so orders surge all at once. This is **restocking**. What makes it sharper still is that suppliers have usually throttled back production or delayed expansion during the destocking phase, so when demand turns, supply can't ramp up instantly to meet it. The overlap of recovering demand and constrained supply is what produces the steep jumps in both order volume and price that show up at cycle turns — an effect that is most pronounced in industries like semiconductors, chemicals, and shipping, where capacity additions take a year or two to come online.

## Why Stock Prices Turn Before Earnings Do

This gets at the question investors care about most: why do chip stocks often bottom and start recovering months before earnings actually trough — sometimes while headlines are still describing the industry as weak? The answer lies in what a stock price is actually pricing: **expected future cash flow**, not last quarter's results. Once early signs appear that destocking is nearing its end — customer inventory approaching a floor, new orders declining less steeply than the drop in real demand — investors start pricing in the restocking phase and the supply-constrained price gains that typically follow a few quarters later. This is the same mechanism covered in [What Is the PMI?](/en/basics/pmi-purchasing-managers-index-explained/), where the new-orders sub-index tends to turn before output or employment does. Because inventory and new orders move ahead of reported earnings, the stock price pricing in that shift tends to turn ahead of the earnings release that eventually confirms it. That said, this is a tendency rooted in how the supply chain mechanically amplifies and leads real demand changes — not a fixed rule that falling inventory automatically means "buy now."

## Korea's Inventory Cycle Indicator: A Building Block of the Official Leading Index

This mechanism isn't confined to individual industries — it's baked directly into Korea's official business-cycle statistics. Statistics Korea's monthly **Composite Leading Index** combines seven components meant to forecast where the economy is headed: the inventory cycle indicator, the economic sentiment index, the machinery domestic shipment index, construction orders, the export-import price ratio, the KOSPI, and the long-short interest rate spread. Notably, the KOSPI itself is one of those seven inputs — a reminder that stock markets are generally treated as leading the real economy, not lagging it. The inventory cycle indicator is generally understood to be derived from comparing the growth rate of inventory against the growth rate of production or shipments; periods where inventory growth lags production growth are typically read as destocking phases, and the reverse as inventory buildup. The exact weighting and calculation methodology, however, is best confirmed directly through Statistics Korea's and the Bank of Korea's published compilation manuals — the point here is the underlying logic for why this indicator is classified as leading, not its precise formula.

## A Numerical Walkthrough of the Amplification

A simplified hypothetical helps make the amplification concrete. Say consumer demand drops from 100 units a month to 95 — a 5% decline. Worried about a growing pile of unsold stock, the retailer cuts its next order from 100 to 80 units, a 20% reduction. The distributor, seeing the retailer's order fall 20%, reads that as a faster-than-expected slowdown and cuts its own order to the manufacturer from 100 to 60, a 40% drop. The manufacturer, facing a 40% order collapse, cuts its own production run by nearly half and trims raw-material orders even more sharply. Consumer demand moved just 5%, but the swing grew to 20%, then 40%, and larger still with each step up the chain. Restocking reverses the same chain in the other direction: if consumer demand ticks back up from 95 to 100, retailers, distributors, and manufacturers — all holding thin inventory — rush to rebuild buffer stock on top of meeting the modest demand increase, so orders jump by far more than that increase at every stage further upstream. In an industry like semiconductors, where new capacity takes a year or two to build, supply can't catch up instantly during this restocking phase, which is exactly what drives both order volumes and prices sharply higher at once.

## The Trap Investors Fall Into: Falling Inventory Doesn't Automatically Mean a Turn

The most common mistake in reading inventory data is treating a single data point — "inventory fell" — as confirmation that the cycle has turned. Inventory means different things depending on where it sits in the supply chain. Distributor inventory can fall while a manufacturer's own warehouses remain full, which would suggest the real turn is still some way off. It also matters whether falling inventory is accompanied by a price rebound. If inventory is falling but prices keep dropping, that may simply mean producers cut output even faster than demand fell — not that demand is actually recovering. More recently, the spread of long-term supply agreements (LTAs) in the chip industry, which lock in volumes and pricing across multiple years upfront, has been cited as a structural change that dampens the amplitude of the traditional inventory-price cycle. The framework still applies, in other words, but what's changing within that framework matters just as much. For the same reason, it's worth watching the timing of new fab startups or capacity expansions alongside inventory trends — if major new capacity comes online right as restocking begins, the inventory and pricing improvement that demand recovery was driving can get pushed back down by the added supply.

## Takeaway

- The inventory cycle is a rhythm created by the gap between production and sales (inventory investment) building up and unwinding, and the bullwhip effect amplifies that gap far beyond the actual change in end demand as it travels up the supply chain.
- The cycle alternates between destocking (early demand softening, as customers burn through existing stock) and restocking (once inventory is thin, a modest demand pickup combines with constrained supply to produce surging orders).
- Because inventory and new orders move ahead of — and more sharply than — actual demand, stock prices that price in that shift tend to turn ahead of the official earnings reports that eventually confirm it.
- Statistics Korea's Composite Leading Index includes an inventory cycle indicator among its seven components, and the KOSPI itself is also one of those components — underscoring that equity markets are generally treated as a leading, not lagging, signal.
- Falling inventory alone doesn't confirm a cycle turn — check where in the supply chain the inventory sits, whether prices are rebounding alongside it, and whether structural shifts like long-term supply contracts or new capacity timing are changing the cycle's usual amplitude.

## FAQ

### If the inventory cycle indicator turns, does that mean the cycle has already changed?
Not by itself. The inventory cycle indicator is a leading indicator that reflects the gap between inventory and production (or shipment) growth rates — it isn't a buy or sell signal tied to a specific reading. This lesson explains the mechanism behind it; in practice, it's generally read alongside where in the supply chain the inventory sits, whether prices are rebounding, and whether other leading indicators (like PMI new orders or sentiment indices) point the same direction.

### Does the inventory cycle only apply to semiconductors?
No. The effect is most visible in industries where capacity takes a long time to add and production can't be adjusted instantly to match shifting demand. Beyond semiconductors, chemicals, steel, shipping, and industrial equipment — the classic cyclical sectors — are all shaped by the same underlying mechanism.

### How does the inventory cycle indicator relate to the PMI new orders index?
Both aim to catch shifts in economic activity before they show up in official statistics, but they're built differently. PMI new orders comes from a monthly survey of purchasing managers, while the inventory cycle indicator is calculated from official production and inventory statistics. When the two point in the same direction, that alignment is generally read as a stronger signal than either one alone.
