## Why Does a Non-Subsidiary's Income Show Up Anyway

Look at a holding company's income statement and you'll sometimes find a line called "equity-method income" (or "share of profit of associates") sitting below operating income, often for a sizable amount — even though that company isn't a consolidated subsidiary and the holding company didn't receive anywhere near that much in dividends. [What Is Non-Controlling Interest](/en/basics/non-controlling-interest-explained/) covered how, once a company crosses the 50% ownership threshold and gains control, its subsidiary's full results get consolidated line-by-line, with the portion that isn't owned stripped out as non-controlling interest. The **equity method** covered in this lesson applies one tier below that — the 20-50% ownership band where a company has influence but not control — and it works on an entirely different logic. It's essential for understanding how Korean conglomerate and holding-company structures actually work, and it's also one of the accounting concepts most often confused with consolidation.

## Where the Line Falls: Significant Influence Without Control

How an investment gets accounted for depends on which band its ownership stake falls into. Below 20%, the stake is generally treated as a simple financial investment — recorded at fair value, with only dividends received counted as income. Between 20% and 50%, if the investor has **significant influence** — real input into board decisions or major policy, not just a passive stake — the investee is classified as an **associate**, and the **equity method** applies. Above 50%, or below 50% with demonstrable control in practice, the investee becomes a subsidiary and gets folded into consolidated financial statements. The key point is that this isn't a purely mechanical percentage cutoff. A stake under 20% can still qualify for the equity method if the investor holds a board seat or has real input into key decisions, and a stake above 20% can stay a simple financial investment if that influence is clearly absent.

## Three Treatments, Side by Side

Lining up all three treatments against the same investee makes the differences concrete. Suppose the investee earns net income of ₩100 billion and pays out ₩20 billion of it as dividends, and compare an investor holding 10% (simple investment), 30% (equity method), and 70% (consolidation).

| | Simple investment (10%) | Equity method (30%) | Consolidation (70%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Basis | No significant influence | Significant influence, no control | Control |
| Balance sheet | Carried at cost or fair value | Carrying value adjusted yearly for ownership share | Assets/liabilities 100% combined, NCI split out |
| Income statement | Only dividends received count as income | Ownership share of profit (₩30bn) booked as equity-pickup income | 100% of profit (₩100bn) combined, then split between parent and NCI |
| ₩20bn dividend | ₩2bn recognized as dividend income | ₩6bn deducted from the investment's carrying value (no income re-recognized) | Eliminated as an intercompany transaction; only the NCI portion leaves the group |
| Revenue/assets/liabilities consolidated? | No | No | Yes, 100% |

The same investee, the same results — but which band you fall into completely changes how it shows up in your own financials. Because crossing the 20% or 50% line changes the entire accounting framework, a disclosure about a group buying or selling a stake near either threshold is worth reading not just as an ownership change, but as a signal that the whole presentation of that stake in the financials is about to shift.

## The Mechanics: From Initial Purchase to Annual Adjustment

The core idea behind the equity method is simple: instead of pulling in the associate's full financial statements, you recognize only your ownership share of what it earned, updating both the income statement and the balance sheet every year. At acquisition, the investment is recorded at cost. Each year after that, when the associate reports net income, the investor recognizes its ownership share of that profit as **equity-pickup income** on its own income statement, and increases the carrying value of the investment by the same amount on the balance sheet. If the associate posts a loss instead, the investor recognizes an **equity-pickup loss** and reduces the carrying value accordingly. The investment's carrying value isn't frozen at the original purchase price — it's a living number that moves every year with the associate's results and dividend payouts.

Dividends get a different treatment. For a plain financial investment, dividends received are simply booked as income. Under the equity method, they aren't — because the investor already recognized its share of the associate's profit as income when it was earned, a dividend is treated as simply converting part of that already-recognized profit into cash. So receiving a dividend only reduces the investment's carrying value; it is not booked as separate income on top of the equity-pickup income already recorded. In other words, the equity method splits income recognition from cash receipt into two distinct steps: profit is booked when the associate reports it, and a dividend is just the mechanism by which part of that profit later turns into cash.

## A Numerical Example

Say Holding Company A buys a 30% stake in Associate B for ₩100 billion. In year one, B earns net income of ₩50 billion. A recognizes its 30% share — ₩15 billion — as equity-pickup income, and the investment's carrying value rises from ₩100 billion to ₩115 billion. If B also pays out ₩10 billion of that profit as dividends, A receives its 30% share (₩3 billion) in cash and deducts that amount from the carrying value.

| | Amount | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Opening carrying value | ₩100bn | Cost of 30% stake |
| Equity-pickup income from B's ₩50bn profit | +₩15bn | Recognized on the income statement |
| Dividend received (30% of ₩10bn) | -₩3bn | Cash received, deducted from carrying value |
| Closing carrying value | ₩112bn | ₩100bn + ₩15bn - ₩3bn |

If B then has a bad year and posts a net loss of ₩20 billion, A recognizes an equity-pickup loss of ₩6 billion (its 30% share) and the carrying value falls from ₩112 billion to ₩106 billion — without a single dollar of cash actually moving. That's the key thing to remember about equity-pickup income and loss: it's a non-cash item whose annual value swings purely with the associate's reported results.

## How It Distorts Valuation Metrics

Equity-pickup income typically shows up as non-operating income (or as a separately disclosed line) rather than inside operating income, but it flows fully into net income. That means a company with a large equity-method contribution can show a growing gap between operating income and net income, and metrics like the P/E ratio — which run on net income — can swing for reasons that have nothing to do with the core business. If core operating income is flat year over year but a key associate's results improve sharply, net income and EPS can jump, making the headline P/E look artificially cheap. The reverse also happens: a core business that's performing fine can show a shrinking net income — and an apparently expensive P/E — purely because an associate had a rough quarter. Anyone analyzing a stock with a meaningful equity-method contribution should check the equity-pickup line disclosed separately near the bottom of the consolidated income statement, and look at operating income and operating cash flow excluding that line to gauge the real health of the core business.

## Equity-Method Impairment: When the Associate Stumbles

Like any other asset, an equity-method investment gets tested for impairment when there are signs of trouble. If the associate's share price has stayed well below the investment's carrying value for a sustained period, or there's clear evidence the associate's financial condition has deteriorated, the investor estimates the recoverable amount and books the shortfall as an **equity-method impairment loss** all at once. As with [goodwill impairment](/en/basics/goodwill-impairment-explained/), this is a non-cash accounting loss, but it can hit net income hard in a single period. When the associate is itself publicly listed, a market cap that sits well below the equity-method carrying value for an extended stretch is itself one of the signals investors watch for as a possible precursor to an impairment charge.

## Why This Matters So Much for Korean Conglomerates

Korean conglomerates — especially groups that have converted into holding-company structures — frequently hold their key affiliates in the 20-50% band rather than owning them outright above 50%. As covered in [The Holding Company Discount](/en/basics/holding-company-discount/), part of why holding-company stock trades below the net asset value of its underlying stakes is that an equity-method associate's entire business gets compressed into a single "equity-pickup income" line, making it hard for investors to intuitively see what's actually happening inside it. In groups tied together through [circular shareholding](/en/basics/circular-shareholding-explained/), affiliates sometimes hold equity-method stakes in each other, which means one affiliate's results can ripple into another's reported profit purely through the equity-pickup mechanism. Because of this, pulling apart each equity-method associate individually — the kind of analysis used in a [sum-of-the-parts (SOTP) valuation](/en/basics/sum-of-the-parts-valuation-sotp/) — is often far more revealing of a group's true earning power than reading the single equity-pickup line on its own.

## What to Check in the Filings

Financial statement notes disclose, under "equity-method investments," exactly which associates a company holds stakes in, at what ownership percentage, and how much equity-pickup income or loss was recognized from each one in the period. If equity-pickup income makes up a large share of net income, it's worth distinguishing whether that figure reflects a one-off event at the associate (an asset sale, a reversal of a prior impairment) or a sustainable trend in the associate's core business. It's also worth comparing the equity-method investment's carrying value against its market value — if the associate is publicly listed, multiply its market cap by your ownership percentage. A wide gap between the two is worth tracing back to either a holding-company discount or the risk of a future impairment. When reading quarterly filings, check whether equity-pickup income or loss swung sharply from the prior quarter — a single associate's results are not infrequently the entire reason behind a holding company's earnings surprise or earnings shock in a given quarter, so an unusually large swing is worth tracing back to the specific associate behind it in the footnotes.

## Takeaways

- The equity method recognizes only the investor's ownership share of an associate's profit or loss each year — applied when ownership is 20-50% with significant influence but no control.
- Where consolidation combines 100% of a subsidiary's results and then splits out non-controlling interest, the equity method starts from the ownership percentage and adjusts the investment's carrying value every year.
- Equity-pickup income and loss are non-cash items; receiving a dividend reduces the investment's carrying value rather than being recognized as separate income.
- Stocks with a large equity-method contribution can show a growing gap between operating income and net income, so P/E and EPS should be checked against the core business separately from that line.
- Korean holding-company and conglomerate structures frequently hold key affiliates through the equity method, which is why SOTP-style analysis of each associate individually often beats reading the equity-pickup line alone.

## FAQ

### Does equity-pickup income mean cash actually came in?
No. Equity-pickup income is simply the accounting recognition of the investor's ownership share of the associate's profit — actual cash only arrives when a dividend is paid. Receiving a dividend reduces the investment's carrying value; it is not recognized as separate income on top of the equity-pickup income already booked.

### Which has a bigger effect on revenue — the equity method or consolidation?
Consolidation. A consolidated subsidiary's revenue, costs, assets, and liabilities are combined 100% into the parent's financials. An equity-method associate contributes none of its revenue or costs — only a single net equity-pickup income or loss line flows into net income. So no matter how large an equity-method associate is, it doesn't add to the parent's own reported revenue.

### Why does equity-method impairment happen?
If an associate's share price stays well below the investment's carrying value for a sustained period, or there's clear evidence its financial condition has deteriorated, the investor estimates the recoverable amount and books the shortfall as an impairment loss all at once. There's no cash outflow, but it can hit net income hard in a single period.
