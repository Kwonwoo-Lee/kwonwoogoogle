## The Subsidiary Is Booming — So Why Isn't the Parent's Profit?

Anyone who has owned a holding company or a conglomerate affiliate has probably seen this pattern: a headline announces a core subsidiary's earnings jumped sharply, yet the parent company's own bottom line barely moved. It's rarely fraud or hidden costs — it's usually the ordinary result of the parent not owning 100% of that subsidiary. Consolidated financial statements pull in a subsidiary's entire results regardless of ownership percentage, then carve out the slice that doesn't belong to the parent under a line called **non-controlling interest (NCI)**. This lesson covers why NCI arises and how it's calculated, and how it connects to the [holding company discount](/en/basics/holding-company-discount/) and [circular shareholding](/en/basics/circular-shareholding-explained/) structures common in Korean conglomerates.

## The Fork in the Road Is Control, Not Ownership Percentage Alone

When one company holds a stake in another, accounting splits the treatment into roughly three bands. Below 20%, it's a simple investment, with only dividends counted as income. Between 20% and 50% — enough for significant influence but not control — the **equity method** applies: only the investor's ownership share of the investee's net income is recognized as income. The pivotal band starts once ownership crosses 50%, or even below 50% if board composition or actual decision-making power shows the parent holds **control**. At that point the investee becomes a subsidiary and is folded into the parent's **consolidated financial statements**.

The mechanism is what matters here. Consolidation doesn't pull in "just my ownership share" the way the equity method does — it sums 100% of the subsidiary's revenue, expenses, assets, liabilities, and net income into the parent's statements, regardless of how much of it the parent actually owns. Own 60% of a subsidiary, and 100% of its revenue, assets, and liabilities still land on the parent's books. Only at the final step does the accounting split the subsidiary's net income and net assets into the portion belonging to the parent and the portion that doesn't — and that leftover portion is non-controlling interest. NCI isn't a "missing loss"; it's what's left over from adding everything in first and sorting out ownership afterward.

## Equity Method vs. Consolidation, Side by Side

The same economic stake can look completely different on paper depending on which side of the 50% line it falls on. Assume the investee earns $100 million in net income either way.

| | Equity method (40% stake) | Consolidation (60% stake) |
|---|---|---|
| How it's recorded | Only the ownership share | 100% summed, then split |
| Revenue/assets/liabilities | Not included (only equity-method income) | 100% fully consolidated |
| Income booked on investor's own statement | $40 million (equity-method income) | Full $100 million enters consolidated net income |
| Investor's actual share of that | $40 million | $60 million (controlling shareholders' net income) |
| Does NCI appear? | No | Yes ($40 million becomes NCI's share) |

The real economic split — 40% either way — is identical. What changes is how it's displayed: one clean equity-method income line versus two lines, consolidated net income and NCI's share. Watch for this presentation itself flipping when a stake crosses the 50% threshold.

## A Worked Example: Controlling vs. Non-Controlling Net Income

Say holding company A owns 60% of core affiliate B, and B earns $100 million in net income this year. A's consolidated income statement includes the full $100 million as "consolidated net income" — but not all of it belongs to A's shareholders. Accounting rules require splitting it into two lines.

| | Amount | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Consolidated net income (total) | $100 million | 100% of B's results |
| Net income attributable to owners of the parent | $60 million | A's 60% share |
| Net income attributable to non-controlling interests | $40 million | The other 40%, minority shareholders |

What matters to someone who owns A's stock is the $60 million, not the $100 million headline figure. If B's earnings grow 20% next year to $120 million, A's share grows to only $72 million — the same 20%, but on a smaller base. Headlines tend to emphasize "subsidiary profit up 20%"; the amount that should actually move A's valuation is that 60% slice, and the lower the ownership stake, the smaller the portion of any subsidiary improvement that ever reaches the parent's controlling net income.

## NCI Also Shows Up on the Balance Sheet

Non-controlling interest isn't confined to the income statement — it appears in equity too. If B's net assets total $500 million, the consolidated balance sheet includes the full $500 million, split into $300 million as "equity attributable to owners of the parent" (A's 60% share) and $200 million as "non-controlling interest." This matters directly for valuation: the denominator for P/B or book value per share should be equity attributable to the parent's owners, not total consolidated equity. Using total consolidated equity for a company with a large NCI balance overstates the equity actually available to shareholders, making the stock look cheaper than it is. Filing footnotes always break these two out separately — always use the parent-attributable figure.

## Dividends Split the Same Way

The effect extends to actual cash dividends. If B pays out $30 million of its $100 million profit as dividends, that $30 million splits by ownership percentage too: A receives $18 million (its 60% share) as dividend income, while the remaining $12 million goes directly to B's non-controlling shareholders. No matter how much B's profit or payout grows, the cash actually reaching A is capped at its ownership percentage from the start. This is central to estimating a holding company's own dividend capacity, since that capacity depends on dividends and brand fees flowing up from subsidiaries at the parent's own (unconsolidated) statement level — not on the consolidated profit figure.

## Ownership Changes Move the NCI Number

NCI isn't fixed once and forgotten — it shifts with every ownership transaction. If a parent buys more shares on the open market or participates in a subsidiary's capital raise to lift its stake from 60% to 80%, the equity and future income that were previously NCI shift toward the parent's side. Notably, under IFRS, buying more of an already-controlled subsidiary isn't treated as a new acquisition — it's booked as an **equity transaction**, so any gap between the price paid and NCI's book value adjusts equity directly rather than flowing through the income statement. The reverse also holds: selling down a stake increases NCI and shrinks the parent's future share of that subsidiary's profit. A disclosure about a group raising or trimming a subsidiary stake is worth reading as a signal about future profit allocation, not just about control.

## Why This Matters More in Korean Conglomerates

NCI is a universal accounting concept, but it's an especially useful signal inside Korean conglomerate structures. As covered in the [holding company discount](/en/basics/holding-company-discount/) lesson, many Korean groups keep control of a core affiliate with a stake well under 100% — often 30–70%. A lower stake means maintaining control with less capital, but it also means a larger share of that subsidiary's profit leaks out to non-controlling shareholders. This ties directly into the ownership-control gap discussed in [circular shareholding](/en/basics/circular-shareholding-explained/): control over the group can be large while the controlling shareholders' actual claim on profit stays capped at the ownership percentage. A sudden jump in NCI's share of consolidated profit is worth investigating — common causes include spinning off and separately listing a key subsidiary, acquiring a new subsidiary without full ownership, or dilution from a capital raise the parent didn't fully participate in.

## What to Check in the Filings

Start with the two lines at the bottom of the consolidated income statement: net income attributable to owners of the parent, and net income attributable to non-controlling interests. Always calculate P/E and EPS using the former, not total consolidated net income — check which figure a broker report or data provider's consensus is actually using. When the gap between the two is unusually wide, the subsidiary notes in the filing usually show which affiliate is driving it, along with that unit's own performance trend. The same logic applies to book value and P/B: use equity attributable to the parent's owners, excluding NCI, to get a number that reflects an actual shareholder's claim.

## Takeaways

- Non-controlling interest is the portion of a consolidated subsidiary's net income and net assets that doesn't belong to the parent, once ownership crosses the control threshold.
- Consolidation sums 100% of a subsidiary's results regardless of ownership stake, then splits the total between controlling and non-controlling shareholders at the final step.
- What matters to an investor is net income attributable to the parent's owners, not total consolidated net income — P/E, EPS, book value, and P/B should all be based on it.
- The lower a subsidiary's ownership stake, the smaller the share of that subsidiary's earnings growth that ever reaches the parent — worth tracking alongside any change in NCI's size.

## FAQ

### Is a large non-controlling interest balance itself a bad sign?
Not by itself — it simply reflects that the parent doesn't own 100% of a subsidiary, which isn't a sign of weakness on its own. A sudden jump in NCI is worth investigating (a subsidiary spin-off listing, a new partial acquisition, dilutive capital raise), but the size alone isn't a red flag.

### Should P/E be based on consolidated net income or controlling shareholders' net income?
Controlling shareholders' net income. Consolidated net income includes profit that belongs to non-controlling shareholders, so using it for P/E makes a stock look cheaper than it actually is. Most broker and data-provider EPS/P/E figures already use the parent-attributable number, but it's worth confirming which one you're looking at.

### What's the one-line difference between the equity method and consolidation?
The equity method brings in "only my ownership share" of the investee's income onto one line. Consolidation brings in 100% of the investee's results first, then carves out the portion that isn't the parent's as non-controlling interest. Whether the parent has control is what decides which method applies.
