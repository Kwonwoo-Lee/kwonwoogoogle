## What Happened

To understand how jarring this reversal was, start with what happened just one day earlier. On Wednesday, November 4, Wall Street rallied hard despite Democrats sweeping both the House and Senate in the midterms. The Dow Jones Industrial Average surged 1,307 points, or roughly 2.5%, its best single day since April, while the S&P 500 gained about 1.5-1.6% and the Nasdaq Composite jumped 1.7-1.8%. That was a complete reversal from the night before, when Nasdaq 100 futures had sunk nearly 2% as the election results came in. Markets read the Wednesday rally as relief that political uncertainty had cleared, and the Nasdaq 100 — which had briefly dipped into technical-correction territory (10%+ below its recent high) intraday on October 30 before closing just above that line — rode the rally further away from correction territory.

That relief lasted less than 24 hours. On Thursday, November 5, a broad selloff in semiconductor and AI-adjacent stocks sent the Nasdaq Composite down roughly 4%, with the S&P 500 off about 2-3%. The Dow held up comparatively well, supported by financials such as Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan Chase, and American Express. But the tech-heavy Nasdaq 100 fell far enough to close more than 10% below its October 29 record closing high — confirming, on a closing basis this time, its third technical correction of 2026. In other words, the index escaped correction territory on Wednesday and was fully back inside it by Thursday's close, a round trip of less than 24 hours.

The damage was sharpest at the single-stock level. AMD (Nasdaq: AMD) tumbled more than 13%, the steepest decline among major chip names — a striking outcome given that AMD had reported a blowout Q3 earnings beat just two days earlier, on November 3, with revenue, EPS, and Q4 guidance all topping Wall Street estimates. The stock that jumped more than 7% the day after that report gave back more than its entire post-earnings gain in a single session. Nvidia (Nasdaq: NVDA) fell as much as 7%, Intel (Nasdaq: INTC) dropped nearly 8%, and Broadcom (Nasdaq: AVGO) also slid sharply, with semiconductor-tracking indexes and ETFs losing anywhere from 7% to 12% on the day. Adding to the pressure, Qualcomm (Nasdaq: QCOM) fell roughly 9% after its own Wednesday-night earnings report showed a sharp handset-revenue decline and mixed guidance, compounding the sector-wide gloom.

## Why the Mood Flipped So Fast

The core explanation for Thursday's reversal is AI regulation risk. When Democrats won control of both chambers of Congress, markets initially bet that political uncertainty was now resolved and rallied. A day later, investors turned to the other side of that same coin: a Democratic-controlled Congress, paired with growing pushback from state officials in regions where AI data centers are concentrated, raises the odds of tighter rules or slower permitting for the data-center buildout that underpins the entire AI trade. Local opposition tied to rising electricity costs, water use, and land demands from data-center construction had already been a live issue heading into the election. The fear that this opposition could now translate into actual legislative or regulatory friction is what flipped Wednesday's "uncertainty resolved" narrative into Thursday's "regulation risk rising" narrative in less than a day.

AMD's move illustrates the mechanism most clearly. Its earnings were, by any conventional measure, excellent. That it still fell harder than almost every other chip stock shows that Thursday's selloff wasn't a verdict on AMD's results — it was a macro re-rating of the entire sector's growth assumptions. AI chip revenue ultimately depends on hyperscalers continuing to build out data centers at the current pace; when that underlying assumption comes under a cloud of regulatory doubt, even a company that just raised its own data-center GPU guidance (AMD's Helios platform ramp, in this case) can see its stock sold regardless of how strong the quarter actually was.

Qualcomm's own earnings miss made the timing worse. Qualcomm's roughly 9% drop was driven by a 26% decline in handset revenue and next-quarter EPS guidance that missed consensus — a company-specific issue unrelated to the broader AI-regulation story. But with two major chip names falling sharply on the same day for two different reasons, investors read it as evidence that "something is wrong across the whole semiconductor sector," a perception that dragged down names like Nvidia, Intel, and Broadcom that had no direct bad news of their own.

The Dow's relative resilience fits the same logic in reverse. The Dow is weighted toward financials, consumer, and industrial names with comparatively little exposure to the AI capex theme, while the Nasdaq 100 and S&P 500 carry far heavier weightings in AI-linked megacaps like Nvidia, AMD, Broadcom, and Microsoft. That's why the same day's news produced wildly different index-level reactions. This echoes a pattern seen earlier in the year, when Nvidia, AMD, Broadcom, and Meta all sold off sharply on one day while the equal-weight S&P 500 actually rose — a reminder that a "megacap AI selloff" and a "broad market decline" are not the same thing, even when headline index numbers suggest otherwise.

## What to Take Away From This

This episode is a vivid reminder that a rally can unwind within a single trading day. The exact same underlying fact — the midterm election outcome — was read by the market one way on Wednesday (uncertainty resolved) and the opposite way on Thursday (regulation risk rising). Treating the first day's reaction to any major news event as the final word is a mistake; it pays to stay open to which facet of a multi-sided story the market will focus on next.

The AMD case is also a useful lesson in separating an earnings beat from the stock's near-term price reaction. Even an excellent quarter can be overwhelmed by a macro re-rating of the entire sector it belongs to — in this case, AI regulation risk. The flip side is that this kind of sector-wide selloff can create a gap between a stock's price and its actual fundamentals, and identifying names whose underlying business wasn't actually impaired by the news that triggered the selloff is often where the next buying opportunity gets built.

Finally, it's worth remembering that "technical correction" describes a snapshot in time, not a permanent state. The Nasdaq 100 entered correction territory intraday on October 30, escaped it by Wednesday's close on November 4, and was back inside it by Thursday's close on November 5 — all within about a week. Whether an index is technically "in correction" matters far less than understanding whether the news driving that move reflects a structural problem or a fast-moving, still-unresolved political uncertainty.

## FAQ

### What exactly does a "technical correction" mean?
A correction is typically defined as a decline of 10% or more from a recent high (usually the most recent record closing price), while a drop of 20% or more is reclassified as a "bear market." The Nasdaq 100 briefly crossed that 10% threshold intraday on October 30 but closed just above it, then crossed it again on a closing basis on November 5.

### If AMD's earnings were so strong, why did the stock fall more than almost anything else?
Thursday's selloff wasn't a verdict on AMD's quarter — it was a broad re-rating of the AI chip sector's growth assumptions, driven by fears that tighter AI/data-center regulation under a Democratic Congress could slow the data-center buildout hyperscalers are expected to keep funding. AMD, as one of the stocks most closely tied to that growth story, absorbed an outsized share of that re-rating regardless of its own results.

### Does this mean more declines are coming?
That's not a given. The regulatory risk behind Thursday's drop hasn't been codified into any actual legislation or rule yet — it's the market repricing probabilities after an election result, not reacting to a confirmed policy change. The fact that the Dow held up far better than the Nasdaq suggests the selling was concentrated in the AI/semiconductor theme rather than reflecting broad economic distress, and theme-driven selloffs like this can reverse just as quickly if the regulatory outlook doesn't materialize as feared.
