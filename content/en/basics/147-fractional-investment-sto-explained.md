## "Own a Piece of a Building for $10" — What Are You Actually Buying?

Ads for buying a sliver of a hit song's royalty stream, a fraction of a Seoul office building, or a tiny stake in a painting have become common in Korea over the past few years. Platforms like Musicow (music royalty claims), Kasa (commercial real estate), and SOYOU (art and luxury goods) all sell what's broadly called **fractional investment** — "조각투자," literally "piece investing." The interesting question isn't how much you can earn from these products. It's what you're legally buying when you hand over money. The short answer: you don't get your name on a building's title registry, and you don't receive an actual copyright assignment. What you get is a **contractual claim** that the platform designed, and the legal fight over what that claim actually is reshaped a chunk of Korean securities regulation in 2022. This lesson draws the line between that kind of claim and the pooled-asset vehicles covered in [what is an ETF](/en/basics/etf-basics/), where you genuinely own a share of a fund's underlying holdings.

## What Fractional Investment Really Sells: A Claim on Performance, Not a Piece of the Asset

When you buy a stock or ETF through a brokerage account, that security is registered electronically in your own name through Korea's central depository system, and you hold the full bundle of legal shareholder rights — dividends, voting, pre-emptive rights on new shares. Fractional investment works differently. Take a music-royalty platform: it buys a portion of a songwriter's right to future royalty income, then slices that income stream into small denominations and sells investors a contractual right to a share of the payouts. You don't own the copyright — you've bought a promise that a portion of whatever royalty income the song generates will be paid to you. Real-estate fractional investment follows the same pattern: what you're buying isn't a deeded share of the building, but a beneficiary certificate issued by a trust or special-purpose vehicle (SPV) that holds the property — a right to a slice of the rental income and any eventual sale gain. Art and luxury-good platforms work similarly: you don't co-own the physical painting or watch; you hold a contractual claim to a share of whatever the platform eventually realizes when it resells the piece. The product is marketed as "splitting an asset," but what's actually being traded is a **claim on the asset's performance**, not the asset itself — and that distinction is the whole key to understanding this category.

## Comparing Asset Types: What You Get Varies by Platform

Fractional investment covers a range of underlying assets, and the legal label attached to the claim shifts slightly depending on what's being fractionalized.

| Asset type | Example platforms | What investors receive | Source of return |
|---|---|---|---|
| Music royalties | Musicow | Royalty participation claim ("music income security") | Actual royalty income the song earns |
| Real estate | Kasa, Lucentblock, Funble | Real-estate beneficiary certificate (trust interest) | Rental income plus any resale gain |
| Art and luxury goods | SOYOU, Tessa | Contractual co-investment claim | Resale price gain |

Across all three categories, the common thread is that investors never directly own the underlying physical asset — a platform, trust, or SPV holds it, and only the right to share in its economic performance gets sliced up and sold. Whichever platform you're looking at, the first thing worth checking in the offering documents is exactly how your claim is legally classified, and whether it went through the registration review required of a formal securities offering.

## Investment Contract Securities: If It Walks Like a Security, Regulators Treat It Like One

Whether these designed claims actually fall under financial regulation comes down to a concept called an **investment contract security**. Korea's Capital Markets Act doesn't limit "securities" to fixed categories like stocks and bonds. It uses a catch-all principle that also captures any contractual right where an investor puts money into a joint undertaking and expects to receive profit that depends mainly on the efforts of someone else running that undertaking. If your money goes into a common enterprise and your return hinges on the platform's performance rather than your own effort, the product can legally be classified as a security — an investment contract security — no matter what it's branded as. The idea tracks closely with the Howey test used in U.S. securities law, which asks whether there's an investment of money, in a common enterprise, with an expectation of profit, derived from the efforts of others. Once something is classified as a security under either framework, issuers face registration-statement requirements, mandatory disclosure, and rules segregating investor funds from the platform's own operating cash. Before that classification is applied, none of those protections are guaranteed — and that gap was exactly what fueled Korea's biggest fractional-investment controversy.

## The Musicow Case: Where the Theory Became a Market-Changing Ruling

In April 2022, Korea's Securities and Futures Commission ruled that Musicow's royalty participation claims were investment contract securities under the Capital Markets Act. The reasoning: investor money funded Musicow's joint business of acquiring and managing music royalties, and returns depended on how well Musicow ran that business, not on anything the investor did. Rather than shutting Musicow down outright for having sold unregistered securities, regulators suspended sanctions on the condition that Musicow build real investor protections — segregating investor funds in the platform's own operating accounts, and structurally separating primary issuance from secondary trading. Musicow subsequently partnered with a securities firm to hold investor deposits in segregated, real-name accounts and later issued products backed by a formal registration statement. The ruling mattered well beyond one platform: it put Korean regulators on record that a fractional-investment product can be a security in substance, regardless of its marketing, and pushed similar real-estate and art platforms to review their own structures against the same standard.

## Not the Same as Fractional Shares

It's worth separating this from something that sounds similar: buying 0.1 of a share through a brokerage's fractional-share feature, or holding more shares after a [stock split](/en/basics/stock-splits-and-buybacks/). Those 0.1 shares are smaller in dollar terms, but they're still genuine shares of a listed, exchange-traded security, carrying the full proportional bundle of shareholder rights and exchange-level liquidity. A fractional-investment claim is different: it typically isn't listed on any public exchange at all and only trades, if it trades, inside the platform's own internal matching system. Whether you can sell and when depends entirely on how the underlying asset performs and how the platform or SPV is run. Same marketing pitch — "buy a small slice" — but very different legal protection and liquidity underneath.

## Three Things to Check Before You Invest

**Liquidity first.** These claims don't trade on a regulated exchange like the Korea Exchange; they trade, if at all, through a platform-run internal matching system with no guarantee a buyer exists when you want to sell. The same structural tension covered in [fund redemption suspensions](/en/basics/fund-redemption-suspension-liquidity-mismatch/) applies here: the underlying asset (a building, a royalty stream) isn't easily converted to cash on demand, while investors expect to trade freely. **Second, there's no deposit insurance or principal guarantee.** These aren't bank deposits, and if the underlying asset underperforms or the platform or SPV runs into financial trouble, you can lose your entire investment. **Third, check where your claim ranks if the platform fails.** Whether investor funds sit in a trust or SPV legally separated from the platform's own assets — versus commingled with the platform's general assets — determines whether you have any real priority claim in a bankruptcy. The segregation requirements imposed after the Musicow ruling exist precisely to address this gap. Reading how investor funds are legally held matters more than the advertised expected return.

## Security Tokens (STO): Bringing Fractional Investment Into the Regulated System

The controversy around fractional investment eventually pushed toward a bigger structural fix: formally bringing these products inside securities regulation through **security tokens (STO)**. Korea's Financial Services Commission issued guidelines in 2023 allowing investment contract securities and similar instruments to be issued and traded using distributed-ledger (blockchain) technology, with amendments to the Electronic Securities Act and Capital Markets Act introduced in the National Assembly to make that framework law. The core idea is to formally recognize distributed-ledger records as valid electronic securities and open a path for investment contract securities to be traded through licensed brokerages, something that wasn't previously permitted. Reporting indicates the relevant legislation advanced through the National Assembly in 2026, but the detailed implementing regulations and supervisory rules were still being finalized as this lesson was written, so the exact rollout timeline remains worth tracking rather than treating as settled. In the meantime, some fractional-investment platforms operated under temporary "innovative financial service" regulatory-sandbox exemptions, and at least one wound down or restructured when its sandbox period ended before full legislation took effect. The direction matters more than any single date: once the STO framework is fully in place, fractional-investment products should move toward issuance, disclosure, and trading standards closer to ordinary regulated securities — which is a reason for more structural confidence, not a reason to assume any given product is automatically safe.

## Key Takeaways

- Fractional investment markets itself as "splitting an asset," but what you actually buy is a contractual claim on that asset's performance, held through a platform, trust, or SPV — not direct ownership of the underlying royalty stream, building, or artwork.
- Under Korea's catch-all securities principle, such claims can be classified as investment contract securities, which triggers registration and disclosure obligations once that classification applies.
- The 2022 Musicow ruling was the moment Korean regulators formally established that fractional-investment products can be securities in substance, prompting broader investor-protection reforms across the industry.
- Unlike fractional shares bought through a brokerage or shares split via a stock split, fractional-investment claims aren't listed securities and carry materially different liquidity and legal protection.
- Before investing, check liquidity (can you actually sell when you want to), the absence of deposit insurance or principal guarantees, and how your claim ranks if the platform fails.
- Security token (STO) regulation aims to bring fractional investment into a more standardized securities framework, but the implementation timeline is still unfolding and worth following rather than assuming is finished.

## FAQ

### Is fractional investment covered by deposit insurance?
No. Unlike a bank deposit, fractional-investment products aren't covered by deposit insurance, and investors can lose their entire principal if the underlying asset underperforms or the platform or SPV becomes insolvent.

### How are gains from fractional investment taxed?
Treatment varies by product structure and how tax authorities classify it. Some art and livestock-based fractional products reportedly moved to dividend-income tax treatment starting in 2025. Confirm the exact rate and classification with the specific platform's disclosure documents and current tax guidance before investing.

### What happens to my money if the platform shuts down?
It depends almost entirely on whether investor funds and the underlying asset are held in a trust or SPV legally segregated from the platform's own operating assets. Where segregation is properly in place, investor claims have a better chance of being protected independently of the platform's own troubles; where it isn't, investors may end up sharing losses alongside the platform's ordinary creditors.
