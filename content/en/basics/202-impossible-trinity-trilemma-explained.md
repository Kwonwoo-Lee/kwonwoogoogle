## Why You Can't Have All Three

Imagine running a central bank. You'd want three things at once: a stable, pegged exchange rate; free movement of foreign capital in and out of your economy; and the freedom to set interest rates purely to match domestic conditions. Can you actually have all three? International finance theory gives a flat answer: no. The proposition, built on work by economists Robert Mundell and Marcus Fleming, is known as the **Impossible Trinity**, or simply the **trilemma**. A country can hold at most two of ① a fixed exchange rate, ② free capital mobility, and ③ independent monetary policy — and it must give up the third. This isn't an abstract academic curiosity. It's the single principle that explains why Hong Kong can't set its own interest rate, why China restricts how freely money crosses its borders, and why South Korea's central bank watches the Federal Reserve's every move so closely. This lesson walks through why the three can't coexist, and how different countries actually choose which two to keep.

## The Three Corners: Exchange-Rate Stability, Capital Freedom, Policy Independence

Let's define each corner. First, a **fixed (or stable) exchange rate** pins a currency's value to a specific level or a narrow band. For businesses planning trade and investment, not having to worry about currency swings is itself a major advantage. Second, **free capital mobility** means foreign investors can buy and sell domestic assets freely, and domestic residents can invest abroad freely — nothing blocks money from crossing the border in either direction. This is the foundation of attracting foreign investment and opening up financial markets. Third, **independent monetary policy** means the central bank can set its policy rate based purely on domestic conditions — inflation, employment — without being tied to what any other country's central bank is doing. Nearly every country wants all three: a stable currency, continued foreign investment, and the freedom to raise or cut rates to match its own business cycle. The problem is that these three goals contain a structural contradiction that makes simultaneous pursuit of all three mathematically unsustainable.

## The Mechanism: Rate Gaps Move Capital, and Capital Flight Breaks the Peg

A simple thought experiment shows why. Suppose Country A pegs its currency to the dollar (①), keeps capital flows open (②), and then — responding to a domestic slowdown — cuts its policy rate below the US rate (③). Investors now have a clear incentive to move out of Country A's currency and into higher-yielding dollar assets. Because capital is free to move, that money actually leaves. The resulting sell orders for Country A's currency in the FX market push directly against the peg the government is trying to defend. Defending it requires the central bank to spend its foreign-exchange reserves buying back its own currency — and those reserves are finite. If the rate gap persists, reserves keep draining, and speculators can target the exact moment those reserves run dry. As covered in [what causes a currency crisis](/en/basics/currency-crisis-speculative-attack-explained/), the central bank eventually has to give up one of the three. Raise rates back to US levels to stop the outflow, and you sacrifice monetary independence. Let the peg break and the currency fall, and you sacrifice exchange-rate stability. Impose capital controls to stop money from leaving, and you sacrifice free capital mobility. Whichever path is chosen, one of the three original goals is gone — which is exactly why the trilemma is described as a binding constraint, not a menu of options.

## The Three Corners in Practice: Hong Kong, China, and Korea

The theory becomes concrete when you compare three places that each chose a different pair. **Hong Kong** picked exchange-rate stability and free capital mobility, giving up monetary independence. Since 1983, the Hong Kong dollar has traded within a 7.75–7.85 band against the US dollar under a currency-board system, where every Hong Kong dollar in circulation is backed by US dollar assets held in reserve. In exchange, the Hong Kong Monetary Authority doesn't set its own policy rate independently — it's typically set by a formula pegged roughly 50 basis points above the lower bound of the Fed funds rate, which is why, reportedly, Hong Kong cut rates in close lockstep with the Fed through late 2025. **China** chose the opposite pair. The People's Bank of China sets domestic interest rates largely independent of the Fed, while keeping the yuan's exchange rate managed rather than fully free-floating. That independence is possible because of capital controls restricting money from freely crossing China's borders — though analysts increasingly note that cross-border lending and investment channels have made those controls leakier than they used to be. **South Korea, the US, and Japan** sit at the third corner: they allow free capital flows and set domestic rates independently, giving up exchange-rate stability instead. When rate gaps widen, the exchange rate simply absorbs the pressure through currency swings rather than reserve depletion. This is also the backdrop behind the [yen carry trade](/en/basics/yen-carry-trade-explained/), where capital borrowed cheaply in one floating currency flows into higher-yielding assets precisely because nothing blocks that flow and the exchange rate is left to do the adjusting.

## A Historical Lesson: The UK's 1992 Black Wednesday

The most frequently cited cautionary tale is the United Kingdom in 1992. Britain was pegging the pound to the German mark within a narrow band under the European Exchange Rate Mechanism (ERM) while keeping capital flows open. The trouble: Britain's economy was in recession and wanted lower rates, but defending the peg against a Germany fighting post-unification inflation with high rates required the opposite — higher rates. Markets concluded Britain couldn't sustain that contradiction for long, and a wave of speculative selling against the pound followed. The Bank of England bought pounds and raised rates twice in a single day, from 10% to 15%, but couldn't stem the outflow. On September 16, 1992 — "Black Wednesday" — Britain withdrew from the ERM, and the pound fell roughly 19% against the dollar by year-end. George Soros's Quantum Fund is widely reported to have profited around $1 billion betting against the pound, though many observers argue the broader market reaching the same conclusion mattered more than any single trader's position. Britain's outcome was clear: rather than keep defending both the peg and open capital flows, it gave up exchange-rate stability to reclaim independent monetary policy.

## Where Korea Stands Today: The Korea-US Rate Gap and the Won

Even a floating-rate country like South Korea isn't entirely free of trilemma pressure — letting the exchange rate absorb rate differentials doesn't mean that movement is unlimited or costless. Reports suggest the Korea-US policy rate gap widened to a record roughly 2 percentage points at one point in 2025, before a series of Fed rate cuts narrowed it to around 1.25 points — reportedly the narrowest since February 2023. The Bank of Korea cut its rate once in May 2025 and then held for an extended stretch, with financial-press reports citing concern that a further cut would widen the gap with the Fed and add pressure on the won. By early 2026, with the won reportedly trading in the upper-1,400s per dollar, the BOK governor was reported to have signaled the easing cycle might be winding down, citing won weakness as a key factor. This isn't the textbook trilemma in its purest form — Korea doesn't officially peg its exchange rate, so it formally sits in the "gave up exchange-rate stability" camp. But because sharp or one-directional currency moves still threaten price stability and financial stability, the BOK has to balance "set rates purely on domestic conditions" against a real-world constraint that it can't fully ignore exchange-rate and rate-gap dynamics. Tools like the [currency swap lines](/en/basics/currency-swap-crs-explained/) Korea holds with the US don't eliminate this pressure — they function as a buffer, providing emergency dollar liquidity during sudden capital outflows so the central bank has more time to choose its response than it did when reserves alone had to carry the defense in 1997. The trilemma, in other words, isn't a binary switch that only applies to countries with hard pegs — it's a background pressure that weighs on every central bank's rate decisions, in varying degrees, depending on how exposed that country is to cross-border capital flows.

## Key Takeaways

- The Impossible Trinity holds that a country can keep at most two of three: ① a fixed exchange rate, ② free capital mobility, and ③ independent monetary policy.
- Cutting rates independently while keeping both a peg and open capital flows invites capital outflows that drain FX reserves, eventually forcing the country to abandon the peg or give up monetary independence.
- Hong Kong gave up monetary independence to keep exchange-rate stability and open capital; China restricts capital flows to keep monetary independence; floating-rate countries like Korea, the US, and Japan give up exchange-rate stability to keep the other two.
- The UK's 1992 Black Wednesday is the classic case of a country abandoning its exchange-rate peg rather than sacrificing monetary independence any longer.
- Even floating-rate Korea isn't fully exempt — a widening Korea-US rate gap creates real won-weakness and capital-outflow concerns that factor into actual rate decisions.

## FAQ

### Is any country completely free of the trilemma?
A country that fully floats its currency and fully opens its capital account is, in theory, free to combine capital mobility and monetary independence. But because large currency swings still create real costs for inflation and financial stability, even fully floating countries tend to compromise somewhat between pure free-float and pure rate independence rather than operating at either extreme.

### Does China's capital control fully shield it from the trilemma?
Officially, yes, but not perfectly. Money moves through trade settlement, outbound investment channels, and various financial products that create leakage around formal controls. Tighter controls reduce trilemma pressure but don't eliminate it entirely.

### How much does the Bank of Korea actually weigh the exchange rate when setting rates?
The BOK officially targets price stability and financial stability, not the exchange rate itself. But because currency moves feed through into import prices and foreign capital flows, which in turn affect both of those official targets, the BOK is widely understood to track the exchange rate and the Korea-US rate gap as a key input alongside its formal mandate.

### How did eurozone countries get around the trilemma?
They didn't get around it — they restructured the choice. By adopting a single shared currency, eurozone members eliminated exchange-rate fluctuations between member states entirely, but in doing so each country gave up having its own independent monetary policy, handing rate-setting power to the European Central Bank instead.
