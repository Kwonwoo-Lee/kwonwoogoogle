## What Happened

On Thursday, November 5, Wall Street was gripped by a historic selloff as it became clear Democrats had swept both chambers of Congress in the U.S. midterm elections, reviving fears of tighter regulation on the AI industry. The Nasdaq Composite tumbled 5.52% that day, and the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index (SOX) dropped somewhere between 9% and 12% depending on the measure. But within that broad chip-sector rout, two of the biggest AI-chip names moved by strikingly different magnitudes. AMD (NASDAQ: AMD) closed down 17.26% at $404.40, while Broadcom (NASDAQ: AVGO) fell 10.43% to $291.53. Both stocks are grouped under the same "AI semiconductor" label and reacted to the identical piece of macro news on the identical day - yet one fell nearly twice as hard as the other.

The selloff's trigger was political. With Democrats taking both the House and Senate, President Trump will spend the final two years of his term facing an opposition-controlled Congress. Wall Street strategists immediately flagged the possibility of congressional hearings, investigations, and tighter oversight aimed squarely at the AI and data-center buildout. Democratic lawmakers have already pushed for consumer-protection legislation to curb data-center-driven electricity price hikes and for mandatory independent testing of AI systems. In states like Texas, where Democrats flipped the governorship and the state House, some strategists - including those at UBS - warned that local-level halts to data-center construction could directly weigh on the growth outlook for AI infrastructure and cloud computing names.

For AMD, the drop had nothing to do with its own numbers. If anything, the timing was almost perverse: AMD had reported third-quarter earnings just two days earlier, on November 3, posting record revenue of $13.38 billion, up 45% year over year and well above the roughly $13.06 billion analysts had expected. The results themselves were about as clean a beat as a chipmaker could deliver. And yet the stock still shed more than 17% once the election outcome hit screens. CEO Lisa Su pushed back in an interview that same day, calling the market's reaction to AI "short-sighted." Citi analyst Atif Malik, meanwhile, kept AMD as a top pick, arguing the underlying AI business momentum remained fully intact. AMD even clawed back 3.38% in after-hours trading to $418.05.

Broadcom fell on the same day too, but its decline came in at roughly 60% of AMD's magnitude. Like AMD, Broadcom had no company-specific news that day - analysts largely described its drop as a sector-wide valuation reset or broad de-risking tied to the regulatory headlines, rather than anything specific to Broadcom's own business. Broadcom's next earnings report isn't due until December 9, so its move on November 5 looked far more like pure "sector beta" - getting dragged down by the group - than a reaction to anything the company itself had done.

## Why the Same Shock Produced Nearly Double the Damage

The first reason is revenue concentration. AMD's business is heavily weighted toward pure semiconductor hardware - AI accelerators and high-performance CPUs - which leaves it more directly exposed when fears about AI capex slowdowns or new regulation surface. Broadcom, by contrast, spreads its revenue across custom AI chips (ASICs), networking equipment, broadband, and the enterprise software business it picked up through the VMware acquisition. Broadcom had also already guided to $58 billion in AI revenue for this fiscal year with a stated path to $115 billion next fiscal year, giving investors a clearer anchor of conviction to hold onto when the broader tape turned ugly.

The second reason is how much optimism was already priced in. AMD's stock had run up sharply through the year on AI momentum, meaning a large share of future growth was already baked into the price before the selloff. Stocks priced for perfection tend to see far more violent round-trips when any negative catalyst - even one unrelated to their own fundamentals - hits the tape. Several analyst notes flagged AMD's valuation multiple as running well above the semiconductor sector average heading into the drop. Broadcom, while not cheap either, carries a heavier weighting in major indexes and a larger base of institutional ownership, which tends to provide more stable buy-side support during a panic.

The third reason is AMD's distinct exposure to export-control risk. AMD's high-end AI accelerators (the MI series) have previously been directly caught up in U.S. export restrictions on advanced chips to China. With Democrats about to take control of Congress, fears that China-facing tech export policy could tighten further landed more heavily on a stock already sensitive to swings in China-related revenue. Broadcom's custom AI silicon business, built on long-term supply agreements with a handful of major cloud customers like Google, carries comparatively less direct geopolitical sensitivity.

A fourth factor is options-market feedback. Stocks with unusually heavy derivatives trading can see forced hedging activity (dealer gamma hedging) amplify a selloff once prices start moving sharply in one direction. AMD is consistently one of the most actively traded options names on the market, and that derivatives positioning may have added extra downward pressure on top of the fundamental selling.

## What to Take Away From This

- **Revenue concentration determines how a stock absorbs an external shock.** A company like AMD, with revenue concentrated in a single product category (AI accelerators), tends to react far more violently to news about that category. A more diversified name like Broadcom tends to move more moderately under the same pressure. When picking a stock within a hot theme, check what share of revenue actually comes from that theme - not just whether the label fits.
- **A strong earnings beat doesn't always protect a stock.** AMD posted record revenue and still fell more than 17% two days later. When the selling is driven by a macro or political event rather than company fundamentals, even excellent results may not shield the stock in the short run.
- **The more optimism priced into a stock, the sharper the round-trip when sentiment turns.** Stocks that have run up heavily on narrative tend to fall asymmetrically hard on bad news. If you're holding a stock after a big run, it's worth separating how much of that gain reflects actual earnings improvement versus a "narrative premium" that can evaporate quickly.
- **Two stocks that fall together don't necessarily recover together.** When an entire sector gets sold off at once, differences in diversification, export-control exposure, and options positioning mean individual names can bounce back at very different speeds. After a panic selloff, it pays to re-examine why each stock fell rather than assuming the whole group will move in lockstep going forward.

## FAQ

### Which stock did analysts see as the better buying opportunity after the drop?
Citi analyst Atif Malik kept AMD as a top pick immediately after the selloff, arguing its AI business momentum remained intact. That's one analyst's view, not a market-wide consensus, so it shouldn't be treated as the final word - investors should weigh multiple sources before making their own call.

### Is it likely that this election result actually leads to new AI regulation?
As of now, no specific bill or executive action has been finalized - what strategists have flagged is possibility and concern, not confirmed policy. There's also a counterargument: the president retains veto power, which limits how far Congress can push regulation unilaterally. The real regulatory outcome will depend on the legislative process over the coming months.

### What exactly is AMD's China export-control risk?
AMD's high-end AI accelerators (the MI series) have previously been subject to U.S. export restrictions limiting sales to China. With the incoming Congress likely to take a different approach to China tech policy, investors are pricing in the possibility that these restrictions could tighten further. This is a market expectation reflected in the stock price, not a policy change that has actually been enacted.
