## Dilution With No Headline Attached

When most people picture a company raising equity, they picture a single announcement: a fixed price, a fixed number of new shares, and a stock price that reacts sharply the next trading day. But a large number of U.S.-listed companies raise money a completely different way — through a broker, selling new shares into the open market at whatever price the stock happens to be trading at, almost continuously, with no single headline event at all. This is called an **at-the-market (ATM) offering**. It's also why investors sometimes open a quarterly filing and discover, almost by accident, that the share count has quietly crept up over the past three months. As more retail investors trade U.S. stocks directly — especially in sectors like biotech and small-cap growth where ATM programs are routine — understanding this mechanism matters, because without it, a steadily rising share count can look mysterious or even alarming when it's actually a disclosed, ongoing financing tool. This lesson covers what an ATM offering is, how it differs structurally from a traditional dilutive offering, and how investors can actually spot one happening.

## What an ATM Offering Is: Selling Into the Market Instead of Pricing a Deal

An ATM offering lets a public company raise capital by selling newly issued shares gradually, through a designated broker-dealer (the sales agent), at the prevailing market price on the exchange — rather than fixing a price and share count for a single transaction up front. A traditional [follow-on or rights-style offering](/en/basics/rights-offering-and-bonus-issue/) is a one-time event: a specific number of shares at a specific, usually discounted, price. An ATM program, by contrast, is an open-ended facility that can run for months or years, through which the company can sell exactly as much as it needs, exactly when it needs it. Because shares sell at whatever price the market happens to offer that day, there's no single discount-driven shock to absorb. That's precisely why companies favor ATMs over large block follow-ons: spreading sales out over many small increments avoids the price impact of dumping a large block all at once, and the company can simply pause sales entirely on a day when the stock price looks unattractive.

## The Mechanics: Shelf Registration and a Standing Sales Agreement

An ATM program depends on a **shelf registration** filed with the SEC on **Form S-3**, which lets a company register in advance the maximum amount of securities it might sell over a set period (typically three years), then actually issue shares in pieces, whenever convenient, within that ceiling. Building on that shelf, the company signs an **equity distribution agreement** with one or more investment banks and discloses the terms in a **424B5 prospectus supplement**. From there, on any trading day, the company can instruct its sales agent — for example, "only sell above this price" — and the agent feeds new shares into the regular order flow at the market price. Unlike a traditional offering, the company can simply tell the agent not to sell anything on a given day, giving it real-time control that a one-shot follow-on doesn't offer. Size matters here, though. A large, well-known company with a public float above $75 million — a "well-known seasoned issuer" — can use the full, unlimited shelf. Smaller companies fall under the so-called **baby shelf rule**, which caps sales at one-third of public float over any 12-month period. This is a big part of why early-stage biotech companies, which tend to have smaller floats and burn cash quickly, are some of the heaviest ATM users: they pace their draws to stay inside that one-third ceiling. The SEC has discussed proposals to eliminate the baby shelf cap altogether, but as of this writing that change has not been finalized, so investors should check each company's own disclosures for the limit currently in force.

## Why Companies and Banks Both Like the Cost Structure

Part of the appeal is fees. A traditional underwritten offering has the bank take on the full block and the resale risk, and charges accordingly — typically 5–7% of proceeds. An ATM sales agent takes on no inventory risk; it simply routes shares into existing market demand, so fees typically run 1–3% of proceeds. For the company, that's effectively a standing line of credit it can draw on for years at a lower cost. For the bank, it's a steady stream of agency fees without underwriting risk. That cost efficiency is exactly why ATMs are most popular with companies that have an ongoing, moderate need for cash rather than a single large funding event.

## ATM Isn't Automatically a Red Flag: The REIT Example

The Bed Bath & Beyond case below might suggest ATM programs are a last resort for struggling companies, but the sector that uses ATMs most heavily and most routinely is actually **REITs** — companies with stable, predictable rental income. Industry data shows U.S. REITs raised roughly $23.1 billion through ATM programs in 2024 alone, following about $19.6 billion in 2023. Because REITs need to keep buying properties to grow and rely on steady cash flows rather than one giant capital raise, drawing smaller amounts through an ATM program as deals come up, matched to the timing of each acquisition, is simply efficient capital planning. In other words, the tool itself carries no verdict — what matters is whether a company is using it to fund growth or because it has run out of other options.

## A Real Case: Bed Bath & Beyond's Fight for Survival

Bed Bath & Beyond's 2022–2023 fundraising shows the opposite end of the spectrum. Facing a liquidity crisis, the retailer launched a $150 million ATM program in October 2022 — a sum large enough, relative to its market cap at the time, that analysts estimated it could dilute existing shareholders by roughly 41%. As the cash crunch deepened, the company announced another ATM program of up to $300 million in March 2023, stating plainly that if the offering wasn't fully completed, bankruptcy would likely follow. For this company, the ATM program wasn't an elegant way to smooth out price impact — it was close to the last lever left to pull. Bed Bath & Beyond filed for bankruptcy in April 2023 anyway. The lesson isn't that ATMs are dangerous; it's that the same neutral tool reads completely differently depending on whether a company is funding expansion or funding survival.

## What Investors Can Actually Check

Because ATM programs run without a single headline announcement, spotting one requires checking specific filings. Any new or expanded ATM program must be disclosed in a **424B5 prospectus supplement**, which reveals the program's existence and ceiling before a single share is sold under it. To see how much has actually been drawn, look at the cash flow statement in the quarterly (10-Q) or annual (10-K) report for a line item like "proceeds from at-the-market offering," and track how the share count outstanding changes quarter to quarter. Since a buyback moves the share count in the opposite direction, comparing both disclosures side by side in the same period tells you whether a company is currently leaning toward returning capital to shareholders or raising it from them.

## Key Takeaways

- An ATM offering lets a company sell new shares gradually, through a sales agent, at the prevailing exchange price, rather than pricing one discounted block all at once.
- Shelf registration (Form S-3) and a standing sales agency agreement create the ongoing facility; companies with a smaller public float are capped at selling one-third of that float in any 12-month period under the baby shelf rule.
- Dilution from a traditional offering shows up in a single disclosure; ATM dilution accumulates quietly and tends to become visible only once a quarterly report shows the change in share count.
- The same tool reads very differently depending on intent — REITs use ATMs routinely to fund ordinary growth, while Bed Bath & Beyond used one as a last-ditch survival measure before bankruptcy.
- Checking 424B5 filings alongside the proceeds and share-count lines in 10-Q/10-K reports lets investors track an ATM program that otherwise generates no headline at all.

## FAQ

### Can companies listed in Korea use an ATM-style offering?
Korean capital markets law categorizes capital raises as shareholder-allocated, general public, or third-party-allocated offerings, and there isn't a standard, separately named mechanism that lets a company drip-sell new shares at market price through a standing sales agent the way U.S. ATM programs work. Korean investors most commonly encounter the term "ATM offering" when investing directly in U.S.-listed companies, particularly smaller biotech and growth names.

### How is an ATM offering different from a block deal?
A [block deal](/en/basics/block-deal-explained/) is typically existing shares — often held by a large shareholder or institution — changing hands in a single off-exchange transaction at a discount; no new shares are created and the company usually receives no new cash, since it's existing stock trading hands rather than new issuance. An ATM offering, by contrast, issues brand-new shares and sends the proceeds directly into the company's own treasury, which is what actually dilutes existing shareholders' ownership percentage.

### Does having an ATM program in place mean a company is in trouble?
Not necessarily. An ATM is simply one financing tool among many, and filing a shelf registration or opening a program doesn't by itself mean a company is in crisis — plenty of healthy growth companies keep an ATM facility open in advance, just to have the option available when they need it. What actually matters is how much the company draws, how often, and for what purpose, which requires checking the 424B5 filings and financial statements described above.
