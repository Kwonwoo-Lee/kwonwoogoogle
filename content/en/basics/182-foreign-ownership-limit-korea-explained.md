## August 2022: SK Telecom Quietly Disappears From an MSCI Index

In August 2022, when MSCI published the results of its semiannual index review, SK Telecom was removed from the MSCI Emerging Markets Index. Nothing had gone wrong with the business. Earnings hadn't collapsed, there was no accounting scandal, and operations carried on as usual. The company was dropped for exactly one reason: foreign investors had almost completely filled the legal ceiling on how much of the stock they're allowed to own. Global passive funds that track MSCI indexes had no choice but to sell their entire SK Telecom position on the effective date, and the market immediately worried about a wave of mechanical foreign selling pressure. This episode is the clearest real-world illustration of a feature unique to the Korean stock market: a **statutory foreign ownership limit**. This lesson covers why these caps still exist, what the "foreign room ratio" actually measures, and what concretely happens in the market once that number fills up.

## What the Limit Is: The Barrier Korea Lifted in 1998 — and Where It Didn't

Korea opened its stock market to foreign investors gradually starting in 1992, and after the 1997 Asian financial crisis, scrapped foreign investment ceilings almost entirely across the board in 1998. Today, a foreign investor can buy as much of nearly any KOSPI or KOSDAQ stock as they want, with no legal restriction at all. A handful of sectors were carved out as exceptions, though. The Telecommunications Business Act caps foreign ownership of facilities-based carriers — SK Telecom, KT, and LG Uplus — at 49%. The Broadcasting Act bans foreign ownership of terrestrial broadcasters entirely: not a single share. The Aviation Business Act sets a 49.99% ceiling for Korea's flag carriers, and breaching it can affect the airline's international route allocations. A handful of public-utility-adjacent names, such as KEPCO, carry their own statutory caps as well. The logic behind all of these is the same: telecom networks, broadcasting, and air transport are treated as national infrastructure tied directly to security and the formation of public opinion, so the law simply forecloses the possibility of foreign capital taking control. The government floated reconsidering these caps in 2023, but the Ministry of Economy and Finance explicitly denied that it was actually considering lifting or raising them. In other words, this is a structural feature that has stayed largely unchanged for decades.

## The Foreign Room Ratio: One Number That Tells You How Full the Cap Is

For a capped stock, the number that actually matters day to day isn't the cap itself — it's the **foreign room (exhaustion) ratio**: the share of the legal maximum that foreign investors currently hold. Say a telecom carrier's cap is 49%, and foreigners currently hold 44.1% of the company. The exhaustion ratio is 44.1 ÷ 49 = 90%. That 90% doesn't mean "90% of the whole company is foreign-owned" — it means "90% of the 49% bucket is filled." The inverse concept, how much buying headroom is left, is called **foreign room**. Korean brokerage trading platforms even have a dedicated screen ranking stocks by this ratio, which tells you how routinely investors in capped names are expected to track it.

## What Happens When the Room Runs Out: Blocked Orders and Suspended Voting Rights

As the exhaustion ratio approaches 100%, the first visible effect is that the exchange's own systems stop accepting new foreign buy orders on that stock — the order simply doesn't execute. This produces a strange paradox: all else equal, heavy foreign demand should make a stock more sought-after, but a fully capped stock becomes one foreign investors want to buy and physically can't, leaving it sidelined even during a foreign-led rally. A subtler problem shows up on the share-count side. If a company buys back and retires treasury shares, total shares outstanding shrinks — and foreign investors' ownership *percentage* rises automatically even though the number of shares they hold hasn't changed. If that pushes their stake past the legal cap, voting rights on the excess portion are automatically suspended, and the company is ordered to bring the ratio back under the cap, typically within six months. To put numbers on it: imagine a carrier with 10 million shares outstanding, 4.8 million of them (48%) held by foreign investors, that buys back and retires 1 million shares. The foreign-held share count stays at 4.8 million, but shares outstanding drops to 9 million, pushing the foreign ownership ratio to 53.3% — past the 49% cap. Voting rights on that excess 4.3 percentage points are suspended, and the company is ordered to bring the ratio back down within roughly six months. A buyback meant as shareholder-friendly capital return can end up triggering an entirely different statute.

## MSCI Builds This Number Directly Into Index Weights

To understand why SK Telecom was dropped from the MSCI EM Index, you need to know about the **Foreign Inclusion Factor (FIF)**, an adjustment MSCI applies when constructing its indexes. For every stock, MSCI calculates how much foreign room is actually left, and the less room there is, the more it discounts that stock's index weight. If [Korea's MSCI Developed Market classification](/en/basics/msci-developed-market-classification-korea/) is about whether the whole market is accessible enough, FIF is about whether an *individual stock* still has real buying capacity left. Once a stock's foreign room is close to exhausted, global passive funds tracking the index can no longer accumulate it at the index-prescribed weight, so MSCI cuts that weight or drops the stock outright. SK Telecom's foreign ownership was approaching the statutory 49% cap at the time, foreign room had nearly run dry, and the stock was removed entirely. Because passive funds operate by replicating the index exactly, they were forced to sell the position in bulk on the effective date — selling driven entirely by index mechanics, with nothing to do with the company's earnings or outlook. The general mechanics of how index additions and removals move prices are covered in [index inclusion and exclusion effects](/en/basics/index-inclusion-exclusion-effect/).

## Why Foreign Flows Behave Differently in These Names

A capped stock layers one more variable on top of the ordinary dynamics covered in [exchange rates and foreign investor flows](/en/basics/exchange-rate-foreign-investor-flows/). An uncapped stock's foreign flows are driven by earnings outlook and currency expectations alone; a capped stock adds the physical constraint of "wanting to buy but finding the bucket full." When the exhaustion ratio is low, foreign capital moves in and out relatively freely. As it climbs past 90% toward 100%, buying room narrows, trading thins out, and competition to buy whatever a departing foreign holder sells can actually intensify. Because a near-fully-capped stock is one foreign investors can't freely enter or exit on their own schedule, some analysts treat that constraint itself as a liquidity-related discount factor.

## Not Unique to Korea — Similar Barriers Exist Elsewhere

Sector-specific foreign ownership caps aren't a purely Korean invention. India maintains separate foreign direct investment ceilings on sensitive sectors like banking, and China runs both its Qualified Foreign Institutional Investor (QFII) framework and per-stock foreign ownership caps — MSCI has, in fact, trimmed or removed Shenzhen-listed names like Han's Laser from its China indexes for the same foreign-room reason. The difference is scope: China and India extend these restrictions well beyond telecom and defense into finance and media broadly, while Korea, since its 1998 liberalization, has kept the carve-out limited to a handful of sectors — telecom, broadcasting, and aviation. That narrowness is exactly why a case like SK Telecom's makes headlines in Korea: it's the rare exception rather than the norm.

## What This Means for an Investor

If you hold shares in one of the capped names — the telecom carriers, terrestrial broadcasting affiliates, or flag carriers — you can check the current foreign exhaustion ratio through your brokerage's trading platform or Korea Exchange's public data systems. If that ratio is already in the high 90s, foreign flows in that name may not behave the way the usual "foreigners are buying because earnings improved" narrative suggests. For the vast majority of KOSPI and KOSDAQ stocks, which carry no cap at all, none of this matters. The point of this lesson isn't to turn the exhaustion ratio into a trading signal — it's to let you read foreign-flow headlines correctly by first asking whether the stock in question is even subject to a statutory cap in the first place. The same phrase, "foreign investors sold heavily," can mean a pure investment decision in an uncapped stock, or something partly mechanical in a capped one.

## Takeaway

- Korea lifted foreign investment ceilings almost entirely in 1998, but telecom (49%), terrestrial broadcasting (0%), and flag carriers (49.99%) remain capped under their own sector-specific laws.
- The foreign exhaustion ratio measures how much of the legal cap foreigners have actually filled; its inverse, foreign room, shows how much buying capacity is left.
- Once the ratio hits 100%, new foreign buy orders stop executing, and if a buyback-and-retirement program shrinks shares outstanding enough to push the foreign ratio over the cap, voting rights on the excess are suspended and the company is ordered to bring it back down.
- MSCI's Foreign Inclusion Factor discounts or removes index weight for stocks with little foreign room left — which is exactly what happened to SK Telecom in August 2022, triggering mechanical selling by passive index funds.
- In capped stocks, foreign flows layer a physical ownership constraint on top of the usual currency- and earnings-driven dynamics, which matters more the closer the exhaustion ratio gets to 100%.

## FAQ

### How do I know which stocks have a foreign ownership cap?
Only sectors named in specific statutes — the Telecommunications Business Act, Broadcasting Act, and Aviation Business Act, among others — carry a cap. The main examples are the three telecom carriers, terrestrial broadcasting affiliates, and Korea's flag carriers. You can check an individual stock's current exhaustion ratio on your brokerage platform or Korea Exchange's data systems.

### If the exhaustion ratio passes 100%, are foreign holders forced to sell?
No. Existing holdings aren't forcibly liquidated. Voting rights on the portion exceeding the legal cap are suspended, and new foreign buy orders for that stock simply won't execute on the exchange. If the breach persists, the company is ordered to bring its foreign ownership ratio back within the legal limit.

### Does this apply to ordinary, uncapped stocks too?
No. The vast majority of KOSPI and KOSDAQ stocks have carried no legal restriction on foreign ownership since the 1998 liberalization. The exhaustion ratio is only meaningful for the small set of sectors — telecom, broadcasting, aviation — that still carry a statutory cap.
