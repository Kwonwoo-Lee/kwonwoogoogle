## Is a Company That Pays More Dividends Always the Better Company?

Samsung Electronics pays a steady dividend every year. Tesla has never paid one since it went public. Some investors assume a company that pays generous dividends cares more about shareholders. Others assume a growing company is right to skip dividends and reinvest everything instead. Both views sound reasonable — but in 1961, Merton Miller and Franco Modigliani asked a more fundamental question: in a perfectly frictionless market, with no taxes and no transaction costs, does it matter at all whether a company pays dividends or not? Their surprising answer was no — not a single won of shareholder wealth changes either way. This is **dividend irrelevance theory**. It comes from the same two economists behind the [Modigliani-Miller capital structure theorem](/en/basics/capital-structure-modigliani-miller/), which showed that the mix of debt and equity doesn't affect firm value — here they apply the same logic to how a company splits its cash between retained earnings and payouts. This lesson walks through how that counterintuitive result holds up, and why real-world investors still care so much about dividends anyway.

## Homemade Dividends: You Can Manufacture Your Own

The clearest way to grasp MM's logic is a thought experiment called the **homemade dividend**. Picture an investor holding 100 shares of a ₩50,000 stock — ₩5,000,000 worth. If the company pays a ₩2,000-per-share dividend, the investor collects ₩200,000 in cash, and the share price should drop by exactly that amount to ₩48,000 (since that value has literally left the company). Total wealth: ₩200,000 in cash plus ₩4,800,000 in stock (100 × ₩48,000) — still ₩5,000,000.

Now suppose the company paid no dividend at all. The investor could simply sell off ₩200,000 worth of shares (a bit over 4 shares) on the open market and end up with exactly the same mix of cash and remaining stock. Whether the company "pushes" the cash out as a dividend or the investor "manufactures" it by selling shares, the end result is identical. The reverse works too: if the company pays a dividend an investor doesn't actually want, that investor can simply use the cash to buy more shares and recreate the no-dividend outcome on their own. With no taxes or transaction costs, there's no difference between these two paths. Because an investor can always construct their own "dividend" regardless of what the company decides, the company's dividend policy itself stops mattering.

One more assumption underpins this: MM treat a company's **investment decisions** and its **dividend decisions** as entirely separate. Whatever positive-NPV projects a company has access to get funded regardless of dividend policy. Dividend policy only governs what happens to cash left over after those investments are already locked in — pay it out now, or retain it in the company. Without this separation, dividends would get entangled with harder tradeoffs about forgone investment opportunities; with it, dividends are purely a question of cash distribution mechanics.

## "A Bird in Hand Is Worth Two in the Bush" — and MM's Rebuttal

MM's conclusion drew an immediate challenge. Myron Gordon and John Lintner argued that investors simply prefer the certainty of a dividend paid today over the uncertain promise of a larger capital gain down the road — the **bird-in-hand theory**, named for the old saying that a bird in hand beats two in the bush. It sounds intuitive: a company that retains cash is only promising a higher stock price someday, while a dividend is cash in hand right now.

MM dismissed this with a label that stuck: the **bird-in-hand fallacy**. Their point was that however uncertain that future cash eventually turns out to be, that uncertainty is set by the company's underlying business risk — how reliably its operations generate cash — not by whether a dividend check happens to arrive today. Paying a dividend sooner doesn't make the company's future cash flows any less risky; it just moves the timing of when cash arrives. Bird-in-hand theory, in MM's view, confuses the *timing* of a cash flow with the *risk* embedded in that cash flow — two entirely different things.

## So Why Does Dividend Policy Get Treated as Such a Big Deal in Practice?

Everything above describes an idealized world. In real markets, a dividend cut announcement alone can send a stock sharply lower, and analysts treat dividend policy as a core part of their research. Economist Fischer Black labeled this gap between theory and practice the **dividend puzzle** in 1976: if dividends are supposed to be irrelevant, why does everyone act like they matter so much? The answer lies in the fact that MM's perfect-market assumptions don't hold in reality. Three frictions explain most of the gap.

### Friction 1: Taxes and Transaction Costs

The most direct friction is tax treatment. Dividend income in Korea is withheld at 15.4% the moment it's paid, and total financial income above ₩20 million a year gets taxed under comprehensive income taxation at rates up to 49.5%. By contrast, the share-price gain created by a [stock buyback](/en/basics/stock-splits-and-buybacks/) isn't taxed until the shareholder actually sells. In a tax-free world, receiving a dividend and manufacturing a homemade one were equivalent; once taxes enter the picture, getting cash taxed immediately is a structurally worse deal than deferring that tax. Selling shares yourself to raise cash also carries brokerage fees — another transaction cost that breaks the equivalence. Once taxes and transaction costs exist, whether a company pays cash out or retains it stops being a matter of indifference.

### Friction 2: The Signaling Effect

The second friction is information asymmetry. Management knows far more about a company's future cash-generating ability than outside investors do. In that setting, a decision to steadily raise the dividend sends a message in dollars rather than words: "we're confident we can keep generating this level of cash going forward." This is the **signaling effect**. Because cutting a dividend once it's raised is painful and reputation-damaging, markets treat a sustained dividend increase as a relatively credible signal — management wouldn't commit to it on shaky confidence alone. That's also why announcing a cut to a long-standing dividend tends to hit a stock harder and faster than many earnings misses: investors read it not as a simple change in cash distribution, but as a much worse signal that management itself has lost confidence in the company's future cash flow.

### Friction 3: Agency Costs and Free Cash Flow Discipline

The third friction is the conflict of interest between managers and shareholders — the agency problem. In 1986, economist Michael Jensen formalized this in the **free cash flow hypothesis**: the more idle cash piles up inside a company with no good use for it, the stronger the temptation for managers to waste it on empire-building acquisitions or value-destroying pet projects that serve their own interests rather than shareholders'. Dividends force that excess cash back out of the company, out of managers' discretion. Once that cash is paid out, the company has to return to capital markets to raise funds for its next investment — subjecting itself once again to the scrutiny of investors and lenders. Seen this way, a dividend isn't just a cash handout; it's a discipline mechanism that shrinks managerial discretion and re-invites market oversight.

## The Clientele Effect: Why Investors Sort Themselves by Tax Situation

The tax friction produces a related phenomenon: the **clientele effect** — investors naturally gravitating toward stocks whose dividend policy matches their own tax situation and cash needs. Retirees who need current income, or investors below the comprehensive-taxation threshold, tend to favor high-dividend stocks. High-income investors already facing steep marginal tax rates, or institutions focused on reinvestment, tend to prefer tax-deferred buybacks and no-dividend growth stocks instead. Because of this sorting, a company that abruptly overhauls its long-standing dividend policy risks more than just a change in how cash gets distributed — it can effectively swap out the entire clientele of investors holding the stock. Many listed companies deliberately keep dividend policy predictable and steady partly because they don't want to disrupt a clientele that has already formed around a particular payout style. The recent shift among Korean companies toward buybacks and cancellations over dividends, discussed in [total shareholder yield](/en/basics/total-shareholder-yield-explained/), is itself a case study in how tax friction makes the choice between these two tools anything but irrelevant.

## Takeaway

- MM's dividend irrelevance theory shows that in a frictionless market with no taxes or transaction costs, total shareholder wealth doesn't change no matter how much a company pays out in dividends, because investors can construct homemade dividends that replicate any payout policy on their own.
- Gordon and Lintner's bird-in-hand theory argued investors simply prefer certain dividends today, but MM countered that this confuses the timing of a cash flow with the risk embedded in it.
- Dividend policy matters in the real world because of three frictions: taxes and transaction costs, the signaling effect of management's payout decisions, and the agency-cost discipline that dividends impose on free cash flow. Fischer Black named this gap between theory and practice the "dividend puzzle."
- The clientele effect describes how investors sort into stocks whose dividend policy matches their tax situation and cash needs — meaning an abrupt policy change can reshuffle not just the stock price, but the entire shareholder base.

## FAQ

### Does paying more dividends always make a company better?
No. Dividend irrelevance theory's core point is that the size of a dividend isn't itself a measure of how good a company is. A company with strong growth opportunities may create more shareholder value by reinvesting cash instead of paying it out, while a mature company with few investment options may reasonably return cash through dividends. What matters is where the company's cash is actually going, not the payout format itself.

### Does this mean dividend investing is a pointless strategy?
Not at all. The theory's conclusion holds only under idealized, frictionless-market assumptions, and real markets clearly have taxes, signaling effects, and agency costs. Understanding the theory actually sharpens dividend investing — it clarifies why getting taxed immediately on a dividend is, in theory, a worse deal, and why a steady track record of dividend increases is treated as a credible signal.

### Is the choice between dividends and buybacks related to this theory?
Yes. In a tax-free, frictionless market, dividends and buybacks have an identical effect on shareholder wealth. In reality, dividends are taxed immediately while the value created by a buyback isn't taxed until shares are sold — and that tax friction is a major reason many companies have shifted more of their payout toward buybacks.
