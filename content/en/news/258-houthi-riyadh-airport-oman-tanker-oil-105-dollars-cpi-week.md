## What Happened

Oil opened the week higher after a fresh round of Houthi strikes hit Saudi Arabia's main gateway airport and a drone struck a tanker in waters that were supposed to be a safe detour from the Strait of Hormuz. Brent crude rose 0.4% to $105.10 a barrel in early Monday trading, while West Texas Intermediate traded near $92. The dollar firmed modestly against major peers, and S&P 500 futures edged higher, building on Friday's 0.6% gain that left the index just shy of its October 6 record close.

The immediate trigger was a second Houthi missile attack on King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh, which came Sunday, October 11, just a day after a strike on the same airport killed 12 people and injured dozens more, according to Saudi authorities. It was at least the third reported hit on the airport in roughly a week — an earlier attack on October 7 or 8 had already forced a temporary closure and set off explosions audible across the Saudi capital. Witnesses described a partial evacuation after Sunday's strike. Separately, UK Maritime Trade Operations reported that a drone hit a tanker near the Sohar anchorage off the coast of Oman — a route outside the Strait of Hormuz that shippers have increasingly used specifically to avoid the chokepoint where Iran has disrupted traffic for months.

That combination matters more than either event alone. Attacking Riyadh's primary international airport — rather than only oil export infrastructure — signals the Houthis are willing to strike civilian and symbolic targets deep inside Saudi territory, raising the odds of a harder Saudi military response. And hitting a tanker near Oman, well outside Hormuz, undercuts the assumption that rerouting shipping away from the strait actually reduces risk. Markets had partly priced in Hormuz-specific disruption for months; a strike on what was supposed to be the workaround route is a different kind of signal.

All of this is landing during an unusually quiet session for one corner of the market: Monday, October 12 is Columbus Day, so while US stock exchanges trade a full normal session, the Treasury bond market, the Federal Reserve, and major banks are closed. That means the 10-year yield — which closed Friday at 5.24%, its highest since 2002 — can't move in response to the oil news until bond trading resumes Tuesday, the same morning JPMorgan and Goldman Sachs report third-quarter earnings. Wednesday brings the September CPI report, one of the last major inflation readings before the Fed's October 27-28 meeting.

## Why It Matters

Higher, stickier oil prices feed almost directly into the inflation data markets are bracing for this week. Energy costs flow into CPI both directly, through gasoline and heating fuel, and indirectly, through transportation and input costs across the economy. With the Fed having already delivered its first hike since 2023 in September specifically because tariff-driven costs kept showing up in the data, a CPI print running hotter because of a fresh oil shock would reinforce the case for another hike later this month — the exact dynamic that has pushed the 10-year yield to a 24-year high and made the stock market's record-high valuations more fragile to any single data surprise.

The equity exposure splits sharply by sector, and that split is itself the trade worth watching. US airlines carry some of the most direct and immediate fuel-cost risk in the market because most large carriers have largely abandoned fuel hedging in recent years, leaving them exposed to spot prices. Delta has already withdrawn its full-year guidance this earnings season and has previously estimated that every one-cent increase in jet fuel costs the airline roughly $40 million annually; United is unhedged entirely and faces the same spot-market exposure without even Delta's partial refinery hedge. On the other side of the ledger, US oil majors benefit directly from a higher price per barrel, which is why a single geopolitical headline can simultaneously hammer one blue-chip sector and lift another on the same trading day — a divergence that shows up clearly in sector ETF performance even when the headline indexes barely move.

There's also a structural point in how this specific escalation differs from recent weeks' Hormuz-focused tanker attacks. When risk is concentrated at a single geographic chokepoint, markets can price a fairly specific "closure premium." When attacks start hitting a civilian airport and a tanker on what was considered a safe bypass route, the risk becomes harder to geographically contain, which tends to support a higher structural floor under oil prices rather than just episodic spikes tied to specific incidents.

## What to Take Away From This

- **A single headline rarely moves every stock the same direction — look at who actually bears the cost.** Airlines and energy producers can move in opposite directions on the identical oil-price headline, because one group pays the input cost and the other collects the revenue.
- **Unhedged exposure is a measurable, disclosed risk factor, not a vague one.** Delta's own disclosed sensitivity ($40 million per penny of jet fuel) and United's lack of a refinery hedge are concrete numbers you can actually compare across the sector before an oil shock hits, not after.
- **When a market that normally reacts to news is closed, the reaction doesn't disappear — it's deferred.** The bond market's Columbus Day closure means Monday's oil shock gets priced into Treasury yields only once trading resumes Tuesday, right alongside bank earnings — a stacked catalyst morning worth watching rather than assuming Monday's calm means the story is over.
- **Geographic diversification of risk (a bypass shipping route, a backup supplier) only works until the risk follows you there.** The Oman tanker strike is a reminder that a hedge against one specific threat isn't the same as a hedge against the underlying conflict.

## FAQ

### Why did a tanker attack near Oman move oil prices if it's outside the Strait of Hormuz?

Because that route exists specifically as a workaround for ships avoiding Hormuz. If attacks can reach vessels there too, shippers lose the one detour that was keeping some cargo moving at lower insurance costs, which raises the effective risk premium on the entire region's oil exports rather than just the strait itself.

### Why are airline stocks more exposed to this than oil companies?

Airlines buy jet fuel as a direct input cost and generally no longer hedge it the way they did a decade ago, so a spot-price spike hits their margins almost immediately. Oil producers, by contrast, sell at the higher price, so the same headline that hurts an airline's cost structure directly improves an energy major's revenue per barrel.

### Will this show up in Wednesday's CPI report?

Not necessarily in the September data being released Wednesday, since that reflects price levels collected before this weekend's escalation. The more relevant risk is to October's inflation reading and to how the Fed weighs energy costs heading into its October 27-28 meeting, where a sustained higher oil price would add to the case for another rate hike.
