## What Happened

Intuit (Nasdaq: INTU), the maker of QuickBooks and TurboTax, reported fiscal first-quarter 2027 results after the close on Thursday, November 19. On the surface, it was a clean beat. Revenue came in at $4.354 billion, up 12% year over year and above the roughly $4.31 billion analysts had modeled. Non-GAAP EPS of $2.91 beat the roughly $2.70 consensus and was up 56% from $1.87 a year earlier. GAAP EPS rose 63% to $2.59, and GAAP operating income climbed the same percentage to $868 million.

The segment detail was just as encouraging. Global Business Solutions, the division housing QuickBooks, grew revenue 15%, with the Online Ecosystem portion up 20%. The Consumer segment, anchored by TurboTax, grew 7%. Revenue, EPS, and the two core operating segments all cleared the bar - close to a triple beat.

Buried in the same release, though, was a number that stopped the rally before it started: full-year guidance. Intuit narrowed its fiscal 2027 revenue outlook to $23.081-$23.365 billion, implying 8%-9% growth. The source of that softer outlook was almost entirely one business line. Credit Karma's full-year growth guidance was cut to 3%-7%, down from the prior 11%-13% - the forecast was effectively cut in half. Notably, non-GAAP EPS guidance was left unchanged at $22.88-$23.12, signaling management believes it can offset the Credit Karma slowdown through cost discipline and strength elsewhere.

The market's response was immediate and severe. Intuit shares fell roughly 10% in after-hours trading following the release - a textbook case of a company beating on every backward-looking number and still getting punished for what comes next.

The same evening brought a second major announcement. Intuit agreed to sell Mailchimp, the email-marketing platform it bought for roughly $12 billion in cash and stock back in 2021, to Italian technology company Bending Spoons (Nasdaq: BSP). According to a Form 6-K filed with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, the deal values Mailchimp at an enterprise value of $3.68 billion - less than a third of what Intuit originally paid. The structure has a wrinkle: Intuit will reinvest $368 million of the proceeds into newly issued Bending Spoons equity, meaning it isn't walking away entirely but is instead taking a minority stake in the company now responsible for turning Mailchimp around. The transaction is expected to close in early calendar 2027, pending regulatory approval.

Mailchimp's own numbers explain the logic behind the sale. The unit's revenue came in at $313 million for the quarter, down 1% year over year, continuing a pattern of flat-to-declining growth that Intuit has flagged for several quarters running. When Intuit bought Mailchimp in 2021, the thesis was that its roughly 13 million users - including about 800,000 paying customers - would anchor a broader small-business marketing ecosystem around QuickBooks. That thesis never fully played out: many small-business customers migrated to other tools already inside the QuickBooks ecosystem, while email marketing itself lost ground to direct advertising on platforms like Meta and Google.

The identity of the buyer adds an interesting wrinkle to the story. Bending Spoons listed on the Nasdaq on July 1, 2026, priced at $29 a share and popped roughly 40% on its first day of trading. Its business model is built around acquiring stalled consumer internet brands at a discount, stripping out costs, and running them for cash flow - it already owns Evernote, WeTransfer, Vimeo, and AOL. Mailchimp becomes the newest addition to that turnaround portfolio.

## Why a Beat-and-Raise Quarter Turned Into a Selloff

The core mechanic here is that markets price the future, not the past. Q1's revenue and EPS describe three months that have already happened. Guidance describes the next nine months. When a company cuts a single segment's growth forecast roughly in half, it is telling investors, in its own words, that the momentum behind that business is fading - and investors reprice the stock on that signal regardless of how good the prior quarter looked.

Understanding why Credit Karma moved the needle so hard requires understanding how the unit actually makes money. Credit Karma offers free credit-score monitoring, then earns referral fees by matching users with credit cards, personal loans, and insurance products from banks, card issuers, and insurers. That revenue depends on how aggressively those lenders are spending on new-customer acquisition - which in turn depends on the broader rate and credit environment. With the 30-year Treasury yield sitting near a 19-year high in recent weeks and the Fed leaving the door open to another rate increase, lenders have reportedly grown more conservative with marketing budgets, a dynamic that lands directly on Credit Karma's referral revenue. Read that way, the guidance cut isn't just an Intuit-specific stumble - it is also a data point on tightening conditions across consumer lending more broadly.

It's also worth resisting the simple read that selling Mailchimp at a steep markdown automatically proves the 2021 acquisition was a failure. In capital allocation, what matters is not what was already paid - that's a sunk cost - but whether continuing to hold an asset is still the best use of capital going forward. For Intuit, redirecting management attention and capital away from a stagnant Mailchimp and toward a buyer that specializes in reviving exactly this kind of asset, while freeing up cash and a minority equity stake to redeploy into AI investment or its faster-growing core businesses, can be a rational decision on its own terms - even if the market chose to read the announcement, on the same evening as the guidance cut, as a formal admission of a costly mistake.

## What to Take Away From This

- **A "beat" headline doesn't guarantee a higher stock price.** Revenue and EPS topping estimates mean nothing if the accompanying guidance disappoints. Always separate last quarter's results from next year's forecast when you read an earnings release.
- **Break guidance down by segment, not just the consolidated number.** Intuit's entire guidance cut traced back to one division. The headline revenue range alone would have told you something changed, but not what - or how concentrated the risk actually was.
- **Flag businesses with hidden rate sensitivity.** A unit like Credit Karma, dependent on lender marketing budgets, is indirectly exposed to interest-rate and credit-market conditions even though it isn't a bank itself. Connecting macro indicators to specific business-segment guidance is a habit worth building.
- **Don't judge a divestiture purely by the gap between purchase price and sale price.** Sunk costs are sunk. The right question for a seller is always "who can run this asset better from here," not "did we lose money versus what we originally paid."
- **When two major stories land on the same night, separate them before reacting.** Here, an earnings beat and a multi-billion-dollar divestiture were announced together. The stock's net move reflects both pieces of news blended together, so pulling them apart is the only way to understand which one is actually driving the price action.

## FAQ

### Did Intuit's stock fall because of bad earnings?
No. Q1 revenue ($4.354 billion, up 12%) and non-GAAP EPS ($2.91) both beat Wall Street's estimates. The stock fell because the accompanying full-year guidance showed Credit Karma's growth forecast cut from 11%-13% to 3%-7%, a signal that outweighed the quarter's actual results.

### What is Credit Karma, and why does it matter so much to Intuit's stock?
Credit Karma offers free credit-monitoring tools and earns revenue by referring users to credit cards, loans, and insurance products, collecting a fee from the lender or insurer for each successful match. It has been one of Intuit's fastest-growing segments, so a sharp cut to its growth outlook carries an outsized effect on how investors value the whole company.

### Why would Intuit sell Mailchimp for a fraction of what it paid?
The $12 billion Intuit paid in 2021 is a sunk cost that can't be recovered either way. What matters now is that Mailchimp's growth has stalled inside Intuit's ecosystem, and management apparently concluded that handing it to a buyer built specifically to revive stalled internet brands - while keeping a minority stake in that buyer - was a better use of capital than continuing to run it in-house.

### Who is Bending Spoons, and why is it buying Mailchimp?
Bending Spoons is an Italian technology company that listed on the Nasdaq in July 2026 under the ticker BSP. Its business model is acquiring consumer internet brands whose growth has flattened - it already owns Evernote, WeTransfer, Vimeo, and AOL - and running them more efficiently for cash flow. Mailchimp fits squarely into that pattern.
