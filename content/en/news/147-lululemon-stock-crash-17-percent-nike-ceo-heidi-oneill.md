## What Happened

Lululemon Athletica (NASDAQ: LULU) shares plunged 17.38% on Friday, September 4, closing at $100.61 - below its 52-week low and its weakest close in roughly eight years. The strange part is that this crash came the day after a quarter that technically beat expectations. Lululemon's second-quarter adjusted earnings per share came in at $2.92, well above the $1.82 analysts had modeled. But that headline number included $0.86 per share from tariff refunds and related interest income; stripped of that one-time boost, adjusted EPS was really $2.06. Revenue fell 4% year over year to $2.4 billion, missing the $2.46 billion consensus.

What actually moved the stock wasn't the quarter that just ended - it was the outlook Lululemon issued alongside it. The company slashed its full-year fiscal 2026 revenue guidance to a range of $10.35 billion to $10.5 billion, implying a 5% to 7% decline, with the $10.425 billion midpoint landing far below the $11.03 billion Wall Street had penciled in. Just one quarter earlier, management had guided to roughly flat revenue or, at worst, a 1% decline. Full-year adjusted EPS guidance dropped just as sharply, to $9.48-$9.73 from a prior $10.95-$11.15, undershooting even the already-lowered $10.84 consensus. The most jarring number was third-quarter EPS guidance of just $0.93 to $0.98, versus a Street estimate of $2.43 - a shortfall of roughly 60%. This marks the third time since March that Lululemon has cut its annual guidance.

The underlying operating numbers explain why. Comparable sales fell 9% overall (10% on a constant-currency basis), with North America - still Lululemon's largest market - down a steep 12%. Within that, sales of leggings, the product category most associated with the brand, fell 20%, which management attributed to a broader shift in consumer taste away from body-hugging silhouettes toward looser, "away-from-body" fits. China Mainland, until recently the company's strongest growth region, also reversed course: reported revenue there rose 4%, but on a constant-currency basis it actually fell 2%. Stifel analysts wrote in a note that they were "disappointed to see another sharp downwards revision in revenue and EPS," adding that "questions on trend softness in the core North America market are not yet answered, and a surprise reversal of China Mainland trends - previously the strongest regional revenue contributor - further impacts the consolidated outlook."

The timing lands at an unusually pointed moment for Lululemon's leadership. Heidi O'Neill, who spent years running Nike's consumer and marketing organization, is set to formally take over as Lululemon's CEO on September 8. She inherits a company that has now cut guidance three times in six months, is projecting a roughly 60% EPS shortfall for the coming quarter, and is seeing double-digit sales declines in its home market.

## Two Athletic Apparel Giants, Two Very Different Roads to the Same Place

What makes this story more than a single bad earnings print is that Lululemon's incoming CEO is walking straight out of one struggling athletic brand and into another. Nike (NYSE: NKE) has been mired in its own multi-year slump: shares closed at $39.09 on August 17, a 12-year low, and kept sliding into early September, touching the low-$38 range - roughly 78% below the stock's all-time high set in November 2021. Two of the industry's most recognizable names are trading near multi-year lows within weeks of each other.

But the mechanisms behind each decline are genuinely different. Nike's problem has built up over years, not months: Greater China revenue has now declined for eight consecutive quarters, and JPMorgan downgraded the stock to Underweight partly because a planned overhaul of Nike's China digital operations is expected to create a revenue headwind north of $1 billion. Lululemon's problem looks more like an acute credibility crisis than a slow-burning structural one. Three guidance cuts in six months is the kind of pattern that makes investors stop taking a company's forward projections at face value, and the fact that this quarter's headline EPS beat was inflated by a one-time tariff refund only deepened that skepticism. The gap between the reported number ($2.92) and the real one ($2.06, paired with a guidance cut implying a 60% EPS miss next quarter) is precisely why the market treated a "beat" as a red flag rather than good news.

The 20% drop in leggings sales carries meaning beyond the raw percentage. It suggests that the fitted-silhouette category that built Lululemon's identity is now on the losing side of a consumer taste shift, and taste shifts in apparel are slow to reverse because product-development cycles take quarters to catch up. The reversal in China constant-currency growth deserves the same scrutiny: it raises the question of whether the local-brand competition and pulled-back consumer spending that have already hit Nike in China are starting to spread to Lululemon as well.

Against that backdrop, hiring O'Neill cuts both ways. Her background running consumer experience and brand marketing at Nike maps closely onto what Lululemon needs right now - reasserting a fraying brand identity and reconnecting with its core customer. But she is also arriving with a problem that Nike itself has not visibly solved during her tenure there: how a sportswear brand keeps pace with shifting consumer preferences faster than its own product cycle allows.

## What to Take Away From This

- **Separate the headline EPS beat from the underlying number.** Lululemon's $2.92 adjusted EPS looked like a clean beat, but $0.86 of it came from a one-time tariff refund. Any earnings surprise built partly on non-recurring items needs to be broken down before you take it at face value.
- **The frequency of guidance cuts is itself a risk signal.** A third guidance cut in six months tells you either that management doesn't have clear visibility into the business, or that forecasting has become structurally harder - and both possibilities should lower your confidence in whatever guidance comes next.
- **A sharp decline in a company's signature product category can be a brand-identity problem, not just a bad quarter.** A 20% drop in leggings sales points to something more fundamental than one weak period - a shift in what customers actually want to wear.
- **Hiring a rival's executive is not a guaranteed fix.** Heidi O'Neill's Nike pedigree is a real asset, but Nike itself hasn't solved a similar structural challenge yet. A proven résumé doesn't automatically transfer to solving a different company's specific problems.
- **When multiple companies in the same sector hit multi-year lows around the same time, look past company-specific explanations for a broader trend.** Lululemon and Nike are struggling for different reasons, but that overlap itself hints at a wider shift underway in athletic apparel demand.

## FAQ

### Lululemon's earnings beat estimates - so why did the stock crash?
Adjusted EPS of $2.92 topped the $1.82 consensus, but $0.86 of that came from a one-time tariff refund, leaving real EPS closer to $2.06. More importantly, the market was reacting to the forward guidance issued alongside the results - full-year revenue, full-year EPS, and third-quarter EPS guidance all came in well below what analysts expected. At any earnings release, the backward-looking results and the forward-looking guidance need to be evaluated separately.

### Who is Heidi O'Neill, and why is she taking over now?
Heidi O'Neill previously ran Nike's consumer and marketing organization and formally becomes Lululemon's CEO on September 8. She takes the helm just as the company has delivered its third consecutive guidance cut in six months, projected a roughly 60% EPS shortfall for the next quarter, and reported a double-digit sales decline in its core North American market.

### Are Lululemon's and Nike's stock declines driven by the same underlying cause?
Not really. Nike's slide reflects a structural, multi-year problem - eight straight quarters of declining China revenue and a costly digital-operations overhaul. Lululemon's decline looks more like an acute credibility crisis following three guidance cuts in six months, compounded by a consumer shift away from its signature legging silhouette. What the two do share is exposure to a broader change in athletic-apparel demand that appears to be affecting the sector as a whole.
