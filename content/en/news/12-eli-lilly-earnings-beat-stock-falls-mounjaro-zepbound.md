## What Happened

Before the opening bell on August 5, **Eli Lilly** (LLY) reported second-quarter results that, on paper, were about as clean a beat as a company can post.

- **Revenue**: $23.0 billion, up 48% year-over-year, well ahead of the roughly $20.9 billion analysts expected
- **Adjusted EPS**: $8.38, sharply above the consensus estimate of around $6
- **Mounjaro** (diabetes): $9.9 billion in sales, up 91% year-over-year
- **Zepbound** (obesity): $4.9 billion in US sales, up 44% year-over-year
- **Foundayo**, the oral obesity pill approved in April, generated roughly $98 million in its first full quarter, beating expectations

On the back of those numbers, Lilly raised its full-year revenue guidance from $82-85 billion to $85-87 billion and lifted its adjusted EPS outlook to $35.50-$36.50. Every headline number - revenue, profit, flagship product growth, and forward guidance - came in above what the Street was looking for.

And yet Lilly shares fell roughly 2% Wednesday morning to around $1,123, moving in the opposite direction of the numbers themselves.

## Why a "Perfect" Quarter Didn't Lift the Stock

- **The stock had already priced in a blowout quarter.** Lilly shares had climbed more than 40% since its previous earnings report and hit a fresh all-time high just weeks earlier in early July. By the time this report landed, the market had already baked in an assumption that Lilly would beat by a wide margin and raise guidance again. When expectations are already set that high, the stock doesn't move on whether the company delivered a great quarter - it moves on whether the quarter exceeded an already-extreme bar. This one didn't clear that second, higher bar by enough.
- **Read alongside Novo Nordisk's report from the day before, the pattern gets clearer.** A day earlier, GLP-1 rival Novo Nordisk also raised guidance and also saw its stock fall - in that case by more than 6% (see our related coverage). The two reports were nearly opposite in substance: Novo's guidance raise was undercut by weakening US sales, while Lilly delivered genuine, broad-based growth in the US and abroad. Yet both stocks sold off. That's a signal the entire GLP-1 sector's bar has risen to the point where merely meeting elevated expectations isn't enough - only clearing them by a wide, fresh margin moves these stocks higher now.
- **Fully re-rated growth stocks need a new story, not a confirmation of the old one.** Once a stock has already priced in years of anticipated growth, an earnings report mostly just confirms that the existing growth trend is intact. To push the stock meaningfully higher from there typically requires something beyond that - an unexpected pipeline result, a new growth driver the market hasn't priced in yet. This report was a strong confirmation of Lilly's existing trajectory, but it didn't introduce a materially new upside catalyst.

## What to Take Away From This

- **Weigh the size of the beat against how far the stock ran before the print, not the beat in isolation.** Buying purely because a company posted a beat-and-raise quarter would have lost money here. A large pre-earnings rally raises the bar for what counts as "good enough" - check the run-up, not just the headline numbers, before assuming a strong quarter will be rewarded.
- **Comparing earnings reactions across an entire sector reveals sentiment that a single company's numbers can't.** Novo Nordisk and Lilly fell for almost opposite reasons on consecutive days. When a pattern like that repeats across a sector, it usually points to a valuation or expectations problem affecting the whole group, not a flaw specific to one company.
- **For richly valued growth stocks, always ask what the next catalyst is.** A stock that has already priced in strong growth needs more than "the growth continued" to re-rate higher. Look for pipeline readouts, new product launches, or market expansion that the current price doesn't yet reflect.
