## What Happened

You may remember yesterday's story: on the night of November 3, as midterm election results rolled in and pointed toward a Democratic sweep of both the House and Senate, Nasdaq 100 futures fell nearly 2% at one point, and by Wednesday morning S&P 500 futures were still down about 1.3%, Nasdaq 100 futures down 2.2%, and Dow futures off 0.8%. The fear driving that selloff was straightforward: a Democratic-controlled Congress raising the odds of tighter scrutiny on the AI trade.

Then the opening bell told a completely different story. On Wednesday, November 4, the Dow Jones Industrial Average surged 1,307 points, or roughly 2.5% - a move CNBC described as the index's best single day since April. The S&P 500 climbed about 1.5-1.6%, and the tech-heavy Nasdaq Composite rose even more, up roughly 1.7-1.8%. CNN's headline captured the reversal plainly: "Dow jumps by 1,300 points as Democrats take control of Congress." CNBC's live markets coverage attributed the rally to political risk easing after what it called a "resounding Democratic victory" in the midterms.

Just hours after the single biggest fear on investors' minds had been confirmed as reality, the market didn't sell off further - it ripped higher into some of its best levels of the year. Understanding why requires separating what overnight futures markets are pricing from what the regular cash session reveals once a much broader set of participants weighs in.

On the Monday before the election, the Nasdaq Composite had already closed up 0.83% and the S&P 500 gained 0.36%, suggesting markets were cautiously positioning for some relief even before the outcome was known. Once the Democratic sweep was confirmed overnight, futures reacted with what looks, in hindsight, like a short-lived panic - one the cash market reversed almost entirely within hours of the opening bell.

## Why the Overnight Plunge Flipped Into a Rally

The most direct catalyst appears to be President Trump's posture toward the results. Ahead of the election, Trump had said repeatedly that he would accept the outcome only "if it's honest" - language that itself injected uncertainty into markets, since it left open the possibility of a contested result. Once the Democratic sweep was confirmed, signals that the administration intended to accept the outcome without a prolonged legal fight removed what may have been the more market-relevant risk: not who won, but whether the result would be disputed. Markets tend to rally once a genuine unknown resolves into a known, even an unfavorable one, and that pattern tends to repeat across elections, Fed meetings, and earnings reports alike - the shift from uncertainty to certainty is often itself the catalyst for buying, independent of whether the news is good or bad on its face.

A second factor is historical seasonality. CNBC's coverage noted that the S&P 500 has risen in the aftermath of every one of the last 11 midterm elections dating back to 1982. That's a narrower, more favorable track record than the Ned Davis Research statistic we cited in yesterday's piece - the S&P 500 finished higher in five of eight cycles since 1930 in which the president's party lost the House. The two datasets use different time windows and arrive at different hit rates, but both point the same direction: short-term wobbles around midterms haven't reliably translated into a down market over the following months. Traders aware of that pattern may well have treated the overnight futures plunge as a buying opportunity rather than a reason to panic.

A third factor is a reassessment of how real the "AI regulation" fear actually was. As covered in yesterday's article, most analysts believe that even with Democrats controlling both chambers, President Trump's veto power makes it unlikely that any sweeping AI-targeted legislation actually becomes law. Some analysts, including Jefferies' Anik Shah, have even argued that moderate regulation could be a net positive for the sector's long-term credibility. Overnight, that nuance appears to have reasserted itself: the initial futures reaction may simply have overstated how much actual policy risk a Democratic Congress poses to AI stocks given the veto constraint.

## What to Take Away From This

- **Overnight futures and the regular cash session can diverge sharply.** Futures markets trade on thinner volume with a narrower set of participants, which can exaggerate an initial reaction to news. Once the opening bell brings in a much deeper pool of capital, that initial move often gets reassessed - sometimes completely reversed. Don't extrapolate a full day's direction from overnight futures alone, especially around major scheduled events.
- **Separate the headline event from the secondary uncertainty wrapped around it.** What spooked futures wasn't simply "Democrats won" - it was the possibility of a contested, drawn-out dispute over the result. Once signals suggested the outcome would be accepted without a fight, that secondary risk evaporated, and the market rallied on the resolution of uncertainty itself rather than the underlying outcome.
- **Historical base rates are context, not a guarantee.** An 11-for-11 record for the S&P 500 after midterms since 1982 is a notable data point, but it's a small sample, and this cycle began with the Nasdaq 100 already in its third technical correction of the year - a structural difference from past cycles. Understand the mechanism behind a historical pattern (uncertainty resolution, veto-constrained legislative risk) before assuming it will repeat exactly.
- **Legal and structural constraints matter more than headlines.** A Democratic sweep sounds dramatic in a headline, but the presidential veto meaningfully caps what can actually become binding policy. Before trading a political headline, ask whether the feared outcome can realistically become law.

## FAQ

### If futures fell so sharply, why did the actual market rally instead?
Futures markets are thinner and tend to overreact to breaking news. Once the regular session opened, signals that the administration would accept the midterm results without contesting them removed a layer of uncertainty that may have mattered more to markets than the election outcome itself, and that relief drove the rally.

### Does this mean AI regulation risk is now fully off the table?
No. Hearings, committee activity, and increased scrutiny of AI companies remain plausible even with the veto constraint in place. Most analysts view this as an ongoing headline risk rather than a resolved issue, since legislation with a real chance of becoming law would need to be something Trump is willing to sign.

### Has the S&P 500 always risen after midterm elections?
According to CNBC, the S&P 500 rose in each of the last 11 midterm election cycles since 1982. A longer-dated Ned Davis Research study going back to 1930 found a less perfect but still favorable record: five of eight instances where the president's party lost the House were followed by a higher S&P 500 by year-end. The exact hit rate depends on the sample window, but the broader direction - resilience despite short-term volatility - holds across both datasets.
