## What Happened

According to Strategy's (Nasdaq: MSTR) weekly disclosure released Monday, October 26, the company bought and sold zero bitcoin during the week of October 19-25. Its holdings stayed flat at 848,517 BTC. According to reporting from The Block, this breaks a five-week streak of weekly purchases - a notable pause for a company whose entire identity has been built around buying bitcoin, week after week, without fail.

Instead of buying bitcoin, Strategy spent $204.9 million that week repurchasing roughly 2.07 million shares of its own STRC preferred stock. The money came entirely out of the company's USD cash balance, which fell 45.8% in a single week, from $447.3 million to $242.4 million. Notably untouched was a separate $4.83 billion reserve the company holds specifically to cover dividend and interest payments. On the Monday the filing landed, MSTR shares slipped from Friday's $146.01 close down toward roughly $137 intraday before recovering some of the loss.

Taken in isolation, this reads like a minor capital-allocation footnote - cash moved from one use to another. But the timing is not a coincidence. Just eleven days earlier, on October 15, index giant MSCI finalized a rule change that could determine whether Strategy stays in some of the world's most widely tracked stock indexes at all.

## Why Does an Index Rule Matter This Much to a Bitcoin Company?

On October 15, MSCI finalized a new methodology that makes "non-operating companies" ineligible for its Global Investable Market Indexes. This matters enormously for Strategy because the company fits that description almost by design. Strategy began as an enterprise software firm, but since 2020 it has converted the overwhelming majority of its balance sheet into bitcoin, effectively becoming a publicly traded wrapper around a bitcoin position. Under MSCI's new test, a company is first screened on whether operating assets make up most of its total assets; if it fails that screen, it's tested against five financial ratios, and failing four or more makes it ineligible.

The rule applies starting with November's index review. The list of which specific companies are affected is due November 11, and the change takes full effect after the market close on November 30. Existing index members do get one buffer: removal only happens after failing the test in two consecutive annual reporting periods, so Strategy's exact fate isn't locked in yet. Still, the market didn't wait for certainty - it reacted immediately. On the first trading session after the rule was finalized, Friday October 16, MSTR stock fell 9.56%, from a prior close of $151.29 down to $136.83. For context, the Nasdaq Composite itself fell just 2.14% that same day amid a broader tech selloff, meaning MSTR's decline was more than four times the index drop.

Why does an index rule move a stock this much? Because index exclusion forces mechanical, valuation-blind selling. Passive funds that track an index are contractually required to dump a stock the moment it's removed, regardless of what they think of the company's prospects. Strategy itself has estimated that MSCI-linked funds hold roughly 13 million of its shares, worth about $1.8 billion at the time - a sum the company argues is smaller than a single day's typical trading volume and therefore manageable. But that estimate only covers MSCI's own funds. JPMorgan has previously estimated that if other major index providers followed MSCI's lead, total forced selling could reach as much as $8.8 billion. It's worth being precise about what index exclusion does and doesn't mean: it is not a Nasdaq delisting, and nothing about it legally forces Strategy to sell a single bitcoin. What it does create is a separate, fundamentals-independent source of selling pressure.

Seen against that backdrop, this week's bitcoin purchase pause looks less like an isolated accounting choice and more like a shift in priorities. Strategy has publicly rejected MSCI's classification, arguing the "non-operating company" framework has no basis in GAAP, IFRS, or any recognized accounting standard, and CEO Michael Saylor has repeatedly said exclusion "won't make any difference." Yet at the same time, the company chose to stop buying bitcoin and instead used cash to retire preferred shares - which reduces a debt-like obligation on the balance sheet - while deliberately leaving the $4.83 billion dividend-and-interest reserve untouched. Public confidence and actual capital allocation can tell two different stories, and this week they diverged.

Bitcoin's own backdrop isn't helping. With the Fed's rate decision due Wednesday, October 28, followed quickly by September's PCE inflation data, risk assets broadly have turned cautious, and bitcoin itself has been trading in the mid-to-high $80,000s - well below its earlier highs this year. Strategy's mNAV (market cap divided by the value of its bitcoin holdings) has also compressed from multiples north of 3x in earlier cycles down to roughly 1x today, which weakens the arbitrage-like logic behind raising fresh capital to buy more bitcoin in the first place.

## What to Take Away From This

- **Index-exclusion risk is a variable separate from company fundamentals.** MSTR's 9.56% drop on the day MSCI's rule was finalized had nothing to do with bitcoin's price or Strategy's actual holdings - it was priced on anticipated mechanical selling by passive funds. Index events can move a stock sharply without a single fundamental fact changing.
- **Watch what management does with cash, not just what they say.** Strategy has publicly dismissed the MSCI exclusion as immaterial, while simultaneously pausing bitcoin purchases and redirecting cash to defensively retire preferred stock. Actual capital flows are often a more reliable signal than public statements.
- **A shrinking mNAV premium undercuts the entire "buy more bitcoin with new capital" growth loop.** When a bitcoin-proxy stock's premium to its underlying holdings compresses, raising fresh equity or debt to buy more bitcoin becomes far less attractive. Anyone holding this kind of stock should track that premium alongside bitcoin's price itself.
- **"Not a delisting" and "no effect on the stock" are two different claims.** Index exclusion doesn't threaten a company's listing or its legal survival, but it does create real, mechanical selling pressure from funds that have no choice but to sell. Conflating the two risks badly misjudging the downside.

## FAQ

### Will Strategy's stock get delisted if MSCI removes it from the index?

No. MSCI index exclusion is entirely separate from a Nasdaq delisting. The stock keeps trading as normal, and Strategy faces no legal requirement to sell any of its bitcoin. What changes is that passive funds tracking the relevant MSCI indexes are required to sell their holdings, creating added selling pressure that has nothing to do with the company's fundamentals.

### Is it certain that Strategy will be excluded on November 30?

Not yet. MSCI's rule includes a buffer for companies already in its indexes: removal only happens after failing the eligibility test across two consecutive annual reporting periods. The specific list of affected companies is due November 11, and the rule's full effect begins after the market close on November 30, but Strategy's ultimate inclusion or exclusion isn't settled yet.

### Does skipping a week of bitcoin purchases mean Strategy is abandoning its bitcoin strategy?

That seems unlikely based on the available facts. The company left its $4.83 billion dividend-and-interest reserve completely untouched, and pausing a weekly purchase isn't unprecedented for the firm. It's more reasonable to read this specific week's choice - redirecting cash to a preferred-stock buyback instead of new bitcoin - as a short-term move toward balance-sheet caution at a moment when both the MSCI index question and bitcoin's own price are working against the company, rather than a strategic reversal.
