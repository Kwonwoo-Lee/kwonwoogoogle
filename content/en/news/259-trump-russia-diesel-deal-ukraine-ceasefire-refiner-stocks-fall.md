## What Happened

On Friday, October 9, President Trump announced that Russia had agreed to immediately ship diesel into US and international markets - an initial 300,000 tonnes right away, with additional volumes to follow, though reported totals vary widely across outlets, with some citing figures north of 4.8 million tonnes annually. Within hours, the US Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control issued General License 135, temporarily suspending sanctions on Russian diesel shipments through April 2027. The legal mechanism to actually trade the fuel arrived almost as fast as the announcement itself. Trump framed the move explicitly as an effort to bring down soaring US fuel prices.

The market reaction was immediate and specific. New York Harbor diesel futures fell toward $4.67 a gallon, and WTI crude briefly slipped from around $91 toward the $90 mark before partly recovering. More notably, shares of the refiners that had posted the best stock returns in the market this year all fell. Reports differ slightly on magnitude, but Marathon Petroleum dropped roughly 1.8%, Valero Energy fell around 2.3%, and PBF Energy - which carries outsized diesel exposure - slid more than 5%. Other tallies put Marathon and Valero closer to 2% lower and PBF down about 4.6%. The exact figures vary by source, but the direction was consistent: a headline about more diesel supply hit the stocks that had been profiting most from diesel scarcity.

Two days later, on Sunday, October 11, a second headline landed on top of the first. Trump posted on social media that Russia and Ukraine had agreed to an "immediate" ceasefire on striking each other's energy infrastructure. No details were released, and neither government confirmed the agreement officially. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky offered a conditional response, saying Ukraine would stop targeting Russian refineries if Russia stopped hitting Ukrainian energy infrastructure first - and on the same day, reports emerged of a fresh Russian strike on a Ukrainian power plant, immediately widening the gap between the word "ceasefire" and events on the ground. The announcement also drew pushback inside Trump's own party: Rep. Brian Fitzpatrick said he plans to introduce legislation blocking US purchases of Russian oil and refined products.

By Monday, October 12, Brent crude was trading modestly lower near $103 a barrel and WTI near $91 in Asian and European hours. US bond markets and the Federal Reserve were closed for Columbus Day, but the New York Stock Exchange and Nasdaq traded a full session, with Dow, S&P 500 and Nasdaq futures edging higher after Friday's close left the S&P 500 just shy of a record. Investor attention was already shifting toward Tuesday's third-quarter earnings from JPMorgan and Goldman Sachs and Wednesday's September CPI report - with this week's diesel and oil headlines now a variable in how that inflation print could come in.

## Why the Refining Trade Is Suddenly Vulnerable

To understand the stock reaction, start with the "crack spread" - the margin a refiner earns by buying crude oil and converting it into products like diesel. It isn't the price of crude or the price of diesel alone that drives refiner profits, but the gap between the two. That gap spent much of 2026 at record levels. According to industry tracker RBN Energy, the New York Harbor diesel crack hit an intraday record of $108.02 a barrel on September 2 and closed at a record $107.72 on September 10, averaging roughly $111 a barrel for the month of September - itself a record. Gulf Coast ultra-low-sulfur diesel spot prices hit $5.0125 a gallon on September 10, the highest level since that assessment began in 2006.

That unusual margin came from two supply shocks landing at once. Russian refined-product exports, including diesel, had been curtailed by sanctions, the war, and Ukrainian strikes on Russian refineries, while tanker attacks and transit disruption around the Strait of Hormuz and the Red Sea squeezed seaborne crude and refined-product flows out of the Middle East. With both supply channels tightening simultaneously, global diesel inventories fell well below seasonal norms, and that scarcity flowed straight into refiners' margins. Phillips 66 disclosed that its worldwide refining margin had roughly doubled to $24 a barrel, and Marathon Petroleum reported a second-quarter refining margin of $36.33 a barrel, more than double the $17.58 it earned a year earlier. Those numbers are the direct source of the outsized 2026 stock gains at Valero, Marathon Petroleum, Phillips 66 and PBF Energy.

What's happening now is a policy move aimed squarely at one of the two legs holding up that scarcity. If Russian diesel actually reaches US and global buyers in volume, the refined-product shortage that has been propping up crack spreads could ease, which is exactly why diesel-exposed refiner stocks sold off on the mere announcement. But skepticism about how much this actually changes runs deep. Michael Lynch of the Energy Policy Research Foundation compared the move to "shuffling deck chairs on the Titanic," arguing it will do little to move pump prices. A Forbes analysis reached a similar conclusion, noting the sanctions relief itself is explicitly temporary: the license tied to Russian oil major Lukoil expires October 22, and Russia's own diesel export ban is separately set to expire October 31, meaning the entire arrangement could shift again within weeks. On top of that, actually getting Russian diesel onto ships and into US ports involves logistics, insurance arrangements, and repairs to war-damaged Russian refineries - steps that can create a long lag between an announcement and any real change in supply. The energy ceasefire carries the same uncertainty: with neither government confirming it officially and a fresh strike reported the same day it was announced, treating it as a settled end to the geopolitical risk premium in oil looks premature.

## What to Take Away From This

- **The best-performing trade of the year is often the first to wobble when the scarcity behind it is directly targeted.** The refiner rally rested on a clear, narrow cause - a diesel supply shortage. A single policy announcement aimed at that exact shortage was enough to move the stocks immediately, which says something about how narrow the assumption underneath the rally really was.
- **Separate announcement risk from delivery risk.** An OFAC license doesn't mean Russian diesel shows up at a US gas station next week. Logistics, insurance, refinery repairs, and sanctions provisions set to expire on October 22 and October 31 all stand between the headline and any real change in supply.
- **Watch the spread, not just the commodity price.** Refiner earnings and stock prices respond far more directly to the crack spread - the gap between product and crude prices - than to the price of oil itself. The same drop in crude can affect refiner margins very differently depending on whether diesel supply is also increasing at the same time.
- **A one-sided announcement that the other party hasn't confirmed is not yet something to fully price in.** The same-day report of a fresh strike after the "ceasefire" announcement is a textbook example of a headline that can fade as quickly as it moved markets.

## FAQ

### If cheaper diesel is good for consumers, why did refiner stocks fall?
Refiners don't profit from the diesel price itself - they profit from the crack spread, the gap between what they pay for crude and what they can sell refined products for. More Russian diesel reaching the market could lower prices for consumers, but it also erodes the supply-scarcity premium refiners had been capturing. What's good news for drivers can be bad news for refiner margins at the same time.

### Will this deal actually bring down US gas and diesel prices soon?
Most analysts are skeptical of a large near-term effect. The volumes involved are small relative to global diesel demand, and getting Russian product into US markets requires logistics, insurance, and repairs to refineries damaged in the war - steps that take time. The sanctions relief is also temporary, running only through April 2027, and Russia's own export ban is due to expire October 31, so the arrangement could change again soon.

### What exactly is a "crack spread," and why does it matter more than the oil price alone?
It's the margin a refiner earns converting crude oil into products like diesel and gasoline. If a refiner buys crude at $90 a barrel and sells the diesel it produces for the equivalent of $150 a barrel, the crack spread is $60. That spread - not the standalone price of crude - is what most directly determines how profitable a refiner's operations actually are in any given quarter.
