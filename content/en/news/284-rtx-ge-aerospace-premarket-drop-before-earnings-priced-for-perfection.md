## What Happened

Before the opening bell on Tuesday, October 20, two of the most reliable earnings performers in the entire market - RTX Corporation (NYSE: RTX) and GE Aerospace (NYSE: GE) - both reported third-quarter 2026 results. What made the morning notable wasn't just the reports themselves. It was that both stocks, along with defense peer Northrop Grumman (NYSE: NOC), were already trading lower in premarket action before either company's numbers even hit the wire. RTX was indicated down roughly 1.9%, Northrop Grumman around 2%, and GE Aerospace about 0.3%, purely on positioning ahead of results that Wall Street broadly expected to be good.

That premarket softness looks strange on its face, given how consistently both companies have delivered. RTX has beaten earnings estimates in each of its last several quarters, and heading into Tuesday, analysts were modeling roughly $1.78 in adjusted earnings per share on about $24.1 billion in revenue for the quarter - both solidly above what the company was guiding to just a year ago. GE Aerospace's consensus sat near $2.00 per share on roughly $12.8 billion in revenue. Options markets, meanwhile, were pricing in unusually large same-day moves for both names: close to a 5.4% swing for RTX and 5.5% for GE Aerospace, according to derivatives pricing cited by Benzinga ahead of the open. A move of that size for two industrial giants with market capitalizations well into the hundreds of billions of dollars is a lot of implied volatility for stocks not normally associated with meme-stock-style swings.

The explanation traces back to what both companies did last quarter. In July, RTX posted adjusted earnings of $1.89 per share against a consensus of roughly $1.66 - a beat of nearly 14% - on revenue of $24.7 billion versus the $22.9 billion Wall Street expected. Management didn't just beat; it raised full-year adjusted EPS guidance to a range of $7.10 to $7.25, up from $6.70 to $6.90, lifted its revenue outlook to $95-96 billion, and pointed to a record backlog of $289 billion, up 22% year over year and split between $170 billion in commercial aerospace work and $119 billion in defense programs. GE Aerospace told a similar story: second-quarter adjusted EPS of $2.02 beat the roughly $1.86 analysts expected, revenue grew more than 21% year over year to $13.35 billion, and management again raised its full-year profit outlook. Both companies have now raised guidance multiple times across 2026, which is exactly the pattern that creates Tuesday's problem: each raise resets the bar higher for the next one, and a stock priced for continued acceleration has very little room for anything less than another clean beat.

## Why a Beat Isn't Automatically Bullish Anymore

The mechanism here is less about these two specific businesses and more about how markets price a long streak of good news. When a company beats and raises guidance once, the stock typically re-rates higher because the market updates its view of future earnings power. But when that pattern repeats quarter after quarter, something else starts happening: the next quarter's expected results get built into today's share price before the report even arrives. By the time Tuesday's session opened, RTX and GE Aerospace weren't really being evaluated against last year's numbers - they were being evaluated against the market's increasingly aggressive assumption about how much better this quarter would be.

This is precisely what happened to GE Aerospace earlier this year. In the first quarter of 2026, the company beat earnings estimates by roughly 13% - a number that, in isolation, should have been cause for celebration. Instead, the stock fell on the day of the report, as investors focused on margin pressure and supply-chain constraints buried in the details rather than the headline beat. The lesson repeated with RTX too: even after its strong July quarter, analysts and financial media have noted that RTX's own stock reactions to earnings have swung wildly in both directions over the past two years, rising sharply after some beats and falling sharply after others of similar magnitude. The size of the beat, in other words, stopped being the main variable. What mattered was whether the result cleared an increasingly elevated bar embedded in the stock price, and whether management's forward commentary suggested the acceleration could keep going.

Northrop Grumman's premarket decline, even though it wasn't reporting earnings itself that morning, reinforces that this is a sector-wide positioning dynamic rather than a company-specific verdict. Investors trade defense and aerospace names as a group, especially heading into a dense cluster of same-day reports, because a disappointment at RTX or GE Aerospace raises questions about demand, cost inflation, and parts availability across the entire sector's supply chain. When one bellwether wobbles, peers often get sold pre-emptively as a hedge, only to be bought back later if the read-through turns out to be benign. That is standard portfolio-level risk management playing out in real time, not necessarily a signal that Northrop's own fundamentals changed overnight.

There's also a structural reason this particular earnings season carries extra tension. Both RTX and GE Aerospace sit at the center of two of the most closely watched demand stories in the market right now: commercial aviation's recovery and global defense spending tied to ongoing geopolitical tensions, including the unresolved standoff over Iran and the Strait of Hormuz that has kept oil prices and defense-sector sentiment elevated for months. A soft quarter from either company would be read as evidence that one of those two growth engines is cooling, which is a much bigger worry for the stock than a routine miss would be in a less-scrutinized sector.

## What to Take Away From This

- **A beat-and-raise streak eventually prices itself into the stock.** After several consecutive quarters of raised guidance, the market stops comparing results to last year's numbers and starts comparing them to how much more acceleration investors have already assumed. The better the recent track record, the higher the bar for the next report.
- **Options-implied moves are a useful gauge of how much uncertainty is actually priced in.** A 5%+ expected swing for a large-cap industrial tells you the market expects today's number to matter a lot, regardless of which direction it lands.
- **A double-digit percentage earnings beat doesn't guarantee a stock goes up.** GE Aerospace's roughly 13% EPS beat in the first quarter of 2026 still ended in a lower stock price, because investors focused on margin and supply-chain commentary buried deeper in the report.
- **Watch how peers trade, not just the company reporting.** Northrop Grumman's premarket decline alongside RTX and GE Aerospace, without its own earnings catalyst that morning, is a sign investors were de-risking an entire sector ahead of a cluster of reports - a pattern worth recognizing before assuming any single stock's move is company-specific.
- **Backlog and guidance direction matter more than the trailing quarter's headline number.** With RTX's backlog at $289 billion and both companies having raised guidance repeatedly in 2026, the market's real question each quarter is whether that forward trajectory is still intact - not simply whether the latest three months beat a consensus estimate.

## FAQ

### Why would a stock fall before its own earnings are even released?
Premarket moves ahead of a scheduled earnings report usually reflect investors adjusting positions based on how much good news is already priced in, rather than new information about the results themselves. If a stock has beaten and raised guidance for several consecutive quarters, some investors choose to trim positions or hedge beforehand simply because the bar for "good enough" has gotten so high that even a solid number might not be enough to push the stock higher.

### Does a high options-implied move mean the stock is risky to own long-term?
Not necessarily. It mainly reflects how much short-term uncertainty traders see around that specific earnings date, not the company's underlying long-term risk. A stock like RTX or GE Aerospace can carry a large one-day implied move around earnings while still being viewed by long-term investors as a stable, well-backed industrial business, particularly given record order backlogs that provide years of revenue visibility.

### What would actually disappoint the market at this point, given how high expectations already are?
Based on the pattern from GE Aerospace's first-quarter report, the details that tend to matter most are margin trends, supply-chain and parts-delivery commentary, and whether full-year guidance moves up by as much as the market has already assumed. A clean beat on revenue and EPS with only an in-line guidance raise - rather than another meaningfully larger one - has been enough to disappoint investors in both stocks before.
