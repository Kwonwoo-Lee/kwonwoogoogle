## Delisting Is Final — So Why Is the Stock Still Trading?

When a stock gets a confirmed delisting notice, many new investors assume that's the end: no more trading, full stop. Then they open their broker's app and see the exact same stock still trading — and swinging by tens of percent in a single day. That's not a glitch. It's **clearance trading** (정리매매), a deliberately designed final stage of the delisting process. As covered in [administrative issue designation and delisting in Korea](/en/basics/administrative-issue-delisting-korea/), a Korean delisting is confirmed only after passing through administrative-issue designation and a listing-eligibility review — and even once that confirmation lands, the stock doesn't vanish from the market instantly. It gets one more short, defined trading window first. This lesson walks through why that window exists, why it runs on a completely different rulebook than ordinary trading, and why that rulebook tends to turn into a trap more often than an opportunity.

## What Clearance Trading Actually Is: A Designed Exit Window

Clearance trading is the mechanism the Korea Exchange (KRX) uses to give shareholders of a confirmed-delisting stock **one last chance to sell out entirely**. If a stock were simply frozen the moment delisting is confirmed, anyone still holding shares at that instant would be stuck with stock that could become completely worthless with no way to recover even a fraction of it. To prevent that, the exchange allows trading to continue for exactly **seven trading days** (business days only, so on the calendar this usually spans close to two weeks) after the delisting-confirmation notice. The stock traded during this window is labeled a "clearance-trading issue." Once those seven trading days end, the stock is permanently removed from the regular KOSPI or KOSDAQ market and can no longer be bought or sold through the exchange at all. The key framing to hold onto: clearance trading is not a procedure that might stop a delisting — it's the **last exit door** handed to shareholders after delisting has already been finalized.

## Why the Price-Limit Band Disappears: The 30-Minute Batch Auction

In ordinary KOSPI/KOSDAQ trading, the [price-limit band](/en/basics/price-limit-band-explained/) caps daily moves at ±30% from the prior close. During clearance trading, that cap is removed entirely. In its place, the trading method itself switches from the usual real-time continuous matching to a **30-minute batch auction**: roughly 13 times over the trading day, the exchange pools whatever buy and sell orders have accumulated and clears them all at once at a single matching price — the same mechanism covered in [call auctions and opening/closing prices](/en/basics/call-auction-opening-closing-price/), just repeated all day long instead of only at the open and close.

Why pair these two changes — no price band, and batch auctions — together? The logic runs like this. A stock heading into clearance trading is almost always one whose underlying business has already deteriorated badly or that has lost its listing qualifications outright, so the price the market actually agrees it's worth is usually far below the prior closing price. If the ordinary ±30% daily band still applied, it could take several consecutive down-limit days just to grind down to that "real" price within the tight seven-day window — and shareholders trying to sell might sit stuck behind a wall of sell orders at the daily floor, never actually getting filled. Removing the band lets the price jump straight to something closer to its true level in one move, maximizing the number of trading opportunities shareholders actually get to exit within those seven days. The 30-minute batch structure, meanwhile, exists because continuous real-time matching on a stock with this little remaining trading interest would let just a handful of orders whip the price around violently; pooling orders over a window before clearing them at least dampens that distortion somewhat.

## What the Numbers Actually Look Like — Why a "Rally" Is Often a Trap

Put those two design choices together and you get a genuinely paradoxical result. No price floor means there's nothing stopping a collapse — but thin trading volume also means a handful of buy orders can produce what looks, on a chart, like an explosive rally in a single day. Publicly reported cases bear this out: one KOSDAQ-listed stock closed its first day of clearance trading down more than 80% from the prior close, while another fell nearly 20% on day one of clearance trading only to surge over 180% the very next day. That surge wasn't the company's fundamentals suddenly improving overnight — it was simply a handful of orders moving a single-price auction on volume so thin that almost any order could swing it. One news report found that, for five stocks delisted in a given year, the average return from the first clearance-trading closing price to the final closing price was -57.4% — a figure specific to that particular sample of stocks and year, not a universal law, but one that's frequently cited because it captures a widely-held industry view: chasing a bounce during clearance trading tends to end in a deeper loss, not a recovered one, more often than not.

This structure also attracts opportunistic manipulation. Speculators sometimes nicknamed "clearance hunters" (정매꾼) exploit the thin order book by posting high buy bids to make the price look like it's climbing, luring in other investors drawn by the apparent rally, then dumping their own shares onto those buyers while quietly canceling the bids they'd posted. The usual market instinct — rising price plus rising volume signals improving demand — can flip into exactly the opposite signal in a market this thin, where a full day's worth of price action clears only 13 times total.

## Where the Stock Goes After Clearance Trading Ends

Once the seven trading days are up, the stock is removed from the KOSPI/KOSDAQ market entirely, and it can no longer be bought or sold through the exchange in any form. The underlying shares themselves still legally exist (shareholders retain their equity stake unless the company is fully liquidated), so some delisted companies end up registered on [K-OTC, Korea's over-the-counter market](/en/basics/k-otc-market-explained/), where trading continues in a much less formal, far thinner way — volume is typically minimal and price discovery barely functions. In rare cases, a company goes through [corporate rehabilitation/receivership](/en/basics/corporate-rehabilitation-receivership-korea/), restructures, and eventually passes a fresh listing review to relist — but this outcome is statistically extremely rare, and even when it happens, existing common shareholders typically see their stake heavily diluted or wiped out well before any relisting materializes. Buying into a clearance-trading stock on the hope that "it's cheap now and will relist later" is, in substance, placing an additional bet on top of a stake whose value has already largely evaporated, resting on a very low-probability outcome.

## What a Reasonable Investor Should Actually Do

Keep the core design purpose in mind — clearance trading exists to give *existing* shareholders a minimal chance to recover cash, not to offer anyone a chance at profit — and the right way to approach it follows naturally. If you were already holding the stock when the delisting notice landed, the structurally sound framing is to treat this window as "a chance to recover some portion of what's left in cash now," not as "a chance to recover the loss." Waiting for a rebound before selling runs directly against the fact that the window is fixed at seven trading days, after which selling through the exchange becomes impossible altogether. On the flip side, an investor who didn't already hold the stock and considers buying in after seeing the wild swings during clearance trading needs to recognize clearly that, given the removed price band and razor-thin liquidity described above, this is a fundamentally different kind of bet than ordinary stock trading — closer to speculation on a nearly-dead order book than an investment decision grounded in business value.

## Key Takeaways

- Clearance trading is a temporary, seven-trading-day window that lets shareholders of a confirmed-delisting stock sell out before it's permanently removed from the exchange.
- During this window, the usual ±30% price-limit band disappears and trading switches to roughly 13 daily 30-minute batch auctions — designed to let price converge quickly toward its "real" level, but also a structure where thin volume alone can produce wild swings.
- Rallies during clearance trading usually reflect thin order flow and opportunistic bid manipulation ("정매꾼"), not improving fundamentals — buying in to chase them is a structurally disadvantaged bet.
- Once clearance trading ends, the stock is permanently delisted; K-OTC trading or a rare rehabilitation-driven relisting remain technically possible afterward, but both the odds and the liquidity involved are very low.

## FAQ

### Could buying during clearance trading actually pay off if the company eventually relists?
It's not theoretically impossible, but relisting after a full delisting is statistically very rare. Most clearance-trading stocks end in either near-total loss of trading activity or outright liquidation, and even in the rare case a company goes through rehabilitation, existing common shareholders typically see their stake heavily diluted or largely wiped out along the way. Treat this as a low-probability, high-uncertainty bet, not a recovery strategy.

### If the price is rising during clearance trading, is that a buy signal?
In ordinary trading, rising price alongside rising volume is often read as a sign of improving demand. But clearance trading runs on extremely thin volume cleared only through 30-minute batch auctions, so a handful of orders can move the price sharply on their own. In that environment, price movement largely loses its usual meaning as a signal.

### Can I still place normal buy and sell orders through my broker's app during clearance trading?
You can place orders, but they won't fill the way you're used to. Instead of real-time continuous matching, orders only clear once every 30 minutes, when the exchange pools whatever has accumulated and settles it at a single price. Don't expect an order to fill the instant you submit it.
