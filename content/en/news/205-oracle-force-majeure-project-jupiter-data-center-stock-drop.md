## What Happened

Oracle (NYSE: ORCL) shares sold off hard on Thursday, September 24, sliding as much as 7% intraday and closing down roughly 3.5%. The trigger was a Bloomberg report that Oracle had sent a "force majeure" notice to the developer of its massive New Mexico AI data center project, known as Project Jupiter. A force majeure clause is a contractual escape hatch: when circumstances outside a party's control - natural disasters, war, or in this case, stalled government permitting - make it impossible to meet an obligation on schedule, the clause lets that party delay or avoid the obligation without being in breach. Oracle is reportedly using the notice to buy itself room to defer lease-style payments if the campus doesn't come online as originally planned in 2028.

Project Jupiter is a $165 billion data center campus under construction in Doña Ana County, near Santa Teresa, New Mexico, designed for roughly 2.45 gigawatts of power capacity - an enormous facility by any measure. It's one of the anchor projects inside the $500 billion Stargate AI infrastructure initiative that President Trump announced in January alongside OpenAI CEO Sam Altman, Oracle co-founder Larry Ellison, and SoftBank Chairman Masayoshi Son. The campus is being developed by a unit of Blue Owl Capital (NYSE: OWL), a major private credit firm, with a consortium of roughly 20 banks providing about $18 billion in project financing. News of the force majeure notice hit Blue Owl shares too, which fell around 4%, while Bloom Energy (NYSE: BE) - contracted to supply fuel cells and power generation equipment to the site - dropped roughly 6%, an even sharper decline.

The proximate cause of the delay is a natural gas pipeline permit dispute. Powering a facility of this scale reliably requires a new pipeline, dubbed the Green Chile Pipeline, and New Mexico's State Land Office denied the right-of-way needed to route it across state trust land. The federal Bureau of Land Management subsequently approved an alternate route across federal land, but the detour still cost at least six months, and regulatory filings point to an earliest possible in-service date of February 1, 2027 for the pipeline itself. Layered on top of the permitting fight, a nonprofit and local residents have separately sued the county over the industrial revenue bonds and tax abatements that made the project's economics work in the first place, adding another layer of local opposition to an already contested build.

## Why the Market Reacted So Sharply

Reading this purely as "one data center running late" doesn't explain a 3.5% single-day drop in a company with Oracle's market cap. What actually moved the stock is the broader financial picture surrounding Oracle's AI buildout. As of late May, Oracle was carrying roughly $130 billion in debt, and fiscal 2026 capital expenditures jumped 162% year over year to nearly $56 billion. That spending outpaced the cash the core business generates, pushing Oracle's free cash flow deficit to $23.7 billion, up sharply from a deficit of just $394 million the year before. Unlike Amazon, Microsoft, or Google, Oracle doesn't have a deep well of existing free cash flow to fund its AI infrastructure race, so it has leaned heavily on debt issuance and off-balance-sheet structures - special-purpose vehicles built with private credit partners like Blue Owl - to bridge the gap. The stock had already fallen more than 25% in the first half of 2026, marking its worst stretch since the 2001 dot-com bust, and remains down more than 50% from last year's record high. The cost of insuring Oracle's debt against default, measured by credit default swap spreads, had also climbed to record levels even before this week's news.

Against that backdrop, the force majeure notice reads as more than a routine legal maneuver. It compounds an existing worry - that some of Oracle's big AI customers might struggle to honor their own multi-year compute commitments - with a new one: Oracle itself now appears to be looking for contractual room to delay its own payment obligations. The legal mechanism is legitimate, but what unsettled investors was the type of risk being invoked. Force majeure clauses are typically reserved for genuinely unforeseeable events, not permitting delays that, while frustrating, were at least partly foreseeable given the local opposition the project had already drawn. Using the clause here reads as a signal that Oracle's capital spending commitments are tighter than the headline growth numbers suggest, and that the company is willing to use every available lever to push payments further out when it can.

The parallel declines in Blue Owl and Bloom Energy show this risk doesn't stop at Oracle's own balance sheet. The financing model behind the AI data center boom increasingly runs through private credit rather than traditional bank lending alone, with firms like Blue Owl fronting construction capital on the assumption that an anchor tenant - here, Oracle - will reliably pay rent-like fees for years to come. When that anchor tenant actually exercises a clause to delay payment, lenders suddenly face real questions about when they'll be repaid. Oracle's drop reflected operational risk - the project itself running behind schedule. Blue Owl's drop reflected credit risk - the possibility that expected cash flows to service its loans get pushed out. Two different transmission channels, same underlying news.

None of this means Oracle's fundamental AI story has collapsed. Just weeks earlier, the company posted fiscal 2027 first-quarter results showing remaining performance obligations (RPO) - its forward booked-revenue backlog - had surged 325% year over year to $664 billion, sending the stock up 7% on the day. The demand pipeline is still enormous by any historical standard. The open question is execution: how smoothly, and how close to budget, Oracle can actually build the physical data centers needed to convert that backlog into recognized revenue. A backlog of any size means little if the infrastructure behind it keeps slipping on permits, and if each slip is met with another deferred payment, the market will increasingly read that as a structural funding problem rather than a temporary growing pain.

## What to Take Away From This

- **Read the "why" behind a force majeure notice, not just the fact of it.** A clause invoked for a genuinely unforeseeable disaster carries very different implications than one invoked for a risk - like permit delays - that was at least partly foreseeable. The latter tends to signal tighter cash flow than the headlines suggest.
- **AI infrastructure exposure runs through more than the headline company.** Oracle's news moved a private credit lender (Blue Owl) and an equipment supplier (Bloom Energy) in the same session. Large data center projects are ecosystems of interlinked balance sheets, not single-company stories.
- **A big backlog and reliable execution are two different things.** Remaining performance obligations can grow 325% and still not translate into revenue on schedule if the physical build-out keeps stalling. Track both numbers, not just the more flattering one.
- **Credit market signals often move before equities do.** Oracle's credit default swap spreads had already reached record levels before this week's stock decline - a reminder that bond and derivatives markets can flag stress earlier than the share price does.
- **Debt-financed infrastructure booms deserve two separate report cards: growth story and balance-sheet resilience.** An exciting demand narrative doesn't automatically neutralize the risk embedded in how that growth is being financed.

## FAQ

### What exactly does a "force majeure" notice mean here?
It's a contractual mechanism that lets a party delay or avoid an obligation when events outside its control make normal performance impossible. In this case, Oracle is reportedly citing permitting delays at its New Mexico data center to buy room to defer payments it would otherwise owe if the facility doesn't launch on the original 2028 timeline.

### Why did Blue Owl Capital and Bloom Energy fall too, not just Oracle?
Project Jupiter links several companies together: Oracle as the anchor tenant, a Blue Owl Capital unit as developer and owner, a consortium of roughly 20 banks as lenders, and Bloom Energy as an equipment supplier. Oracle's ability to delay payments raises credit risk for Blue Owl's lenders and creates equipment-order uncertainty for Bloom Energy, so the same headline hit each stock through a different mechanism.

### Does this mean Oracle's AI business is in trouble?
Not necessarily. Oracle's most recent quarterly results showed remaining performance obligations up 325% year over year to $664 billion, indicating the demand pipeline remains historically strong. This episode is better understood as evidence that turning that backlog into actual revenue depends on physical infrastructure that can still run into serious permitting and financing friction.

### Is this an Oracle-specific problem or an industry-wide one?
Oracle's unusually high reliance on debt makes this more acute for Oracle specifically, but the underlying financing model - using private credit and off-balance-sheet vehicles to fund data center construction - is now common across the AI infrastructure industry. That's why this episode is being watched as a cautionary signal well beyond Oracle alone.
