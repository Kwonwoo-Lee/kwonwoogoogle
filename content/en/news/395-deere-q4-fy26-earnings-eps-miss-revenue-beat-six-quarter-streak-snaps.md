## What Happened

Before the close on Wednesday, November 25, Deere & Company (NYSE: DE), the world's largest farm equipment maker, reported fiscal fourth-quarter 2026 results (for the quarter ended in late October). The report landed on one of the roughest trading days of the year - the same session in which a hotter-than-expected October core PCE inflation reading and a fresh White House announcement of 20% tariffs on 14 trading partners sent the Dow tumbling more than 1,000 points intraday (see our earlier coverage: [Dow Drops 1,000 Points, Nasdaq Plunges Over 4% as Hot PCE Meets New Trump Tariffs](/en/news/stocks-sink-october-pce-hot-trump-tariffs-nvidia-nasdaq-correction/)). With the entire market convulsing that day, Deere's own numbers risked getting lost in the noise - which is a shame, because the details were genuinely interesting.

Non-GAAP diluted EPS came in at $3.89. Depending on the source, Wall Street had been looking for something in the $4.03-$4.07 range, so by any measure this was an earnings miss. That matters symbolically: Deere had strung together six consecutive quarters of beating EPS estimates, and this report snapped that streak. Revenue, however, told a very different story. Sales came in at roughly $12.1 billion (specifically $12.115 billion), topping the roughly $10.3-$10.4 billion analysts had modeled by more than 15%. In other words, Deere delivered a scorecard that pointed in two opposite directions at once - a clear EPS miss paired with a sizable revenue beat.

For the full fiscal year, net income attributable to Deere came in at $4.86 billion, or $17.95 per diluted share, down 3% from the prior year's $5.027 billion ($18.50 per share). Full-year net sales and revenues, by contrast, rose 4% to $47.7 billion - again, revenue growing while profit shrank, the same pattern that showed up in the quarter. Alongside the results, the company issued fiscal 2027 net income guidance of $4.5 billion to $5.25 billion, explicitly noting that this range already bakes in a one-time, non-cash pension settlement charge of roughly $0.25 billion to $0.40 billion.

In premarket trading the day after the report - the session ahead of the Thanksgiving holiday - Deere shares actually rose about 2.71%, climbing into the $630 range. A quarter that missed on the bottom line got a "buy" response from the market. Unpacking why requires looking past the headline EPS number.

## Why the Stock Rose Despite the EPS Miss

The first thing worth noting is the sheer size of the revenue surprise. Analysts had modeled roughly $10.3-$10.4 billion in sales; Deere delivered $12.1 billion - a gap of nearly $1.8 billion, or more than 15%. A beat of that magnitude is hard to explain away as a pricing quirk or a one-off accounting item; it reads as a signal that actual underlying demand (unit sales, dealer inventory drawdown) ran meaningfully ahead of what the market expected. That, in turn, suggests the EPS miss likely traces back to cost-side items - tariffs, production costs, one-time charges - rather than to any weakness in pricing or demand itself.

Second, this report reinforced the "2026 marks the bottom of the large-ag cycle" narrative that Deere's management has been repeating since its fiscal 2025 Q4 report a year earlier. Back then, the company guided fiscal 2026 net income to a range of $4.0 billion to $4.75 billion. The actual full-year result - $4.86 billion - landed near, and slightly above, the top end of that range. The fact that a cycle Deere itself had flagged as "the bottom" actually bottomed out roughly where (or slightly better than) management predicted gives investors a concrete reason to believe the next phase is more likely to be a recovery than a further decline. Several market commentators framed the revenue beat specifically as evidence of stabilization at the cycle trough, rather than continued deterioration.

Third, the composition of the EPS miss matters a great deal. The $0.25 billion to $0.40 billion pension settlement charge baked into the fiscal 2027 guidance is explicitly a one-time, non-cash item - not a recurring hit to operating profitability. Investors evaluating a company's core earning power typically strip out charges like this, and the fact that management proactively disclosed and quantified it at the time of guidance tends to be read as a point in favor of transparency, not a red flag. It supports a narrative of "the core business isn't deteriorating - the headline number just looks lower because of a known, isolated item," which is a very different story from a margin problem showing up broadly across the business.

Fourth is the path of expectations heading into the print. Deere had spent much of this fiscal year warning investors about roughly $1.2 billion in annual tariff-related cost headwinds. By the time this report landed, the market had likely already priced in some expectation that tariffs would make the numbers messy. Against that backdrop, a revenue figure that beat by more than 15% carried an even more important message than the headline would suggest: demand is holding up despite the tariff drag, not despite some assumption that demand itself was weakening.

It's also worth considering Deere's segment mix. The company's business splits roughly into Production & Precision Ag (the large tractors and combines that get the most market attention), Small Ag & Turf, and Construction & Forestry. For the past couple of years, the market's focus has been fixed almost entirely on large ag, where persistently depressed grain prices have pushed farmers to delay replacing expensive equipment. Deere has responded with a deliberate underproduction strategy since fiscal 2025 - intentionally running large-ag output below retail demand to work down bloated dealer inventories, a move that hurts near-term revenue and profit but is generally viewed as the healthier long-term choice compared to entering the next up-cycle with inventories still too high. Whether this quarter's revenue surprise came mainly from Construction & Forestry and Small Ag & Turf, or from large-ag demand reawakening faster than expected, will only become fully clear once segment-level detail is parsed in more depth. Either way, the market's positive reaction to the top-line beat suggests concerns that inventory destocking was dragging on indefinitely have eased somewhat.

Deere's results also function as a bellwether for the broader agricultural and construction equipment sector. Its numbers and guidance tend to ripple - indirectly - into shares of rivals like AGCO and CNH Industrial, as well as suppliers of farm equipment parts and tires. Farmer capital spending intentions are highly sensitive to three variables: grain prices, interest rates, and tariffs. A revenue beat of this size is, at minimum, an indirect signal that this combination of variables may be shaping up somewhat less badly than the market had feared.

## What to Take Away From This

A snapped streak doesn't automatically mean bad news for the stock. Ending six consecutive quarters of EPS beats sounds like a negative headline in isolation, but the market responded far more strongly to the scale of the revenue surprise and the demand signal embedded in it. Reacting to a single headline number without digging into what's actually inside it is a common way to misread a stock's next move.

Decompose the "why" behind an earnings miss before assuming the worst. An EPS miss driven by a disclosed, one-time, non-cash charge is a fundamentally different story than one caused by structural deterioration in the core business - and the market tends to price these two scenarios very differently once the distinction becomes clear.

In cyclical industries, the moment a previously forecast trough is actually confirmed by real numbers can function as an important inflection point. Deere had been calling fiscal 2026 the bottom of the ag cycle for a full year before this report; landing at or slightly above the top of its own guidance range gave that forecast credibility and reinforced expectations of a turn toward recovery.

When a major macro shock (like this day's PCE/tariff selloff) dominates headlines, individual company results can easily get buried in the noise. On days like that, it's worth deliberately separating the index-level story from a specific company's own earnings reaction rather than assuming one explains the other.

## FAQ

### In one sentence, why did Deere's stock rise despite missing EPS estimates?
Revenue beat consensus by more than 15%, a meaningful chunk of the EPS miss traced back to an already-disclosed one-time pension charge, and the results reinforced management's year-old call that fiscal 2026 would mark the bottom of the large-ag cycle - together, these outweighed the headline EPS shortfall in investors' eyes.

### Why does the pension settlement charge in the fiscal 2027 guidance matter?
The $0.25 billion to $0.40 billion charge is a one-time, non-cash item tied to settling a pension obligation, not a recurring operating expense. Investors typically evaluate a company's core earning power by excluding charges like this, and the fact that management disclosed and quantified it upfront, at the time guidance was issued, is generally viewed as a transparency positive rather than a warning sign.

### Does Deere's report tell us anything about other farm or construction equipment stocks?
To some degree, yes. As the largest player in the industry, Deere's results and guidance often serve as an indirect read-through for competitors like AGCO and CNH Industrial, as well as companies supplying farm equipment parts and tires. That said, each company has a different product mix and geographic exposure, so Deere's numbers are better treated as a directional signal for sector-wide demand than as a direct proxy for any single peer's results.
