## What Happened

Over two consecutive sessions - Thursday, November 19 and Friday, November 20 - a pair of AI-adjacent stocks came under heavy pressure for two very different reasons. One was hit by a specific allegation from an activist short seller. The other gave back most of a historic debut-day rally on no new company-specific news at all. Looking at both side by side shows how a single headline like "AI stocks fell today" can be masking two completely different mechanisms underneath.

Start with AMD (Nasdaq: AMD). On Thursday, Hunterbrook Media, an activist short-selling research outfit, published a report titled "AMD has its own China problem." Its core claim: IEIT Systems, a Chinese server maker that the U.S. Commerce Department added to its export blacklist back in March 2025, continued running newer AMD firmware well after that listing - firmware Hunterbrook says is more than 99% identical to AMD's own code. The report also flagged that China and Hong Kong billings account for roughly $7.8 billion of AMD's fiscal 2025 revenue, or about 22% of the total. Shares sank 7.7% on the news Thursday, closing at $489.19. The decline didn't stop there: in Friday's pre-market session, AMD fell roughly another 7%, pushing the two-day cumulative loss past 14%.

That same Friday produced a second, unrelated decline. Anthropic (Nasdaq: ANT), which had made its Nasdaq debut just one day earlier, saw its stock give back most of its opening-day gains. Anthropic had priced its IPO at $83 a share - above its original $70-$78 marketed range - raising $81 billion in what became the largest IPO on record, surpassing SpaceX. On debut day Thursday, shares opened 20% above the IPO price and closed up 17.1% at $97.17. On Friday, ANT fell roughly 10% intraday, dropping into the $86-$87 range. That's still comfortably above the $83 IPO price, but it erased nearly all of the gain the stock had built up in its first session.

Both declines happened on the same day, under the same broad umbrella of "AI stock weakness." But the engines driving them are distinct. One was triggered by a pointed, unverified accusation against a single company. The other reflects a sector-wide mood shift bleeding into a brand-new listing that, on its own fundamentals, hadn't changed at all in 24 hours. Put together, the pair is a useful case study in why not every red day in AI-adjacent stocks means the same thing.

## Why Two Stocks Fell on the Same Day for Different Reasons

To understand the AMD move, it helps to know who Hunterbrook actually is. The report explicitly disclosed that Hunterbrook Capital, Hunterbrook Media's affiliated investment fund, holds a short position in AMD - meaning the entity publishing the allegations stands to profit directly if the stock falls. This is what's commonly called an "activist short seller" setup. That doesn't automatically make the claims false, but it is a conflict of interest that any reader needs to weigh differently than they would a neutral third-party analysis. Tellingly, Hunterbrook itself acknowledged in the report that it couldn't establish how IEIT Systems obtained the firmware code, or whether a valid license existed covering its use. In other words, this is an unproven allegation, not a confirmed violation. AMD pushed back directly, with a spokesperson saying the report "misrepresents" the company's business practices and relationships and asserting that AMD "complies with all applicable export control laws."

Even so, the market reacted fast and kept reacting for a second straight day, for two reasons. First, chip export controls to China are a well-worn pressure point this year - Nvidia and other large semiconductor names have already been whipsawed multiple times by export-restriction news and allegations of circumvention, so investors have effectively learned to treat a fresh allegation in the same category as "sell first, verify later." Second, resolving whether the claim is true takes time. Until the Commerce Department, Congress, or some other authority actually investigates - and there's no guarantee that happens quickly, or at all - the market sits in a window of uncertainty, and markets tend to price in the worst-case outcome by default during that window rather than wait patiently for clarity. Friday's additional 7% slide likely reflects both a delayed reaction to Thursday's report and investors trimming uncertain positions heading into the weekend.

Anthropic's decline works through an entirely different channel: there was no fresh negative news about the company itself. To make sense of the second-day drop, it helps to zoom out to a pattern seen repeatedly after blockbuster IPOs. SpaceX, Cerebras, and other recent mega-listings have all shown a similar arc - a large first-day pop followed by a meaningful give-back over the following days or weeks. First-day prices tend to form under distorted supply-and-demand conditions: the float available for immediate selling is limited, while demand from investors anxious not to miss a hyped listing is often outsized. That combination builds a scarcity premium into the opening price that sits on top of whatever the "fair" long-term valuation might be. As more shares change hands and the initial excitement cools over subsequent sessions, that premium has historically tended to fade.

Layered on top of that pattern, Friday happened to be a day when the broader chip and AI sector - AMD very much included - was under pressure. Nothing changed about Anthropic's actual business, but a sector correlation effect kicked in: when other AI-adjacent names wobble, a freshly listed AI company tends to wobble too, almost by association. That sensitivity is amplified by Anthropic's own valuation, which came in around $1.5 trillion even at the IPO price - a figure that only makes sense if investors assume extremely optimistic future revenue growth. A valuation built on that kind of assumption is inherently fragile to any uptick in broader AI skepticism, even skepticism that has nothing directly to do with Anthropic. On the other hand, the fact that shares are still trading above the $83 IPO price means investors who got shares at the offering price haven't actually lost money yet - they've simply watched a chunk of an extraordinary first-day gain melt away.

## What to Take Away From This

- **A shared headline doesn't mean a shared cause.** AMD's decline was an idiosyncratic, single-company event triggered by a specific allegation. Anthropic's was a sector-sentiment effect bleeding into a new listing. Just because two stocks fell on the same day under the same "AI stocks slide" headline doesn't mean the same underlying driver is at work.
- **Treat short-seller reports as allegations, not verdicts** - especially when the publisher discloses its own short position, as Hunterbrook did here. That's a textbook conflict of interest. Before reacting to the initial sell-off, it's worth checking the company's rebuttal and watching for any actual regulatory follow-through, rather than assuming the claim is settled fact.
- **A huge first-day IPO pop is not, by itself, a buy signal.** As SpaceX and other recent mega-listings have shown, an outsized opening gain can reflect temporary scarcity and hype rather than a durable re-rating. Give a freshly listed stock's first several sessions room to find its real level before treating any single day's move as meaningful.
- **Richly valued stocks are more exposed to sector mood swings.** A name like Anthropic, priced on aggressive growth assumptions, can get dragged down by bad news affecting an entirely different company in the same sector. The higher the multiple, the more a stock behaves like "the whole theme" rather than just itself.
- **Markets tend to price in worst-case scenarios while uncertainty is unresolved.** AMD's continued slide on Friday happened before anyone could confirm or deny the underlying allegation. Expect volatility to persist in situations like this until there's an actual resolution - a regulatory finding, an independent audit, or further disclosure - rather than assuming the initial sell-off fully reflects the truth.

## FAQ

### What is Hunterbrook, and why does its short position matter?
Hunterbrook Media is a research and publishing outfit that investigates and reports on companies, while its affiliated fund, Hunterbrook Capital, trades on those findings. In the AMD report, Hunterbrook disclosed that Hunterbrook Capital holds a short position in AMD - meaning it profits financially if the stock falls after the report's release. That doesn't make the underlying claims untrue, but it's a clear conflict of interest that sets this apart from a neutral, disinterested analysis, and it's a reason to treat the claims with extra scrutiny until independently verified.

### Has it been confirmed that AMD actually violated export control rules?
No. As of now, this remains an unverified allegation from a short seller, not a confirmed violation. Hunterbrook itself acknowledged it could not determine how IEIT Systems obtained the AMD firmware or whether a valid license covered its use. AMD has formally denied wrongdoing and says it complies with all applicable export regulations. Whether any violation actually occurred would need to be established through an investigation by the Commerce Department or another relevant authority.

### Is Anthropic stock still trading above its IPO price?
Yes. Even after Friday's roughly 10% intraday decline, Anthropic shares were trading in the $86-$87 range, above the $83 IPO price. That said, the stock has pulled back significantly from Thursday's debut-day close of $97.17.

### Is it common for newly listed stocks to fall on their second or third day of trading?
Yes, particularly for mega-IPOs that posted large first-day gains. Immediately after listing, the tradable float is often limited while demand runs hot, which tends to build a temporary premium into the opening price. As more trading days pass and that initial scarcity eases, the premium has historically tended to fade - a pattern seen repeatedly in prior large IPOs such as SpaceX's.
