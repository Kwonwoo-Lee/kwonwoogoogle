## Same Company, Same Credit Rating — So Why Does One Bond Get Called "Green"?

You may have seen a headline where a company issues an ordinary corporate bond rated AA, and on the same day issues another bond with the same rating and maturity, except this one carries the label "green bond." Both are debt owed by the same company, with the same credit rating and the same maturity. So why label one differently, and why do investors sometimes flock to it? As covered in the [credit spreads lesson](/en/basics/credit-spreads-explained/), a bond's price is fundamentally a reflection of the issuer's default risk. A green bond doesn't change that default risk at all — what it changes is a tag attached to one question: where does this money actually go. This lesson covers what that single tag actually does to demand and pricing, and the controversy over whether the tag can be trusted.

## What a Green Bond Is: Defined by Use of Proceeds, Not Collateral

A green bond is a bond where the issuer commits to spending the raised funds on environmentally focused projects — renewable energy, EV charging infrastructure, energy-efficiency retrofits, green buildings, and similar categories. The key word is "restricted use." An ordinary corporate bond lets the company spend the proceeds on whatever it needs, from working capital to refinancing existing debt. A green bond, by contrast, has to specify in its offering documents exactly which project categories the money will fund, and the issuer has to report periodically after issuance on whether the money was actually spent that way. Everything else about the bond — repayment seniority, collateral, the issuer's overall financial structure — is identical to an ordinary bond from the same company. If the issuer defaults, green bond holders take losses alongside every other bondholder at the same seniority; it is not a project-finance structure where repayment depends on whether the specific green project itself generates revenue. The debt is still backed by the issuer's full credit, full stop — the only thing that changes is where the money is earmarked to go and what has to be disclosed about it afterward.

The framework that standardized this globally is the Green Bond Principles (GBP), published by the International Capital Market Association (ICMA). It carries no legal force — it's a voluntary guideline — but it sets out four core components: (1) proceeds must go to clearly defined green projects, (2) the process for selecting those projects must be transparent, (3) proceeds must be tracked in a dedicated account, and (4) the issuer must report regularly after issuance on how the funds were used and what environmental impact resulted. Many issuers hire an outside reviewer to verify compliance with these four points — a "second-party opinion" — and disclose that verification alongside the bond itself. The market has grown steadily since the European Investment Bank and World Bank issued some of the earliest green bonds in the late 2000s, with government agencies, utilities and financial institutions now among the most frequent issuers worldwide.

## The Greenium: Why the Same Risk Can Trade at a Slightly Lower Yield

A **greenium** — a blend of "green" and "premium" — refers to a green bond trading at a lower yield than an otherwise-identical conventional bond from the same issuer at the same time. Since bond prices and yields move inversely, a lower yield means the green bond is priced higher, which means the issuer effectively borrows slightly more cheaply. This happens because of supply and demand. As more pension funds, insurers and dedicated ESG funds either require or prefer holding assets that satisfy environmental mandates, the supply of bonds that can check that box hasn't always kept pace with demand. When that pool of capital concentrates on green-labeled bonds carrying the exact same credit risk as a conventional alternative, the extra demand pushes the price up and the yield down.

That said, the measured size of the greenium is consistently smaller than most investors assume. Studies from several central banks and investment banks converge on a figure in the single-digit basis points (1bp = 0.01 percentage point) range, rarely exceeding 10bp. Some studies report an average gap of around 3-4bp; in some issuances the greenium is close to zero or even reverses. A bigger complication is separating how much of that small gap is really about the green label versus other factors entirely. A company's first-ever green bond issuance, for instance, can attract extra investor attention independent of its environmental merits, and during periods when investors are chasing safe assets broadly, government and government-adjacent bonds can move for reasons that have nothing to do with being green, distorting any comparison. The honest summary: the greenium is a real, measurable phenomenon, but it's small and has generally been narrowing — it is not a mechanism that hands issuers a large guaranteed discount just for attaching a green label.

## The Greenium in Numbers

Imagine a state-owned utility issues two five-year bonds on the same day. The conventional bond prices at a 4.00% yield, while the green bond — same credit rating, same maturity — prices at 3.96%. That 0.04 percentage point (4bp) gap is the greenium. On a $100 million issuance, that works out to roughly $40,000 saved per year in interest, or about $200,000 over the five-year life of the bond. For the issuer, that's a modest but real reduction in borrowing cost. For the investor, it means accepting a slightly lower yield on the exact same credit risk, in exchange for holding an asset that satisfies ESG allocation requirements. As this example shows, the greenium is far too small a number to be the main driver of investment returns — investors generally choose green bonds for non-financial reasons, like portfolio mandates or environmental impact, rather than chasing the yield difference itself.

## The Greenwashing Debate: The Gap Between the Label and the Reality

As the green bond market has grown, the most persistent criticism has been **greenwashing** — the practice of marketing proceeds as environmentally dedicated while the money either refinances debt from projects that were already underway anyway, or gets loosely attached to projects whose environmental benefit is murky at best. A Climate Bonds Initiative review found that less than 60% of green bond issuers fully disclosed both how proceeds were used and what environmental impact resulted. Part of the structural weakness here is that disclosure obligations rest on a voluntary framework — the Green Bond Principles — rather than binding law. Even where an outside reviewer provides a second-party opinion, that review often only confirms that the bond meets the formal checklist of the Green Bond Principles, not that the underlying project actually achieved a rigorously measured reduction in emissions. To address this gap, the European Union introduced the legally binding European Green Bond Standard (EuGB) in 2023, which requires that a substantial share of proceeds fund activities that qualify under the EU's Taxonomy Regulation — a far stricter bar than the voluntary GBP framework.

## How It Differs from Social Bonds, Sustainability Bonds, and Sustainability-Linked Bonds

The broader category of ESG-labeled bonds includes several distinct structures, and the line between them comes down to two questions: where does the money go, and is the issuer's behavior itself tied to the bond's terms? A **social bond** works exactly like a green bond except the earmarked projects are social rather than environmental — affordable housing, healthcare access, job creation. A **sustainability bond** blends both categories, funding a mix of environmental and social projects. All three share the same underlying architecture: a "use-of-proceeds" structure that tracks where the money flows.

A **sustainability-linked bond (SLB)** is built on an entirely different logic. Proceeds carry no restriction whatsoever — the issuer can spend the money on anything. Instead, the bond's terms are tied to a company-wide key performance indicator (KPI) the issuer commits to hitting by a set date, something like "cut greenhouse gas emissions 30% by 2030 versus a 2020 baseline." If the issuer misses that target, the bond typically carries a penalty clause — a coupon step-up — that raises the interest rate for the remainder of the bond's life. In short: a green bond is a promise about where the money goes, while an SLB is a promise that the whole company will be penalized with a higher interest rate if it fails to deliver on a company-wide commitment. One structure controls the path of the money; the other controls the consequences of the company's overall behavior. The two philosophies are genuinely different tools.

The table below summarizes the four structures side by side.

| Type | Restricts use of proceeds? | What's tied to the interest rate | Penalty for missing the goal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Green bond | Yes — environmental projects only | Nothing | None |
| Social bond | Yes — social projects only | Nothing | None |
| Sustainability bond | Yes — environmental + social | Nothing | None |
| Sustainability-linked bond (SLB) | No — general purpose | Company-wide KPI achievement | Coupon step-up |

This split explains a pattern in who issues what: utilities and infrastructure operators, which can point to a specific, identifiable project (a wind farm, a transit line), lean toward green bonds. Manufacturers and oil refiners, whose environmental footprint is harder to tie to any single project but easier to express as a company-wide emissions target, lean toward SLBs instead.

## What Investors Should Actually Check

When evaluating a green bond, or an ESG fund that holds them, the specificity of disclosure matters far more than the yield gap. What matters is whether the issuer publishes the actual list of funded projects, whether the second-party opinion gets refreshed after issuance rather than appearing only once at launch, and whether quantitative environmental outcomes — tons of emissions avoided, megawatts of capacity added — are actually reported, rather than just a formal "green" label. For SLBs, the question shifts to whether the KPI target is genuinely ambitious or something the issuer would likely hit anyway with no real effort; a loosely set target makes the penalty clause little more than decoration. In either case, a green bond or SLB still carries the issuer's full credit risk — the ESG label is never a substitute for the ordinary diligence of checking [credit ratings](/en/basics/credit-rating-agencies-explained/) and financial health.

## Key Takeaways

- A green bond carries the same credit rating, maturity and repayment seniority as an ordinary bond from the same issuer — the only thing restricted is what the proceeds can be spent on.
- The greenium is the phenomenon of green bonds pricing at a lower yield than an identical conventional bond, but the measured gap is typically just a few basis points and has been narrowing over time.
- The greenwashing debate stems from a structural weakness: disclosure of how proceeds are used and what impact resulted rests on a voluntary framework with limited enforcement.
- Sustainability-linked bonds (SLBs) take a fundamentally different approach from green bonds — rather than restricting use of proceeds, they tie the interest rate to whether the issuer hits a company-wide performance target.

## FAQ

### Is a green bond safer than a regular bond?
No. Credit risk is determined by the issuer's overall financial health, and the green label doesn't reduce default risk at all. If the funded project fails to generate revenue, the issuer still has to repay the bond out of its general business, exactly like an ordinary corporate bond.

### Does the greenium mean green bond investors lose money?
In the narrow sense of accepting a slightly lower yield, yes — but the gap is very small, and many ESG-mandated investors care more about meeting portfolio-level environmental requirements than about that yield difference. It's more accurate to call it a small trade-off between a non-financial goal and yield than a "loss."

### Can individual investors buy green bonds directly?
Some green bonds issued by financial institutions are available to retail investors through over-the-counter bond channels, but the bulk of most issuances is allocated to institutional investors. Retail investors more commonly get exposure indirectly, through bond funds or ETFs that hold green bonds.
