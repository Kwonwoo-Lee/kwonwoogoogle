Maybe your company asked, right before its IPO, "how much do you want to put into the employee stock ownership plan?" Or maybe you've seen an IPO disclosure stating that 20% of the offering was set aside for the company's own employees. Most employees treat this as a perk — a bonus the company is handing out. In reality, it's a specific investment structure that ties both your monthly paycheck and your savings to the fate of a single stock: the one issued by the company that also pays your salary. This lesson doesn't cover when to sell employee stock for the best return — that kind of timing call belongs to the trading-strategies course. Here, the focus is on how Korea's employee stock ownership plan (우리사주조합, literally "our company stock association") actually works, and why a program designed with the good intention of making employees "owners of the company" can, in certain situations, do the opposite of diversifying risk.

## What the Plan Is: Employees Becoming Shareholders of Their Own Company

Korea's employee stock ownership plan is established under the Employee Welfare Framework Act, under which a company's employees voluntarily form an association ("woorisaju johap") to acquire and hold shares of their own employer. The idea rests on two pillars. One is that turning employees from pure salary-earners into shareholders creates an incentive to share in the company's performance and cooperate with management rather than simply clock in. The other is that it functions as a long-term wealth-building benefit for staff. In practice, this plays out when a company goes public, or conducts a follow-on offering after listing, and the association requests a portion of the offered shares on behalf of its member-employees. Unlike a retail IPO subscription, where each investor applies individually through a brokerage account, the association applies and receives the allocation collectively, as a single entity representing the company's workforce.

## Why 20% of an IPO Goes to Employees First

Korea's Capital Markets Act allows companies listing on the KOSPI (the main board) to set aside up to 20% of an IPO's shares for priority allocation to the employee stock ownership association, and in practice most KOSPI listings allocate close to that ceiling. KOSDAQ-listed companies, by contrast, are under no such obligation, so employee participation in KOSDAQ IPOs tends to be smaller and far more inconsistent. The logic behind giving employees first access is similar to the general-investor allocation covered in [IPO Subscription: Equal vs. Proportional Allocation](/en/basics/ipo-subscription-equal-proportional-allocation/). An IPO's shares are typically split among institutional investors, the employee stock ownership association, and retail subscribers, and the employee tranche exists specifically to give long-term incentive shares to the people who presumably understand the business best — its own staff. If employees apply for less than the full 20% ceiling, the unsubscribed portion rolls over to retail investors. Crucially, participation itself is a choice: employees decide individually whether to subscribe and how much, rather than being assigned shares automatically.

## The One-Year Lock-Up: Unable to Sell Even If You Want To

Shares allocated through the employee ownership association come with a condition that ordinary IPO shares don't: a mandatory holding period, typically one year from listing, during which the shares cannot be sold. Where a retail investor can trade freely from day one, employee-allocated shares are held in custody at the Korea Securities Depository and are mechanically blocked from sale for that period. The rationale matches the program's stated purpose: without a lock-up, employees could subscribe purely to flip the shares for a quick post-listing gain, defeating the point of a long-term ownership incentive. There's an important exception, though — if an employee leaves the company during the lock-up period, the condition that assumed continued employment no longer applies, and the shares can be sold even before the year is up. For an employee who stays, however, there's no such escape hatch: if the stock falls well below the IPO price, that employee simply has to wait out the year regardless of how badly they'd like to cut the loss. Korean media has repeatedly reported on IPO stocks that fell under the offering price shortly after listing, leaving employee shareholders locked in and watching paper losses for the full year with no way to act on them.

## Leveraged Employee Stock Purchases: Doubling the Risk With Borrowed Money

Not every employee funds their subscription out of savings. When the allocated amount is large relative to an employee's cash on hand, or the offering price is high, some associations arrange loans — often through the company itself or an affiliated financial institution — so employees can borrow the purchase funds. This turns the purchase into a leveraged position, structurally similar to the [margin trading](/en/basics/margin-trading-leverage/) covered elsewhere in this course, except the borrower is buying their own employer's stock rather than a stock of their choosing. The risk compounds if the price falls below the offering price: the employee is now absorbing a loss on the shares themselves while still owing interest, and in some cases principal, on the loan used to buy them. Korean news coverage of leveraged employee stock purchases has repeatedly used phrases along the lines of "stuck paying interest while the stock falls," which captures exactly this double bind. It's also worth noting that the lender in many of these arrangements is the employee's own company or an institution tied to it — meaning a downturn in the company's business can hit employment, the stock price, and the loan repayment obligation all at once.

## Tax Benefits Exist, But the Fine Print Changes Often

Korea's Special Tax Treatment Control Act provides income-tax deductions, up to certain limits, for amounts employees contribute to the stock ownership association, and in the past there have also been additional income-tax reduction schemes tied to how long shares were held. These deduction caps and reduction rates have been revised multiple times through tax-law amendments, and the rules applied to small and medium-sized enterprises have sometimes differed from those for larger companies. Rather than quoting a specific deduction limit or reduction percentage here — numbers that may well be out of date by the time you read this — the safer takeaway is simply that a tax benefit exists in principle. Anyone actually considering a subscription should confirm the year's exact limits with the National Tax Service, the company's HR or finance department, or the association's own governing rules before deciding.

## How Stock Options and RSUs Differ From This

Employee ownership plans are often confused with stock options and RSUs (restricted stock units), which produce a similar-looking result — employees ending up holding company shares — through a completely different mechanism. As covered in [Stock-Based Compensation and Dilution](/en/basics/stock-based-compensation-dilution/), a stock option is a right the company grants to buy shares at a fixed future price, and an RSU is a grant of shares outright once a vesting period passes. In both cases, the employee doesn't pay cash up front; the company issues new shares or hands over treasury shares as compensation. The employee ownership plan works differently: at subscription time, the employee actually pays real money — their own or borrowed — to buy shares at the IPO price, just like any other investor in the offering. Because stock options and RSUs are compensation, a falling stock price doesn't cost the employee money they've already spent (an underwater stock option can simply be left unexercised). An employee ownership plan, by contrast, makes the employee an actual buyer, so a price drop below the offering price produces a real, out-of-pocket paper loss exactly like it would for anyone else who bought the stock. Treating employee stock purchases as "just another company perk" obscures this distinction.

## The Concentration Risk, in Numbers

Consider an employee earning 60 million won a year who puts 20 million won — essentially their entire savings beyond a housing deposit — into the employee ownership allocation at their company's IPO. That employee's financial position now looks like this: both their monthly income (salary) and their accumulated savings (the stock) are tied to the fortunes of the same single company. When the company does well, a raise and a rising stock price arrive together, compounding the good news. When it struggles, the same compounding works in reverse — a round of layoffs can hit at the exact moment the stock is falling, and the lock-up means that stock can't even be sold for cash during the crunch. As covered in [Correlation and Diversification](/en/basics/correlation-and-diversification/), the whole point of diversification is combining assets that don't move in the same direction to reduce overall risk. An employee's salary, however, is already about as tightly linked to the employer's fortunes as an asset can be. Adding a concentrated equity stake in that same employer on top of it isn't diversification — it's stacking the identical risk a second time.

## The Lesson From Enron: Being an "Owner" vs. Being "All In" on One Company

The most extreme illustration that this risk isn't merely theoretical is the 2001 collapse of Enron in the United States. According to multiple accounts, over 60% of Enron employees' retirement-plan assets were concentrated in Enron's own stock at one point. When the company collapsed under an accounting fraud scandal, its stock fell from the $90s to under a dollar, and employees are estimated to have lost more than $1 billion in retirement savings combined. What made it especially brutal was that employees lost their jobs at the same moment their savings evaporated — income, accumulated assets, and retirement security were all riding on the same single company, so when that company failed, all three safety nets disappeared simultaneously. The aftermath prompted ongoing policy debate in the US over limiting how much company stock retirement plans could hold, and giving employees more freedom to diversify out of it. Korea's employee ownership plan is legally structured quite differently from the US 401(k)/ESOP framework behind Enron's case, and the 20% allocation cap plus one-year lock-up make an extreme, Enron-scale concentration unlikely. Still, the underlying lesson carries over directly: the good intention of making employees "owners" and the risk of someone whose income is already riding on a company also betting their savings on it are, structurally, the same thing.

## Takeaway

- Korea's employee stock ownership plan lets employees form an association to buy their own company's shares, with KOSPI-listed companies able to allocate up to 20% of an IPO to the plan as a priority tranche (KOSDAQ companies face no such requirement).
- Shares acquired this way are generally locked up for one year after listing and cannot be sold, except if the employee leaves the company during that period.
- Buying with borrowed money through a leveraged employee stock purchase compounds the risk, stacking loan interest on top of any price decline; tax benefits exist under the Special Tax Treatment Control Act, but their exact limits change over time.
- Because an employee's income and savings both end up tied to the same company, this cuts against the basic logic of diversification — a risk the 2001 Enron collapse illustrated in its most extreme form, when income, savings, and jobs vanished together.

## FAQ

### Do I have to subscribe if my company offers an employee stock allocation?
No. Subscribing, and how much to subscribe, is an individual employee's choice. That said, Korean media has occasionally reported on workplace cultures where participation is informally encouraged, so it's worth weighing the amount and the lock-up period carefully before deciding, rather than subscribing by default.

### What happens to my shares if I quit during the lock-up period?
Leaving the company removes the continued-employment condition the lock-up depends on, so you can sell your shares even if the one-year period hasn't finished yet. That said, if the stock is trading below the offering price at that point, selling still locks in a real loss.

### If the stock falls below the IPO price, is there any way to recover the loss?
Generally, no. An employee stock purchase carries the same market risk as any other IPO investment — neither the tax benefit nor the priority allocation offers any guarantee against a falling price. If the purchase was financed with a loan, the obligation to repay that loan remains separate from, and in addition to, any loss on the stock itself.
