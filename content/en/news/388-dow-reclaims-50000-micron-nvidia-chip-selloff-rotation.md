## What Happened

On Tuesday, November 24, the Dow Jones Industrial Average closed back above the 50,000 mark for the first time since it fell below that level on November 19 - rising 423.39 points, or 0.85%, to 50,018.70. But the headline number told only half the story. The S&P 500 slipped 0.50% to 7,201.09, and the tech-heavy Nasdaq Composite fell a sharper 1.48% to 23,733.54, as a broad chip-stock selloff dragged on the broader market even while the blue-chip Dow pushed to a fresh high.

The split was driven almost entirely by sector rotation. The S&P 500's information technology sector fell about 3.4% - by far the worst-performing group of the day - while healthcare rose roughly 2.2% and consumer staples gained about 1.8%. Industrial and power-equipment names, several of which carry heavy weight in the price-weighted Dow, helped pull that index higher even as money fled the AI trade that had dominated headlines just one trading session earlier.

The selling hit semiconductor and memory-chip stocks hardest. Nvidia (NASDAQ: NVDA) fell roughly 5.9% to close near $155.38, giving back a chunk of the previous day's rally. AMD (NASDAQ: AMD) dropped about 9%, Intel (NASDAQ: INTC) fell 5.8%, and Broadcom (NASDAQ: AVGO) slid roughly 4.4%. But the steepest declines landed on memory-chip names: Micron (NASDAQ: MU) tumbled about 11% to roughly $629.79, SanDisk (NASDAQ: SNDK) dropped more than 12%, and SK Hynix's U.S.-listed shares fell nearly 10% to around $111.98. A specific catalyst compounded the memory group's pain: BNP Paribas slashed its price target on Micron from $1,700 to $775, citing concerns that memory supply is set to grow faster than demand and could pressure NAND and DRAM pricing into next year.

The timing made the reversal especially striking. Just one trading session earlier, on Monday, November 23, Nvidia had surged as much as 33% after its board reinstated Jensen Huang as CEO only days after initially ruling out his return - a move that sparked Wall Street's biggest single-day rally in roughly a year and a half and pulled the Nasdaq 100 out of its worst week since 2020. Tuesday's pullback erased a meaningful share of those gains, a reminder that outsized one-day rallies driven by a single corporate event rarely hold their full magnitude once the initial shock fades and investors start locking in profits.

Looking ahead, Wednesday, November 25 brings a cluster of market-moving data ahead of the Thanksgiving holiday: October's core PCE inflation reading (the Federal Reserve's preferred inflation gauge), the second estimate of third-quarter GDP, October durable goods orders, and Dell's fiscal third-quarter earnings report. That lineup arrives against a backdrop where St. Louis Fed President Alberto Musalem had already warned, in minutes from the Fed's October meeting released earlier in the week, that up to four more rate hikes could be needed by spring - a hawkish signal that tends to weigh disproportionately on richly valued growth and technology stocks.

## Why Micron Fell Harder Than Nvidia on the Same Selloff

At first glance, it looks like one story: "AI stocks sold off." But the gap between Nvidia's roughly 6% decline and Micron's roughly 11% decline - nearly double - shows that two distinct forces were layered on top of each other that day, and separating them matters for understanding what actually happened.

The first force was simple mean reversion. Nvidia's 33% one-day surge on Monday was an extraordinary move by any standard, and markets rarely let gains of that size stand unchallenged for long. Some of Tuesday's Nvidia weakness was nothing more than traders who bought the rally locking in profits a day later - a pattern so common after outsized single-day pops that seasoned traders often plan for it in advance rather than being surprised by it.

The second force was company- and industry-specific, and it explains why memory-chip stocks fell so much harder than logic-chip makers like Nvidia or AMD. Micron, SanDisk, and SK Hynix all make memory chips - DRAM and NAND - a notoriously cyclical, commodity-like business where prices swing hard based on the balance between supply and demand. BNP Paribas's price-target cut specifically flagged a risk that memory manufacturers, having enjoyed a strong pricing environment through 2026 on booming AI server demand, are now ramping production fast enough that supply could outrun demand into 2027, pressuring the chip prices that drive these companies' profits. That is a fundamentally different concern than anything affecting Nvidia's GPU business, where demand visibility (backlog, data-center contracts) looks far more durable. In other words, the memory stocks weren't just caught in general AI-sector profit-taking; they had their own, separate bad news stacked on top of it.

There is also a structural reason the Dow and Nasdaq diverged so sharply on the same day. The Dow Jones Industrial Average is a price-weighted index of just 30 large, mostly "old economy" companies, with comparatively light exposure to the AI-chip names that dominate the Nasdaq 100 and much of the S&P 500's recent gains. When money rotates out of semiconductors and into healthcare, consumer staples, and industrials - exactly what happened Tuesday - the Dow can post a record close on the very same day that the more tech-concentrated Nasdaq falls over a full percentage point. Neither index is "wrong"; they're simply weighted toward different parts of the market, and days like November 24 are when that difference becomes visible to everyone.

## What to Take Away From This

- **A single index headline can hide two opposite stories.** "Dow hits a record" and "Nasdaq falls on a chip selloff" were both true on the same day, because the Dow and Nasdaq are built from very different sets of companies. Always check what's driving an index move - sometimes it's broad market sentiment, and sometimes it's just that one sector is up or down while another cancels it out.
- **Explosive one-day rallies usually give back some of their gains.** Nvidia's 33% Monday surge partially reversing on Tuesday isn't a sign the original catalyst (Huang's reinstatement) was fake news - it's just how markets normally digest an extreme move. Don't assume a reversal the next day invalidates the prior day's news.
- **"The chip sector fell" is rarely the whole story - check for stock-specific catalysts.** Micron's 11% drop wasn't simply AI profit-taking; a specific analyst downgrade tied to memory oversupply fears piled on top of the broader selloff. Grouping unrelated stocks into one narrative can cause you to miss the real reason a particular name moved so much more than its peers.
- **Watch where money rotates to, not just where it leaves.** Healthcare and consumer staples both gained while tech fell - a classic "risk-off" rotation pattern. Tracking which sectors absorb money leaving an overheated trade can tell you how nervous (or calm) the broader market actually is.

The Dow Jones Industrial Average is price-weighted and made up of just 30 mostly non-tech companies, so it has much less exposure to semiconductor and AI stocks than the Nasdaq Composite or Nasdaq 100. When investors rotated out of chip stocks and into healthcare, consumer staples, and industrials on November 24, that rotation lifted the Dow to a fresh high at the same time it dragged the more tech-heavy Nasdaq down 1.48%.

That is a company- and industry-specific risk layered on top of the broader selloff, which is why memory names like Micron, SanDisk, and SK Hynix fell harder than logic-chip makers like Nvidia.
