## "Redemptions Have Been Suspended" — Wasn't I Supposed to Be Able to Pull My Money Out Anytime?

One of the biggest differences between a bank deposit and a fund is the promise underneath it: sell your shares today, and you'll get cash back at a set price within a few days. That promise is what makes headlines like the 2019 Woodford Equity Income Fund suspension, or Korea's Lime Asset Management freeze the same year, so unsettling — the fund manager hasn't absconded with the money, and the fund hasn't technically gone bankrupt, yet investors are told their cash simply isn't coming out right now. This lesson isn't about whether to buy into any specific fund today. It's about the structural reason a perfectly ordinary-looking open-end fund can reach a point where it has to stop honoring redemptions altogether.

## The Open-End Fund's Promise: A Door That's Open Every Day

A standard mutual fund — an open-end fund — issues new shares whenever an investor buys in, and when an investor asks to redeem, the fund sells off some of its holdings to raise the cash to pay them. That door is supposed to stay open every business day, and investors sign up on the understanding that a redemption request today gets settled at that day's price within a short, predictable window. As covered in [closed-end fund discounts](/en/basics/closed-end-fund-discount-explained/), a closed-end fund doesn't offer this door at all — getting your money out means selling your shares to another investor on an exchange instead. Put the other way around, an open-end fund is built on the promise that the fund itself will do the selling on your behalf, and that promise only holds if the fund can actually convert its holdings into cash as fast as investors ask for it. That's exactly where things can go wrong.

## Liquidity Mismatch: When Assets Sell Slower Than Redemptions Arrive

To honor a redemption, a fund has to sell something to raise cash — but not every asset sells at the same speed. Listed stocks or government bonds can usually be converted to cash within a day or two without much loss. Private company stakes, mezzanine bonds (the kind of instrument covered in [convertible bonds](/en/basics/convertible-bonds-cb-bw/) or [redeemable convertible preferred stock](/en/basics/rcps-redeemable-convertible-preferred-stock/) that don't trade on an exchange), distressed loans, and illiquid emerging-market holdings can take weeks or months to sell at anything close to fair value. The moment a fund mixes these slow-to-sell assets into a vehicle that promises daily redemptions, it's carrying a structural tension: assets that move slowly, sitting behind a promise that can be called on fast. Under normal conditions, new money coming in roughly offsets redemptions going out, so the tension stays invisible. But if some shock sends a wave of redemption requests all at once, the fund is forced to dump its illiquid holdings quickly, often at a steep discount to what they're really worth. This is liquidity mismatch, and it became a widely recognized risk after the 2008 financial crisis, when a number of hedge funds and real estate funds hit exactly this wall and froze redemptions.

## The Lime Case: How One Mother Fund's Trouble Spread to Its Feeder Funds

The clearest domestic illustration of this risk is the 2019 Lime Asset Management collapse. Lime pooled money from a number of "feeder funds" into a smaller set of "mother funds," which concentrated heavily in illiquid assets — mezzanine bonds and private loans. When Kosdaq-listed stocks weakened, the returns on those mezzanine holdings deteriorated, and the mother funds found it increasingly hard to raise cash. That strain then spread at once to every feeder fund tied to the affected mother fund. According to reporting at the time, roughly ₩820 billion sat in closed-end structures where redemption was never an option in the first place, versus roughly ₩480 billion in open-end funds where investors could actually request their money back — and it was the open-end side where the liquidity shortfall surfaced first and fastest. The closed-end funds faced no immediate pressure to raise cash because no one could ask for a redemption; the open-end funds were hit the moment real redemption requests demanded cash the fund didn't have readily available. The episode shows that liquidity mismatch isn't an abstract textbook risk — it's a structural trap that any open-end fund holding illiquid assets can walk straight into once markets turn.

## First to Redeem Wins: The Investors Left Holding the Bag

Liquidity mismatch creates a second problem: unfairness between investors. Whoever redeems while the fund still has liquid assets on hand gets paid close to full value — but every redemption shrinks the pool of liquid assets left behind and pushes up the proportion of illiquid holdings relative to the fund's total. Investors who redeem late can end up holding a stake in a fund that's now almost entirely illiquid. This "first-come, first-served" dynamic creates a sharp incentive to redeem before everyone else the moment markets look shaky, which itself feeds back into even heavier redemption pressure. Left unchecked, that feedback loop widens the gap between investors who got out early and those stuck holding the remainder — which is exactly the point at which a fund manager typically decides to close the redemption window altogether rather than let the imbalance keep growing.

A simple example makes the mechanics concrete. Say a fund holds $100 million in net assets, split $40 million liquid and $60 million illiquid. If investors suddenly request $30 million in redemptions, the manager can cover it by selling $30 million of the $40 million liquid sleeve. The problem shows up in what's left: liquid assets have fallen to $10 million against $60 million of illiquid holdings — a total of $70 million, where the illiquid share has jumped from 60% to roughly 86%. Anyone still holding shares is now exposed to a far more illiquid fund than the one they originally bought into, and the next wave of redemption requests arrives with almost nothing liquid left to sell against. This shift in composition is exactly the logic fund managers invoked in the Lime case and similar freezes elsewhere: letting early redeemers cash out in full would have left the remaining investors holding an increasingly illiquid, increasingly worthless remainder.

## The Tools Managers Use: Gates, Side Pockets, and Suspensions

Once a liquidity mismatch turns serious, fund managers generally reach for one of a handful of tools.

| Tool | How It Works | Effect on Investors |
|---|---|---|
| Suspension | Freezes the entire redemption window for a period | No investor can redeem, applied equally to everyone |
| Gate | Caps the amount or percentage that can be redeemed at once | Investors get only part of what they requested, paid out over several rounds |
| Side pocket | Splits illiquid holdings into a separate account | The liquid portion keeps redeeming normally; the side-pocketed portion waits until those assets are actually sold |
| Swing pricing | Adjusts the fund's quoted price against large redemptions | The redeeming investor absorbs more of the cost their own redemption creates |

In Korea, the Financial Investment Services and Capital Markets Act (Article 237 and related enforcement provisions) gives managers legal grounds to delay redemptions when a market is closed or suspended, or when the fund genuinely cannot dispose of its holdings. Lime's own justification for freezing redemptions was that a forced fire sale would have hurt every remaining investor — which reflects the actual intent behind redemption-suspension rules: protecting the whole investor base from being penalized by whoever redeems first. That said, the same tool has also been used, in some episodes, to paper over poor fund management or outright misconduct for longer than it should have — so a suspension announcement by itself shouldn't automatically be read as "the safeguard worked as intended." Internationally, the 2015 freeze at a major U.S. high-yield bond fund and the UK's 2019 Woodford fund suspension both involved the same underlying problem — an open-end structure holding assets that were never liquid enough for the redemption terms it offered.

## Why ETFs and Plain-Vanilla Stock Funds Rarely Run Into This

As covered in [ETF tracking error and premium/discount](/en/basics/etf-tracking-error-premium-discount/), ETFs create and redeem shares through in-kind baskets handled by authorized participants, which largely insulates them from this specific shock, and most ordinary equity mutual funds hold liquid, exchange-listed stocks almost exclusively, so they can usually raise cash fast enough even under redemption pressure. [Private equity funds](/en/basics/private-equity-fund-pef-explained/) sit at the opposite extreme for a different reason: LPs are contractually locked in for a fixed term with no right to redeem on demand, so no liquidity mismatch can arise even though the underlying assets are themselves illiquid. The lesson here isn't that illiquid assets are inherently dangerous — it's that the danger comes specifically from combining illiquid assets with a daily-redemption promise made to investors who were never told to expect anything less.

## What to Check Before You Invest

- **Underlying asset liquidity**: Check the prospectus or monthly fund report for the split between exchange-listed securities and private, unlisted, or loan-type assets. A higher illiquid share means more structural risk.
- **Mother fund / feeder fund structures**: If several funds all feed into one mother fund, looking only at the feeder fund you bought can hide what it's actually exposed to underneath.
- **Track record under stress**: Has this manager, or similar funds, delayed or suspended redemptions during past market stress?
- **Concentration relative to fund size**: If a large share of the fund sits in just a few illiquid positions, trouble in any one of them can destabilize the whole fund at once.

## Key Takeaways

- Open-end funds are built on a promise of daily redemption, but that promise creates a structural tension — a liquidity mismatch — whenever the underlying assets can't be sold as fast as the fund can be asked to pay investors back.
- The 2019 Lime Asset Management collapse showed the liquidity shortfall surfacing first in the open-end funds that could actually be redeemed, even though the underlying illiquid mezzanine assets sat mostly in linked closed-end mother funds.
- To stop early redeemers from leaving remaining investors holding an increasingly illiquid remainder, managers can use suspensions, gates, side pockets, or swing pricing.
- ETFs and plain-vanilla equity funds rarely face this risk because their holdings are liquid; private equity funds avoid it because LPs have no on-demand redemption right in the first place.
- Before investing, check the liquidity of the underlying assets, whether a mother fund/feeder fund structure is involved, and the manager's track record during past periods of market stress.

## FAQ

### If redemptions are suspended, is my money gone for good?
No — a suspension delays when you get paid, it doesn't cancel the payment. It postpones redemption until the fund can actually sell its holdings at something closer to fair value. The final amount you receive can still end up lower than your original valuation depending on what those assets actually sell for, and the longer the suspension drags on, the more that uncertainty compounds.

### Can an ETF suspend redemptions too?
It's structurally possible but far rarer. ETFs create and redeem shares through in-kind baskets handled by authorized participants, so the fund itself rarely needs to sell assets to raise cash, and the assets it holds are usually liquid, exchange-listed securities. That said, if the underlying market itself freezes (a foreign exchange closure, for instance), even an ETF tracking it can be affected.

### Does this risk apply to closed-end funds at all?
No. A [closed-end fund](/en/basics/closed-end-fund-discount-explained/) never offers investors a redemption window with the manager in the first place, so "redemption suspension" isn't a concept that applies to it. Getting cash out means selling shares to another investor on the exchange instead — which carries a different risk, since that sale can happen at a discount to NAV.
