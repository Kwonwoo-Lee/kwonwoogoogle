## One Headline, Two Possible Ownership Stakes

Startup funding news tends to read like this: "Company A raised $50 million at a $1 billion valuation in its Series C round." The number looks precise, but that sentence alone doesn't actually tell you what percentage of the company the new investors received. Everything hinges on whether that $1 billion is the valuation **before** the new money came in or **after**. That distinction is what **pre-money** and **post-money valuation** are for, and it's the foundation for two concepts that show up constantly in startup coverage: **dilution** and the **down round**. This lesson walks through how private funding rounds actually price a company and divide up ownership.

## Pre-Money and Post-Money: Before and After the Check Clears

Pre-money valuation is what a company is worth **immediately before** new investment comes in. Post-money valuation is what it's worth **immediately after**. The relationship is simple:

**Post-money = Pre-money + new investment**

And the new investor's ownership stake follows directly:

**Investor's stake = New investment ÷ Post-money valuation**

Say Company A is valued at a $40 million pre-money and raises $10 million from a venture fund. Post-money becomes $40M + $10M = $50M, and the investor's stake is $10M ÷ $50M = 20%. Now suppose the "$40 million valuation" quoted in the press release was actually post-money, not pre-money. Then pre-money works out to $40M − $10M = $30M, and the investor's stake rises to $10M ÷ $40M = 25%. Identical headline — "$40 million valuation, $10 million raised" — but a five-percentage-point swing in who owns what, depending on which side of the check the $40 million refers to. On rounds sized in the tens or hundreds of millions, that kind of gap translates into real money, which is exactly why a term sheet always specifies explicitly which basis the valuation is quoted on.

## From the Founder's Side: Same Check, Different Sacrifice

This isn't just an investor's concern. For founders, a higher pre-money valuation means giving up less equity for the same dollar amount raised. Go back to the example: at a $40 million pre-money, raising $10 million costs the company 20% of itself. If investors had instead priced the company at a $24 million pre-money, post-money becomes $34 million and the new stake rises to roughly 29%. The lower the pre-money number, the more equity existing shareholders — founders included — have to hand over for the same check size. That's exactly why every funding round involves a negotiation over "what is this company worth right now," and the number both sides land on becomes the pre-money figure that sets everything else.

## What Compounds Across Rounds: Dilution and the Fully Diluted Count

Startups typically raise money in a sequence — seed, then Series A, B, C, and so on. Each round issues new shares to new investors, which means the ownership percentage held by founders and earlier investors shrinks a little more every time. This is dilution: the pie is getting bigger, but your slice of it keeps getting proportionally smaller. Dilution by itself isn't necessarily bad news — if the pie is growing fast enough, a shrinking percentage can still be worth more in absolute dollars. The real risk shows up when the pie stops growing or shrinks outright, which is the down-round scenario covered below.

One extra wrinkle complicates the math: many investors require the company to create or expand an **option pool** — equity reserved for future employee stock grants — as a condition of the round. That pool is typically carved out of existing shareholders' stakes right **before** the new money is calculated in, so founders end up absorbing dilution from both the new investor's stake and the enlarged option pool at once. For this reason, evaluating a term sheet properly means looking past the headline investor percentage and checking ownership on a **fully diluted** basis — assuming every option and convertible security has already been issued — to see the real size of the slice left over.

## Running the Numbers: What Three Rounds Does to a Founder's Stake

| Round | Pre-money | New money | Post-money | New investor stake | Founder stake after |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Seed | $4M | $1M | $5M | 20.0% | 80.0% |
| Series A | $15M | $5M | $20M | 25.0% (new) | 60.0% |
| Series B | $80M | $20M | $100M | 20.0% (new) | 48.0% |

At seed, the founder's stake drops from 100% to 80%. At Series A, a new 25% stake dilutes the founder down to 80% × (1 − 0.25) = 60%. At Series B, a new 20% stake brings that down to 60% × (1 − 0.20) = 48%. Across three rounds, the founder's ownership fell from 100% to 48% — but the company's post-money valuation grew 20x, from $5 million to $100 million. The percentage was cut by more than half, but the absolute value of that 48% slice ended up far larger than 100% of the much smaller company ever was. That's the whole point of the "dilution isn't automatically bad" argument — it depends entirely on how fast the pie is actually growing. This table simplifies by assuming every existing holder dilutes proportionally; in practice, option-pool expansions and investor-specific preference terms (discussed next) make any individual holder's real outcome messier than this clean math suggests.

## The Down Round: When Valuation Moves in Reverse

Everything above assumed valuations climb with each round, but they don't always. A **down round** is a financing round priced below the valuation of the previous one. When interest rates rise or investor sentiment cools, companies that once commanded premium valuations can find the market unwilling to pay as much the next time they raise. Sweden's Klarna is a widely cited example: the fintech was valued at roughly $45.6 billion in 2021 at the peak of the funding boom, then raised its next round at a fraction of that figure once financing conditions tightened sharply. Korean media have reported similar patterns among domestic "unicorns" that commanded outsized valuations during the pandemic-era e-commerce boom, only to see those valuations fall sharply — and IPO plans shelved or withdrawn — once growth slowed and profitability questions mounted.

Down rounds don't distribute pain evenly across all shareholders. Venture financing agreements typically include **anti-dilution provisions**, which adjust the conversion terms of existing investors' preferred shares in their favor when a down round occurs, partially insulating them from the hit. Most such agreements also carry a **liquidation preference**, which guarantees preferred shareholders get paid back before common shareholders — usually founders and option-holding employees — if the company is sold or wound down. Stack a down round on top of a liquidation preference and a sale can leave founders and employees holding common stock worth far less than the paper valuation implied, or in some cases close to nothing. The headline "valuation cut in half" hides a structure where losses land very differently depending on what kind of security you actually hold.

## Why This Matters Even After a Company Goes Public

Pre-money math, dilution, and down rounds all happen before a company lists on an exchange, but the consequences follow it there. The bookbuilding process described in [How an IPO Price Gets Set](/en/basics/ipo-pricing-bookbuilding/) is, in effect, a test of whether public markets will validate the valuation set in that company's last private round. A "down IPO" — pricing below the final private round — locks in losses for the investors who bought in at that last private price, and signals to retail subscribers that the private-market valuation may have been inflated to begin with. The overhang risk that can hit a stock once an [IPO lock-up](/en/basics/ipo-lockup-period-overhang/) expires traces back to this same structure: early investors who bought in cheaply at a low pre-money valuation are often the ones holding the largest blocks once that lock-up lifts. Understanding how pre-IPO valuation actually works makes a lot of post-IPO price action easier to read correctly.

## Key Takeaways

- Pre-money is a company's value before new investment; post-money is its value after, following post-money = pre-money + new investment.
- An investor's ownership stake equals new investment ÷ post-money valuation, so the same headline valuation figure implies different ownership depending on which basis it's quoted on.
- Ownership dilutes with every funding round; checking a term sheet on a fully diluted basis — including any expanded option pool — reveals the real size of what's left over.
- A down round prices a company below its previous round's valuation; anti-dilution provisions and liquidation preferences mean the resulting losses land very differently across security types.
- Private-market valuation logic carries through to IPO pricing and to overhang risk once lock-up periods expire, making it relevant well beyond the private-funding stage.

## FAQ

### If a company raises $20 million at a $200 million pre-money valuation, what stake does the investor get?
Post-money is $200M + $20M = $220M, and the investor's stake is $20M ÷ $220M ≈ 9.1%. Whenever you see a valuation figure in funding news, check whether it's quoted pre- or post-money before assuming you know the resulting ownership split.

### What exactly does "unicorn" mean?
It's industry shorthand for a privately held startup valued at $1 billion or more. That valuation isn't a real-time market price — it's a figure negotiated between the company and investors in its most recent funding round, so it can be revised downward in the next round if market conditions shift.

### What happens to a founder's stake when a down round hits?
A down round doesn't directly reduce the number of shares a founder holds, but anti-dilution provisions typically adjust existing preferred shareholders' conversion ratios in their favor, which further dilutes common shareholders — founders included. Combined with a liquidation preference, this can leave founders and employees with a common-stock payout, in a sale, that's far smaller than the paper valuation would suggest.

### Can pre-money and post-money apply to SAFE notes or convertible instruments too?
Yes. Early-stage startups often raise through a SAFE (Simple Agreement for Future Equity) or a [convertible note](/en/basics/convertible-bonds-cb-bw/) specifically because it's too early to pin down a precise valuation. These instruments defer that valuation to the next priced round, usually with a valuation cap or discount built in — so the exact pre-money/post-money split isn't fixed today, but the same formula still determines it once conversion happens.
